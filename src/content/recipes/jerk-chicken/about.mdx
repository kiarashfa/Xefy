Jerk began as a way of not being found. Maroons — Jamaicans who escaped
slavery and built independent communities in the island's mountainous
interior — could not risk a visible fire giving away their position to the
search parties sent after them, so they devised a method of seasoning and
cooking meat in an underground pit over dying embers, which produced heat
without betraying smoke <Cite ref="jis-jamaican-food"/>. In their mountain
settlements, escaped Africans and the island's surviving Taíno inhabitants
exchanged food traditions and methods of preserving meat, and jerk is one
product of that exchange rather than a technique either group carried whole
from elsewhere <Cite ref="smithsonian-jerk"/>.

The seasoning was built from what grew wild around the Maroons rather than
from anything imported: pimento berries — allspice — salt and bird peppers,
a small, fiery relative of the chillies used today, rubbed into wild boar and
wrapped in pepper elder leaves before it went into the pit
<Cite ref="smithsonian-jerk"/>. Jamaican literary scholar Carolyn Cooper has
described jerk as one of the clearest surviving markers of that fusion of
African and Taíno culture on the island <Cite ref="smithsonian-jerk"/>.

Once the Maroons no longer needed to hide, jerking moved back above ground,
onto open grills, and the meat itself broadened from wild boar to pork,
chicken and fish alike <Cite ref="jis-jamaican-food"/>. What survived the
move outdoors is the principle rather than the concealment: pimento wood,
burned for its low, fragrant smoke rather than a hot flame, is still what
distinguishes jerk cooked to the traditional standard from jerk seasoning
simply rubbed onto meat and grilled or roasted by other means.
