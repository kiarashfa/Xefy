The name most English speakers know this dish by is itself a source of confusion.
Despite being called Kabuli pulao, the dish is not believed to have originated in
Kabul at all — the fuller record places its beginning near Afghanistan's northern
border with Uzbekistan, inside a much older regional tradition of rice cooked with
meat and stock that likely reaches back as far as Bactrian times
<Cite ref="wiki-kabuli-pulao"/>. An early written trace of the *qabili* style of
pilaf — the name most likely comes from Persian, rather than from Kabul the city
— appears in a Persian cookbook from 1521, Ali Bavarchi's *Kar-nama*, among the
oldest surviving cookbooks in the language <Cite ref="wiki-kabuli-pulao"/>.

A separate, more local telling puts the dish's rise squarely in the city its
common name points to. On this account, Kabuli palau was created by the wealthy
families of Kabul itself, who could afford to dress plain rice with caramelised
carrot, raisins and nuts — expensive additions at the time — and only later, as
prosperity spread more widely through Afghan society, did the dish become common
enough to need renaming, taking the name Qabili palau from the Dari word for "well
accomplished," a nod to the skill needed to balance its flavours
<Cite ref="afghan-culture-unveiled-kabuli"/>. The two accounts do not agree on
where the dish itself began, even as they agree closely on what its name has
meant and on the luxury ingredients — carrot, raisins, nuts — that mark it out
from a plainer pilaf.

The same qabili style of pilaf is known by other names across the wider region it
grew out of: as *osh palov* in Uzbekistan, and, carried by Central Asian émigré
communities, as "Bukhari rice" in parts of Saudi Arabia, after the Uzbek city of
Bukhara <Cite ref="wiki-kabuli-pulao"/>.
