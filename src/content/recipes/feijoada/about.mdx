Feijoada's most repeated origin story holds that enslaved people on Brazilian
plantations invented it, stewing black beans with the pig's ears, feet and
tail their masters discarded. It is a story that frames the dish as an act
of resourcefulness born from deprivation, and it is the version most
Brazilians are told. Historians working from the documentary record now
treat it as largely a modern myth. The food historian Carlos Augusto Ditadi
argued the story is "born of modern folklore, in a romanticized vision" of
plantation life, unsupported by either culinary tradition or historical
evidence, and pointed out that pig's trotters were already an ordinary part
of Portuguese cooking well before Brazil existed as a nation
<Cite ref="wiki-feijoada"/><Cite ref="portalsaofrancisco-feijoada"/>. Ditadi
was not the first to make the case: the writer Guilherme Figueiredo had
already argued in 1964 that feijoada did not originate in the slave
quarters, and the sociologist Gabriel Bolaffi later asked, pointedly, how
two discarded pig parts could plausibly have fed plantations of hundreds
<Cite ref="portalsaofrancisco-feijoada"/>.

The documented history points toward an Iberian stew adapted and expanded in
Brazil rather than an enslaved invention. Feijoada's elaborate,
many-meats form has been compared to Portuguese cozido, French cassoulet and
similar European bean-and-pork stews built from a shared old-world tradition
<Cite ref="southerncultures-feijoada"/>. Scholar Carlos Alberto Dória draws a
sharper distinction still: plain beans, eaten with cornmeal or manioc flour,
were genuinely the food of enslaved people, but the full, offal-rich
*feijoada completa* eaten today was for a long period a dish of elite urban
restaurants, not plantation kitchens <Cite ref="southerncultures-feijoada"/>.
The earliest known newspaper references to feijoada, from Recife in 1827 and
Rio de Janeiro in 1833, likewise say nothing about enslaved origin
<Cite ref="wiki-feijoada"/><Cite ref="portalsaofrancisco-feijoada"/>; the
earliest restaurant known to have served feijoada as a public dish, G. Lobo
in Rio de Janeiro, dates only from the late nineteenth century and was
itself demolished decades later during the construction of Avenida
Presidente Vargas <Cite ref="portalsaofrancisco-feijoada"/>.

What is better documented is feijoada's twentieth-century political life.
The anthropologist Luís da Câmara Cascudo placed the dish's spread into
ordinary Brazilian kitchens around 1930, the same period in which Brazil's
elites began promoting an ideology of "racial democracy" that cast the
country as a harmonious blend of its African, Indigenous and European
inheritances <Cite ref="southerncultures-feijoada"/>. Feijoada's adoption as
a national dish, uniting a stew once associated with both elite tables and
enslaved poverty under one symbol, served that story well, which is exactly
why its popular origin legend deserves more scrutiny than it usually gets.
