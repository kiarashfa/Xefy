Sticky toffee pudding has no agreed birthplace. The account repeated most
often credits the Sharrow Bay Country House Hotel on Ullswater in Cumbria,
where Francis Coulson and Robert Lee are said to have popularised the dish
through the 1970s <Cite ref="wiki-stp"/><Cite ref="gbl-cartmel"/>, but even
this best-known version of the story is usually framed as an account of who
popularised the pudding rather than who invented it; no source claims
documentary proof that would settle the question of a single inventor.

What is better documented is what happened next. The pudding most people now
buy ready-made traces not to any of those hotels but to the Cartmel Village
Shop, a few miles from Sharrow Bay, where Howard and Jean Johns developed a
take-home version after taking over the shop in 1989 <Cite ref="gbl-cartmel"/>.
Jean Johns has been direct about the distinction: "We didn't invent sticky
toffee puddings but we found a way for them to be taken away"
<Cite ref="gbl-cartmel"/>; by her own account, the dish was already on the
menu at both Sharrow Bay and another Lake District hotel, Miller Howe, before
Cartmel's version existed <Cite ref="gbl-cartmel"/>. From a production run of a
few dozen puddings a week, the shop grew into a business selling roughly a
million a year and supplying retailers including Fortnum & Mason
<Cite ref="gbl-cartmel"/>.

So the pudding's fame and its invention are two separate stories that get
told as one. Cartmel deserves the credit for turning a hotel dessert into a
national grocery item; nobody has a comparably solid claim to having invented
it in the first place, and the honest account is that a genuinely popular
dish arrived at its now-standard form (date sponge, bicarbonate of soda, hot
butterscotch sauce) by the separate efforts of several kitchens rather than
one.
