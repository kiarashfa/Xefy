Crème brûlée is a custard of cream, egg yolk and sugar, baked slowly and finished with a sheet of caramel. Its origin is contested, and the sources do not settle it. The dish is first recorded in French cookbooks under the name *crème brûlée* in 1691 <Cite ref="spectator-creme"/>.

The English claim rests on accounts that the dish had been made in England for at least sixty years before the French took it up. The Spectator states this as what "accounts suggest", not as a fact <Cite ref="spectator-creme"/>.

Trinity College, Cambridge, is the most often named English source. The college was serving the custard by 1879, with its crest branded into the caramel <Cite ref="spectator-creme"/>, <Cite ref="wiki-creme"/>. The college itself says the story that the dish was invented there "almost certainly has no basis in fact" <Cite ref="spectator-creme"/>. A separate tale of an undergraduate's recipe, refused by the kitchens and adopted decades later, comes from Florence White's *Good Things in England*. The article presents it as her account and does not date it <Cite ref="spectator-creme"/>.

Catalonia's *crema catalana* is close to the same dish, and the sources describe the resemblance without deciding which came first <Cite ref="spectator-creme"/>. Taken together, the record gives three national claims and no settled answer, and the fair description is that the origin of crème brûlée is unknown.
