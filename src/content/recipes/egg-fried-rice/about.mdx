Fried rice's earliest written trace in China dates to the Sui dynasty, in
records connected to the city of Yangzhou, and the reason for it is entirely
practical rather than festive: cooked rice left overnight turns hard and dry,
and frying it with a little oil and whatever else is on hand (egg, in the
plainest and, by most accounts, oldest version) was simply a way of not
wasting it <Cite ref="wiki-friedrice"/> <Cite ref="vice-friedrice"/>. That
practicality is still the
method's whole logic today: cold, day-old rice fries into separate, distinct
grains in a way that freshly cooked rice, still full of surface moisture,
cannot.

How far back the specific pairing of egg and rice goes, and where exactly it
was first cooked, is less settled than the Sui-dynasty date suggests. Local
legend in Yangzhou credits an imperial visit for the dish's association with
the city, while a competing account places its real origin in Guangzhou
instead, centuries later under the Qing, and food writers who have gone
looking for a documented answer generally conclude that the dish's own
history remains genuinely vague, whichever version gets repeated locally
<Cite ref="vice-friedrice"/>.

What is not in question is the technique's spread and its usefulness. Once
established, egg fried rice became the base that other, more elaborate fried
rice dishes were built from, taking on shrimp, barbecued pork or whatever
else a kitchen had leftover, while the plain version stayed exactly what it
always was: rice, egg, and a hot pan, cooked hard and fast rather than
composed.
