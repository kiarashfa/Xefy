Dhaka's kacchi biryani is usually told as an accident of politics rather than
an invention. When the Mughals made Dhaka the provincial capital of Bengal in
1610, the officials and Subahdars posted there came disproportionately from
Lucknow, and they brought their own cooks with them
<Cite ref="tbs-kacchi"/>. Those cooks carried the *kacchi* method (raw,
marinated meat sealed under raw rice and finished entirely by steam in one
pot), a technique itself built on the Persian *dum*, sealed-pot cookery that
had reached the Indian subcontinent generations earlier
<Cite ref="dailystar-biryani"/>.

What happened next is the part of the story that makes Dhaka's version
distinct. Lucknow kept developing: over the following centuries its
kitchens moved toward *pakki* biryani, in which the meat is at least partly
cooked before it meets the rice, valued for its consistency and its more
delicate use of saffron and rose water <Cite ref="dailystar-biryani"/>. Dhaka,
removed from that evolving centre, is said to have kept cooking the older
kacchi method largely as it was received, so that what is eaten in Old Dhaka
today is closer to seventeenth-century Awadhi technique than to what
Lucknow itself now serves <Cite ref="dailystar-biryani"/><Cite ref="tbs-kacchi"/>.

Biryani's still deeper ancestry is unsettled, and the sources disagree with
each other rather than converging on one account: it is variously traced to
Timur's fourteenth-century invasions, to nineteenth-century Awadhi court
cooks under Nawab Wajid Ali Shah, and to Central Asian Chagatai cooking
carried in from Tajikistan and Uzbekistan <Cite ref="tbs-kacchi"/>. None of
this affects what is specific to Dhaka: a dish that began as elite,
Persian-descended court cooking and only slowly widened out, so that it is
now the centrepiece of the Old Dhaka wedding table as much as of its
street-side biryani houses <Cite ref="tbs-kacchi"/>.
