Ndolé takes its name from its defining ingredient: *ndoleh*, a bitterleaf
native to West and Central Africa, cooked down into a peanut sauce with
fish, beef, shrimp or some combination of the three
<Cite ref="wiki-ndole"/><Cite ref="ja-ndole"/>. Both its ingredient and its origin trace to the
same coastal stretch of Cameroon: the dish is generally placed in Douala,
in the Littoral Region, and credited to the Duala people and the wider
Sawa coastal communities of that area
<Cite ref="wiki-ndole"/><Cite ref="ja-ndole"/>. Precisely how old the
dish is, or when it moved beyond Duala kitchens, is not documented in
detail; what is recorded is where it started and who is credited with it,
rather than a fixed date.

The bitterleaf itself is the technical heart of the recipe. Left
untreated it is unpleasantly bitter, and reporting on the dish notes that
the Sawa people take particular pride in having mastered a leaf otherwise
close to toxic <Cite ref="ja-ndole"/>. It is boiled several times in
salted water (the water changed each time) before it ever meets the
peanut sauce, a step that separates a competently made ndolé from a
harsh-tasting one <Cite ref="wiki-ndole"/><Cite ref="ja-ndole"/>. That labour is part of why the
dish carries a status above the everyday: it is traditionally cooked for
guests considered worthy of particular esteem, prepared communally and
passed down mostly between women across generations, each family guarding
its own version of the recipe <Cite ref="ja-ndole"/>. From that coastal
starting point, ndolé has since spread across Cameroon's other regions and
communities widely enough to be described, informally but consistently, as
the country's national dish <Cite ref="wiki-ndole"/><Cite ref="ja-ndole"/>.
