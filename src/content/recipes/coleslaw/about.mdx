The name is Dutch before it is anything else. Koolsla — literally "cabbage
salad," from kool (cabbage) and sla (salad) — was brought to New Amsterdam,
later New York, by Dutch settlers in the seventeenth century, in a region
whose Hudson Valley farmland was well suited to growing cabbage
<Cite ref="britannica-coleslaw"/>. English-speaking Americans anglicised
koolsla into "coleslaw" by the late eighteenth century, though the spelling
"cold slaw" also took hold early and persists in places today, on the
reasonable but mistaken assumption that the name refers to the dish being
served chilled rather than to the cabbage in it <Cite ref="mw-coleslaw"/>.

The idea behind the dish — cabbage cut fine and dressed while raw — is far
older than the Dutch name for it. A recorded Greek recipe from the fourth
century BC already calls for chopped cabbage dressed with coriander, rue,
vinegar and honey, showing that dressed raw cabbage has been eaten in some form
for well over two thousand years before it acquired the name coleslaw
<Cite ref="britannica-coleslaw"/>. What is specifically American is the
mayonnaise-based version now most common; vinegar-dressed slaw, closer to the
cabbage salads that predate it, remained and remains the more traditional
alternative in parts of the South.
