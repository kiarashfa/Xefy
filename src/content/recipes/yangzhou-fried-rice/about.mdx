Yangzhou fried rice carries an unusually direct contradiction built into its
own name: even the dish's own encyclopedic record states outright that it did
not originate in the city it is named for <Cite ref="wiki-yangzhou"/>
<Cite ref="vice-yangzhou"/>. What actually happened instead is harder to pin
down. Local legend in Yangzhou
credits a visit from a Sui-dynasty emperor for the dish's association with
the city; a separate, competing account argues the version of fried rice that
became internationally famous really developed later and further south, in
Guangzhou under the Qing, and simply carried the older city's name once it
spread <Cite ref="vice-yangzhou"/>. Writers who have gone looking for a
documented answer tend to conclude the same thing: the dish's real history is
genuinely vague, and neither claim rests on strong evidence
<Cite ref="vice-yangzhou"/> <Cite ref="wiki-yangzhou"/>.

Whatever the truth of its birthplace, what distinguishes this fried rice from
the plain egg version it grew out of is scale rather than technique: shrimp,
char siu and a small dice of vegetable turn a way of using up rice into
something substantial enough to be a dish in its own right, while the
method (cold rice, a hot wok, constant tossing) stays exactly the same.
