American waffle-eating predates any Belgian connection by close to two
centuries. Dutch settlers brought wafel irons to the colonies, and Thomas
Jefferson returned from his posting in France in 1789 with several waffle
irons of his own, reportedly sparking a fashion for "waffle frolic" parties
once he was back home <Cite ref="time-waffles"/>. What is sold in the United
States today as a "Belgian waffle" — thick, made from a lighter batter, with
noticeably deeper squares than the older American griddle waffle — is a much
newer invention with a traceable start date.

At the 1964 World's Fair in Flushing, Queens, Maurice and Rose Vermersch ran a
stand selling a yeast-raised Brussels waffle topped with strawberries and
whipped cream, serving reportedly thousands a day <Cite ref="forbes-belgianwaffle"/>.
Believing American visitors would not recognise "Brussels" as a place, the
family renamed the item after the country instead, and "Belgian waffle" stuck
<Cite ref="tt-belgianwaffle"/>. The Vermersches had tested the format two years
earlier at the 1962 Seattle World's Fair before bringing it to New York
<Cite ref="tt-belgianwaffle"/>.

The waffle eaten at most American breakfast tables today is a hybrid of both
threads: thinner and less yeasted than a true Brussels waffle, but deep-pocketed
in a way the American waffle iron sold before the 1964 fair did not produce
<Cite ref="tt-belgianwaffle"/>, and still carrying the borrowed name.
