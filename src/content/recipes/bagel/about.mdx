A bagel is a ring of yeasted dough that is boiled before it is baked. The boil is what separates it from other rolls: it sets the outside, so the crust is chewy and the inside stays soft <Cite ref="aish-bagel"/>.

The bagel's history begins in Poland. National Geographic traces it to thirteenth-century Jewish bakers in what is now Poland, who made a boiled, lean, ring-shaped bread called the obwarzanek <Cite ref="natgeo-bagel"/>. Aish records a decree of 1264 in which Prince Boleslaw the Pious allowed Jews to buy, sell and touch bread, and it says Jewish bakers adapted the obwarzanek by boiling it and called the result "bagels" <Cite ref="aish-bagel"/>. The name probably comes from the Yiddish beigen, to bend <Cite ref="aish-bagel"/>.

A famous origin story ties the ring to a Viennese baker who is said to have made it in 1683, to honour King John III Sobieski after the Battle of Vienna. National Geographic calls it "a prominent bagel myth" <Cite ref="natgeo-bagel"/>. The bagel's later history is firmer. It reached the United States with European Jewish emigrants in the nineteenth century, and in 1907 the New York bagel bakers formed the International Beigel Bakers Union <Cite ref="natgeo-bagel"/>.
