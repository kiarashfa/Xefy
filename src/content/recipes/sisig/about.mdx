The word is centuries older than the dish it now names. *Sisig* is recorded
in Fray Diego Bergaño's 1732 Pampango-Spanish dictionary, where it meant a
salad of sour fruit dipped in salt and vinegar — the kind of sharp, acidic
snack craved in pregnancy, with no meat in it at all
<Cite ref="pf-sisig"/>. For generations after that, *sisig babi* meant simply
boiled pig's head, sliced and dressed with nipa-palm vinegar, onion, salt,
pepper and chilli — closer to a cold salad than to the hot, sizzling dish
served today <Cite ref="pf-sisig"/>.

The grilled, chopped version now known worldwide was created in Angeles
City, Pampanga, by the restaurateur Lucia "Aling Lucing" Cunanan in the
1970s, working from a row of barbecue stalls near the city's railroad
crossing <Cite ref="philstar-cunanan"/> <Cite ref="pf-sisig"/>. By her
family's account, she began from a kitchen accident: a batch of pig's ears
left too long on the grill, burnt rather than discarded, which she chopped
and dressed the way the older boiled version had been, calling it her own
take on the traditional recipe <Cite ref="pf-sisig"/>. It caught on
immediately with local diners, drawing customers from as far as Manila, and
Cunanan's name became attached to the dish's reinvention
<Cite ref="philstar-cunanan"/> <Cite ref="pf-sisig"/>.

The dish kept evolving after her. In the 1980s another Angeles City cook,
Benedict Pamintuan, began serving it on a sizzling hot plate rather than a
regular one, and chef Claude Tayag's Quezon City restaurant added chopped
chicken liver to the mix — the combination, on a hot skillet, that spread
sisig across Manila and beyond its home province
<Cite ref="pf-sisig"/>.
