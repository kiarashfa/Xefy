Greek cooks were making savoury pies of grain dough and vegetable filling long
before the pastry now associated with spanakopita existed — flatbreads folded
around cheese, herbs and legumes are attested from ancient Greece onward
<Cite ref="philosokitchen-spanakopita"/>. The paper-thin, many-layered filo
that wraps a modern spanakopita is a separate and later development, and its
own origin is contested rather than settled. One line of scholarship traces
it to *yufka*, a thin flatbread developed by Turkic peoples of Central Asia
and perfected into filo's extreme thinness by Ottoman pastry cooks by the
fifteenth century <Cite ref="wiki-filo"/>. A competing view points to evidence
of layered pastry within the Byzantine and even earlier Greco-Roman
tradition — the historian Speros Vryonis has described a Byzantine sweet,
*koptoplakous*, as effectively the same dish as what is later called baklava,
and the Roman writer Cato recorded a layered cheese pastry, *placenta*, that
predates any Ottoman contact by well over a thousand years
<Cite ref="wiki-filo"/><Cite ref="philosokitchen-spanakopita"/>.

Spanakopita specifically is most often placed on the Ottoman side of that
argument, with its filo layers understood as an adaptation of yufka that
arrived during the centuries of Ottoman presence in Greece
<Cite ref="philosokitchen-spanakopita"/>. But given how unsettled filo's own
history is between these two accounts, the honest position is that spanakopita
sits downstream of a pastry technique whose deeper origin is still argued over
by the people who study it, rather than downstream of one confidently dated
event. What is not in question is the pairing the dish is built on: spinach,
feta and dill, wrapped in a pastry engineered specifically to shatter into
flakes rather than bend.
