Cou-cou is cornmeal cooked down with okra until it forms a smooth, sliceable
mash, and its closest relatives are found not in the Caribbean but across the
Atlantic: Ghana's banku, made from fermented maize and eaten with okra stew,
and Angola's funge, a cornmeal starch of the same texture, both work the same
staple the same way. In Barbados, cornmeal and
okra reflect the African culinary traditions carried to the island under
slavery, and the dish stayed affordable and common through much of the
island's colonial history because both its main ingredients were cheap and
easy to grow <Cite ref="dine-bajan-coucou"/>.

Flying fish is what turns the dish from a starch into a plate. The species
was once so abundant in the waters around Barbados that the island earned the
nickname "the land of flying fish," and the particular skill of catching and
boning it for the table was refined there over generations
<Cite ref="dine-bajan-coucou"/>. Steamed in a light gravy of onion, thyme and
lime or seasoned and fried, the fillets are served over the mash, and the
cou-cou itself — worked smooth in its pot with a wooden swizzle stick called
a "cou-cou stick" <Cite ref="caribnewsroom-flying-fish"/> — is what gives the
dish its name.

Cou-cou and flying fish was formally recognised as Barbados' national dish in
the mid-twentieth century <Cite ref="dine-bajan-coucou"/>
<Cite ref="caribnewsroom-flying-fish"/>, a status that
persists even as flying fish stocks in Barbadian waters have declined in
recent decades <Cite ref="dine-bajan-coucou"/>. It remains most closely
associated with Fridays, when it is served in homes and at local restaurants
across the island.
