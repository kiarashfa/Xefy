Bucatini all'amatriciana takes its name from Amatrice, a town in the province of
Rieti in Lazio, and the sauce is generally traced to that place
<Cite ref="wiki-amatriciana"/> <Cite ref="gazzetta-amatriciana"/>. Its first form had
no tomato at all. Shepherds on long seasonal journeys carried aged guanciale and
pecorino, which keep well, and the two were eaten with pasta as a dish called
gricia <Cite ref="gazzetta-amatriciana"/>. The amatriciana of today is that dish
with tomato added, and the tomato came late: it entered at the end of the
eighteenth century <Cite ref="wiki-amatriciana"/> <Cite ref="gazzetta-amatriciana"/>.

The first written record of pasta with tomato sauce is the 1790 cookbook
*L'Apicio Moderno* by the Roman cook Francesco Leonardi
<Cite ref="wiki-amatriciana"/> <Cite ref="gazzetta-amatriciana"/>. The
Gazzetta del Gusto dates the first amatriciane to the beginning of the nineteenth
century and places their earliest spread in upper Lazio, around Amatrice
<Cite ref="gazzetta-amatriciana"/>. The dish became popular in Rome towards the
end of the nineteenth century and into the twentieth
<Cite ref="gazzetta-amatriciana"/>.

Guanciale, pecorino and tomato have been its core since the tomato arrived, and
the ingredients have changed little since. Long pasta shapes, bucatini and
spaghetti among them, became its usual partners only later
<Cite ref="gazzetta-amatriciana"/>.
