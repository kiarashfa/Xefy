Gyoza is a recent arrival in Japanese food by the standards of most of the
dishes it now sits beside. Its usual route into the country runs through
Manchukuo, the Japanese-controlled state in northeast China, and through the
Japanese settlers who returned from it after the end of the Second World War
carrying jiaozi recipes with them <Cite ref="wiki-gyoza"/>. Many of those
returnees had cooked and eaten the dish for years in Manchuria and opened
noodle stalls back in Japan once they were home; jiaozi, refashioned as gyoza,
was often what they sold alongside the ramen <Cite ref="wiki-gyoza"/>. The word
itself is thought to come not from Mandarin but from Manchu, borrowed as
*giyose* by Japanese settlers before it settled into its current
pronunciation <Cite ref="wiki-gyoza"/>.

The dish changed once it took root. Japanese gyoza carry noticeably more
garlic than the Chinese original, to the point that the smell of a gyoza
restaurant is part of how the dish is recognised <Cite ref="wiki-gyoza"/>.
Commercial gyoza wrappers in Japan are also thinner than a typical Chinese
jiaozi skin, a difference traced partly to machine-rolling becoming the norm
in Japanese production in a way it did not for home-made jiaozi
<Cite ref="wiki-gyoza"/>. And where jiaozi is boiled as often as it is fried,
gyoza in Japan is overwhelmingly a pan-fried dish, cooked in rows in a flat pan
until the bases fuse into a single crisp sheet — the form most people outside
Japan now picture first when they hear the word "dumpling" at all.
