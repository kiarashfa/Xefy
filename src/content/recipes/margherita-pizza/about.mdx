The story is that on the tenth of June 1889 the Neapolitan pizzaiolo Raffaele
Esposito baked three pizzas for Queen Margherita of Savoy, that she preferred the
one in the colours of the new Italian flag — red tomato, white mozzarella, green
basil — and that it took her name <Cite ref="wiki-margherita"/>.

It is a very good story and it does not survive much examination. No newspaper of
the period reports the visit, and both the story and the name appear to have been
promoted only in the 1930s and 1940s, decades after the fact
<Cite ref="wiki-margherita"/>. The document usually offered as proof — a
thank-you note from the royal household — has had its authenticity seriously
questioned, and the historian Zachary Nowak has examined the seal it carries
against other seals of the period as part of a broader argument that the whole
episode is an invented tradition <Cite ref="nowak-2014"/>.

The more telling problem is that the pizza is older than its own origin story. In
1849 Emanuele Rocco described Neapolitan pizza topped with basil, tomatoes and
thin slices of mozzarella, and Francesco De Bourcard recorded the same
combinations in 1866 <Cite ref="wiki-margherita"/>. Whatever happened in 1889, it
was not the invention of this pizza. At most a name was attached to something
Naples had been eating for forty years.

None of which makes the dish less itself. What the Margherita actually is, is a
demonstration of how little is needed: a dough given time, tomatoes that are not
cooked before they go on, cheese that is torn rather than grated, and heat
violent enough to finish the whole thing before the base dries out. The reason it
travels so badly is that the last of those is the one thing a domestic kitchen
cannot supply — which is why the home-oven version here trades ninety seconds of
ferocious heat for a day of cold fermentation.
