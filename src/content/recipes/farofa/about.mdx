Cassava flour is Indigenous to Brazil, but farofa itself is not, and the
distinction matters more than it first appears. The sociologist Carlos
Alberto Dória points out that Indigenous cooking added seasoning to a
finished dish rather than working spices and fat into a flour as it toasted
— the technique that defines farofa — which places its invention later, with
enslaved cooks rather than the people who first domesticated the plant
<Cite ref="continente-farofa"/>. The word itself supports an African route:
researcher Maria Lecticia Monteiro Cavalcanti traces "farofa" to *falofa*, a
term from Kimbundo, a Bantu language spoken in Angola, describing flour
worked with fat or water <Cite ref="continente-farofa"/>.

That the flour itself was already central to Brazilian and Atlantic life by
the time farofa emerged is well documented. Manioc flour had been a
provisioning staple across the Portuguese Atlantic system since the late
sixteenth century, packed as standard rations on slave ships alongside
soldiers' and sailors' supplies, and by the eighteenth and nineteenth
centuries it made up the great majority of food entering at least one major
colonial storehouse in Salvador <Cite ref="redalyc-mandioca"/>. Farofa, in
other words, was built from an ingredient that already sustained the ships
and garrisons of an empire, adapted into a seasoned, fat-cooked side dish by
the enslaved cooks who prepared food within that same system.

One of the earliest written descriptions of the dish comes from the
American traveller Daniel Parish Kidder, who recorded manioc flour prepared
with fat, pepper and vinegar under the name farofa in his account of
travelling through Brazil's northern provinces <Cite ref="continente-farofa"/>.
From that base — flour, fat, seasoning — cooks have added bacon, egg,
banana, olives and dried meat ever since, but the dry-toasted, richly fatted
method itself has not changed.
