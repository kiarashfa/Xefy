The dish's standard origin story names a real, documented person: Ding
Baozhen, a Qing dynasty governor of Sichuan whose household chefs are
credited with inventing a stir-fried chicken and peanut dish in his honour,
later named for the title he held, *gongbao*, roughly "palace guardian"
<Cite ref="wiki-kungpao"/> <Cite ref="sixthtone-kungpao"/>.

The documentary trail does not support it nearly as cleanly as the story
suggests. A catalogue of nearly a hundred Chengdu dishes compiled in 1902,
only sixteen years after Ding's death, includes nothing resembling kung pao
chicken, and the dish does not appear in Chinese cookbooks until the 1950s —
and even then in strikingly inconsistent forms, some built on pork rather
than chicken, some leaving out the peanuts entirely <Cite ref="sixthtone-kungpao"/>.
A recognisably standard version, with the chicken, chilli, Sichuan pepper and
peanuts fixed in something like their current proportions, does not settle
into place until the 1990s <Cite ref="sixthtone-kungpao"/>. Whether Ding
Baozhen ever ate anything resembling the dish that now carries his name is,
on this evidence, simply not something the record can answer.

What has stayed constant through that unsettled history is the balance the
dish is built to strike: dried chilli for heat, Sichuan pepper for the
tingling numbness particular to the region, and peanuts added only at the
very end to keep their crunch against the soft chicken and glossy sauce.
