Chilli peppers are American plants, and nothing resembling this condiment
existed in Sichuan before Europeans carried the pepper to Asia following
Columbus's voyages, sometime in the sixteenth century
<Cite ref="scmp-chilli"/>. What is less expected is how long the pepper then
took to reach Sichuan's own kitchens. Neighbouring Guizhou was already using
chillies as a salt substitute by the late seventeenth or eighteenth century,
but no surviving record shows Sichuan cooks doing the same at that time, and
the province's own authoritative cookbooks from the mid-eighteenth century
still make no mention of hot peppers at all <Cite ref="whc-wang"/>. Sichuan's
identity as China's chilli-hot cuisine only takes shape in written sources
during the nineteenth century, and by 1909 a traveller could describe local
diners as unable to imagine a meal without extreme spice
<Cite ref="whc-wang"/>.

Before the chilli arrived, the province's heat came from an older, unrelated
spice: Sichuan pepper, whose numbing quality is why the cuisine's
characteristic sensation is called *málà*, "numbing and hot," rather than heat
alone. Chilli oil brings both together deliberately (chilli flakes for
colour and burn, Sichuan pepper infused into the same oil for the tingling
numbness) rather than treating either as a substitute for the other, and the
same balance the two ingredients strike is what separates Sichuan's
condiments and braises from a plain chilli heat found elsewhere in Chinese
cooking <Cite ref="scmp-chilli"/>.

Once adopted, the chilli spread through Sichuan cooking with unusual speed
and thoroughness, ending up in pastes, sauces and infused oils rather than
staying a garnish, and chilli oil is one of the simplest and most direct
results of that adoption: a way of capturing the pepper's colour and aroma in
a form stable enough to keep on hand and spoon over almost anything.
