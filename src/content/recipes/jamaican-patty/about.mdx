The pastry shell owes its shape to Britain, not the Caribbean. "Patty"
shares its root with "pasty," "pasta" and "pâté," and the hand pie itself
descends from the Cornish pasty brought to Jamaica during the colonial
period, alongside Spanish-introduced empanadas as a second European
influence on the form <Cite ref="pier21-patty"/> <Cite ref="jmcc-patty"/>.
What went inside, and the colour of the crust around it, are where Jamaica
made the dish its own: enslaved African cooks adapted the pastry with local
and imported ingredients, and turmeric brought by the Indian spice
trade — later reinforced by indentured Indian labourers who arrived after
emancipation — gave the crust the yellow colour that identifies a Jamaican
patty at a glance <Cite ref="pier21-patty"/> <Cite ref="jmcc-patty"/>.

The patty's most improbable chapter happened not in Jamaica but in Toronto.
By the mid-1980s, patty shops — many founded by Chinese-Jamaican families
who built the Tastee and Juici Patties chains into commercial institutions —
were well established in Canada's large Jamaican community
<Cite ref="pier21-patty"/>. In February 1985, a federal food inspector
argued that a pastry-wrapped product could not legally be called a "patty"
under meat inspection rules, a challenge that provoked public protest from
Toronto's Jamaican community until the Jamaican Consulate intervened and a
compromise was reached: the product would be sold as a "Jamaican patty"
<Cite ref="pier21-patty"/> <Cite ref="jmcc-patty"/>. Toronto still marks
February the twenty-third as Jamaican Patty Day in memory of the dispute
<Cite ref="pier21-patty"/> <Cite ref="jmcc-patty"/>.
