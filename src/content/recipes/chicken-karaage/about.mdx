Karaage is a Japanese method of frying meat or vegetables coated in flour or starch. Outside Japan
the word usually means soy-marinated fried chicken. Food in Japan notes that it can be written with
two kanji: 唐, for Tang China, which suggests a Chinese-style technique, or 空, for empty, which
suggests frying without batter. Sources disagree on the earliest use of the second form, and many
publishers write からあげ in kana <Cite ref="fij-karaage"/>.

A frequently cited account credits Mikasa Kaikan, a restaurant in Ginza, Tokyo, with adding an
inexpensive fried chicken dish in 1932 to help a struggling business. Customers reportedly liked it
at once, and other restaurants copied it <Cite ref="fij-karaage"/>, <Cite ref="tokyo-mikasa"/>. The Japan
Karaage Association recognises it as the first karaage <Cite ref="tokyo-mikasa"/>. Chinese-style restaurants also
helped to popularise chicken karaage in the 1930s <Cite ref="fij-karaage"/>.

Local accounts in Oita trace boneless karaage to Chinese restaurants in the city of Usa during the
post-war decades. Takeaway shops then appeared in neighbouring Nakatsu, where customers ordered by
weight. The Usa origin rests on those local accounts, and the description of Nakatsu as the dish's
home is the city's own <Cite ref="fij-karaage"/>.

The rise of karaage in the home is tied to chicken becoming cheap. Imported broiler breeds made
chicken affordable, and karaage moved from restaurants into domestic kitchens <Cite ref="fij-karaage"/>.
