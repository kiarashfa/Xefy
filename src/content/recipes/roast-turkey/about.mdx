The only surviving contemporary account of the 1621 gathering at Plymouth
Colony, written by the colonist Edward Winslow, records venison, fish and corn
without ever mentioning turkey by name; wild "fowl" is mentioned, and that more
plausibly meant duck or goose, which were hunted in far greater numbers at the
time <Cite ref="wiki-firstthanksgivingmyth"/>. The idea that turkey was the
meal's centrepiece is a later addition to the story rather than a detail
recovered from it.

Turkey's fixed place at the holiday table is largely the achievement of one
writer. Sarah Josepha Hale, editor of Godey's Lady's Book, began campaigning
in 1827 for Thanksgiving to be declared a national holiday, writing letters to
governors, senators and presidents for 36 years, and in laying out her vision
of the ideal Thanksgiving dinner she placed turkey at its centre — long after
1621, and with no documented link to what was actually eaten there
<Cite ref="history-turkeymeal"/>. Abraham Lincoln proclaimed the national
holiday in 1863, and the turkey Hale had promoted came with it
<Cite ref="smithsonian-thanksgivinghistory"/>.

Its practical popularity followed the same logic that keeps it in ovens today:
a bird large enough to feed an extended family from a single roast, raised
widely enough across the country to be affordable everywhere by the time the
holiday became fixed on the calendar. The centrepiece is a nineteenth-century
convention dressed as a colonial one — which does nothing to change how it is
best roasted.
