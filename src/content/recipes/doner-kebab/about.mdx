Meat roasted on a rotisserie is an old idea in Ottoman cooking. The earliest known
depictions are two miniature paintings by an Istanbul artist, dated to between 1616
and 1620, showing a horizontal spit turned over an open fire <Cite ref="natgeo-doner"/>.
The dish stayed horizontal for the next two centuries.

The vertical spit — the technique that defines döner as it is known today — is a
19th-century Bursa innovation, and even that is disputed. Family accounts credit
İskender Efendi, who is said to have started roasting lamb upright at his father's
restaurant in Bursa in the 1850s, but he may have been preceded by Hamdi Usta of
Kastamonu, working around 1830 <Cite ref="wiki-doner"/>. A photograph taken between
1853 and 1855 by the British photographer James Robertson shows an unidentified
vendor already selling meat from a vertical spit, ahead of either man's claimed date
<Cite ref="natgeo-doner"/>. Standing the spit upright let the fat baste the meat as it
turned and let a cook build layers of different cuts rather than roasting one whole
joint, and it is generally read as a space-saving move in crowded Istanbul kitchens
<Cite ref="wiki-doner"/>.

The dish now eaten as a sandwich is a later and separate invention. Between 1960 and
1973 nearly a million Turkish workers migrated to West Germany, and it was among them,
in Berlin, that döner met flatbread. Kadir Nurman is widely credited with selling the
first flatbread filled with sliced döner meat from a stand at Zoo Station in 1972, a
claim the Association of Turkish Döner Manufacturers formally endorsed in 2011
<Cite ref="natgeo-doner"/>.

The vertical spit did not stay in Turkey. Greek refugees carried it to Greece in the
1920s, where it became gyros, and the technique separately reached the Levant as
shawarma <Cite ref="wiki-doner"/>. Lebanese migrants later carried shawarma to Mexico,
where it was adapted again into tacos al pastor — three dishes descended from the same
Ottoman spit, each shaped by the meat, marinade and bread of the kitchen it landed in.
