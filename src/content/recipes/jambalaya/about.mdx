Nobody agrees on where the word "jambalaya" comes from. The most repeated
theory joins *jambon*, French for ham, to *ya* or *yaya*, said to mean grain
in some West African languages; a second derives it instead from the Spanish
*jamón* fused with *paella*; a third traces it to *jambalaia*, an existing
word in the Provençal dialect of French for a mixed rice dish, with one
culinary historian proposing a simpler link to the English "jumble" instead
<Cite ref="parishes-jambalaya"/>. The dish itself is dated more confidently
than its name: the earliest known recipe appears in an Alabama cookbook of
1878, and the first Louisiana version is printed in *La Cuisine Creole* in
1885 <Cite ref="parishes-jambalaya"/>.

The dish also is not one thing but two, and the difference is not
decorative. Creole jambalaya, the "red" version associated with New Orleans,
builds its color and sweetness from tomatoes, cooked into the trinity of
onion, celery and green pepper before the rice goes in, often alongside
seafood <Cite ref="tastingtable-jambalaya"/>. Cajun jambalaya, the "brown"
version from the rural bayou parishes settled by Acadian exiles, leaves
tomatoes out entirely; its color and depth come instead from how hard the
meat — usually andouille sausage and tasso ham — is browned, and from the
dark fond that browning leaves on the bottom of the pot for the rice to cook
in <Cite ref="tastingtable-jambalaya"/>. Both trace part of their method to
Spanish paella, adapted in Louisiana to ingredients paella never used, which
is one reason no single etymology for the name has ever won out.
