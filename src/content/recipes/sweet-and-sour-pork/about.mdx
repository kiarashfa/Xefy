Sweet and sour pork is widely traced to Guangdong province, where late
nineteenth-century cooks are said to have reworked a simpler pork dish using
ingredients brought in by foreign merchants, adapting it to suit foreign
appetites alongside a local clientele <Cite ref="vice-sweetandsour"/>
<Cite ref="wiki-sweetandsour"/>.

Even the dish's Cantonese name, *gu lou yuk*, is older and less settled than
that adaptation history might suggest. One theory holds that *gu lou* is pure
onomatopoeia, imitating the gulping sound of eating something this rich; a
second holds that the same syllables, said with different tones, mean
"ancient," pointing to the dish's own long pedigree rather than to any
sound at all. Neither explanation has displaced the other, and the dish's
own history is repeated as one of Cantonese cooking's open questions rather
than a settled fact <Cite ref="scmp-sweetandsour"/>.

The dish followed its Cantonese cooks abroad. Guangdong supplied the first
large wave of Chinese emigration to North America (over three hundred
thousand people entered the United States between 1852 and 1882, before the
Chinese Exclusion Act cut the flow off), and the restaurants those emigrants
opened, adapting their recipes further for European-American customers, made
sweet and sour pork one of the first Chinese dishes many Western diners ever
ate <Cite ref="vice-sweetandsour"/>. That
early role as an introduction to Chinese food, rather than an insider's
dish, is also why it is sometimes dismissed as inauthentic, a judgment that
mistakes the dish's original purpose, appealing across a cultural divide, for
a failure to do so.
