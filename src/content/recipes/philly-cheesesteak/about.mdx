The sandwich began as a change of lunch rather than a plan. In 1930, Pat
Olivieri, who ran a hot dog cart in South Philadelphia, grilled chopped beef
and onion instead and put it in a roll — with no cheese at all
<Cite ref="natgeo-cheesesteak"/>. Cheese, usually credited to provolone added
at one of Olivieri's own locations around a decade later, came after the
sandwich had already found its audience, which makes "cheesesteak" a
somewhat late name for the dish it now describes.

Ownership of the invention itself is not settled. Olivieri's business, later
known as Pat's King of Steaks, is the account most often repeated, but his own
family spent decades in dispute over credit, and a rival stand, Geno's Steaks,
opened directly across the intersection from Pat's in 1966 and has maintained
its own competing claim to the sandwich ever since
<Cite ref="phillymag-cheesesteak"/>. The two stands still face each other on
the same corner, each selling its own version as the original.

What is not in dispute is the technique that defines it: the meat is sliced
thin enough to chop and shred on the griddle as it cooks, rather than seared
whole and left in strips, which is what separates a cheesesteak from the many
other thin-sliced beef sandwiches served elsewhere in the country.
