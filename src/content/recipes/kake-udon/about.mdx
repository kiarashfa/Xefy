Udon was more popular in Kyoto and Osaka than in Edo during the Edo period, where soba
dominated, according to Kitagawa Morisada's account as Nippon.com quotes it
<Cite ref="nippon-udon"/>. Both udon in Kyoto and Osaka and soba in Edo were called
*nihachi*, meaning two-eights. One theory ties the name to a price of sixteen mon, which
the article says fits all-wheat udon <Cite ref="nippon-udon"/>.

The broths differ by region. Kansai broth was light in flavour, against the stronger
broth of Kantō <Cite ref="nippon-udon"/>. The same article describes kake, in its account of soba, as noodles served
in a bowl with hot broth poured over them, as against the cold dipping form
<Cite ref="nippon-udon"/>.

The origin of udon is told as legend in the places that make it best known. The Japan
Tourism Agency's entry on Sanuki udon, from Takinomiya in Kagawa, gives the ninth-century
origin credited to the priest Kūkai as legend and tradition rather than verified history
<Cite ref="mlit-sanuki"/>. The documented history it gives is later: by the Edo period
(1603–1867) noodles much like today's were served to pilgrims from all over Japan on
their way through Takinomiya to Kotohiragu Shrine <Cite ref="mlit-sanuki"/>.
