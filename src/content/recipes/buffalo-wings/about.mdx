Buffalo wings are usually dated precisely: 1964, at the Anchor Bar in Buffalo,
New York, made by Teressa Bellissimo. Past that point the story splits. One
version has her improvising with a batch of chicken wings delivered by mistake
in place of the necks she had ordered for a sauce; another, told by her own
son, has her simply frying up a late-night snack for him and his friends after
a night out <Cite ref="tastingtable-wings"/>. Notably, a 1969 newspaper profile
of the Anchor Bar does not mention the dish at all, which sits awkwardly with
a firm 1964 invention date <Cite ref="dailymeal-wings"/>.

The credit is also not uncontested outside the family. John Young ran a
Buffalo restaurant called Wings and Things around the same period, serving his
own whole wings in a sauce he said was adapted from a similar dish he had seen
in Washington, D.C.; he maintained that Frank Bellissimo, Teressa's husband,
had eaten at his restaurant before wings turned up at the Anchor Bar. The
paper trail for either claim is thin — dates are uncertain, no contemporary
account corroborates one version over the other, and it remains possible that
both cooks were independently adapting an idea already circulating around the
city <Cite ref="dailymeal-wings"/>.

What is not in question is the shape the dish settled into and kept: wings
fried unbreaded rather than battered, tossed hot in a cayenne pepper sauce
loosened with butter, and served with celery and a blue cheese dip to cut the
heat. That combination, whoever is responsible for it, is what travelled far
beyond Buffalo and gave the city's name to a way of preparing a chicken wing
rather than to one restaurant's dish.
