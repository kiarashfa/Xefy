The standard account of Texas brisket credits German and Czech immigrants, who
arrived in the Hill Country in the mid-nineteenth century and opened meat
markets that smoked the week's unsold cuts to keep them saleable, a habit that
by the 1920s and 1930s had hardened into the barbecue joints of towns such as
Lockhart and Elgin. That much is well attested. What is less settled is why
brisket in particular — a cheap, tough forequarter cut that most of American
butchery treated as an afterthought — became the centerpiece.

The documentary trail is thinner than the folklore. The earliest newspaper
advertisements for smoked brisket found so far date to 1910, and they were
aimed at Texas's Jewish communities rather than the general public
<Cite ref="tm-brisket-history"/>. Brisket already held a place in Ashkenazi cooking,
where it was prized as an inexpensive cut permitted under dietary law, and
Texas butchers appear to have begun offering it smoked specifically to Jewish
customers in the early twentieth century <Cite ref="moment-brisket"/>. Black's
BBQ in Lockhart, which credits itself as the first Central Texas meat market
to put brisket on the menu by itself, only did so in the late 1950s — decades
after Central Texas's German and Czech butchers were already smoking beef,
and mostly because customers had until then simply asked for "beef" and been
handed whichever cut, fatty or lean, the counter had on hand
<Cite ref="tm-brisket-history"/>. One
turning point often cited is Walter Jetton, the caterer who served brisket at
Lyndon Johnson's ranch barbecues and helped put it in front of a national
audience <Cite ref="moment-brisket"/>.

So the general immigrant-meat-market story and the specific rise of brisket
are two claims of different strength: the markets are documented, but the leap
from "German and Czech butchers smoked beef" to "brisket became Texas
barbecue's defining cut" runs through a narrower, later, and less-repeated
history than the usual telling allows.

What is not in dispute is the technique that resulted: a whole, largely
untrimmed cut, seasoned with nothing but salt and coarse pepper, smoked low
over post oak for most of a day until the connective tissue running through it
has broken down, then rested and sliced against the grain. Sauce is beside the
point — in Central Texas barbecue it is served on the side, if at all, and the
smoke and the meat are expected to stand on their own.
