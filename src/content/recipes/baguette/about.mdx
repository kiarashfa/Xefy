The baguette is one of the most recognisable breads in the world and one of the least firmly documented. Long, thin loaves existed in France well before the word that now names them came into common use. A slimmer loaf already went by the name *flûte*, and the baguette spread through Paris in the early twentieth century <Cite ref="sortiraparis-baguette"/>.

The word itself is usually dated to around 1920 <Cite ref="sortiraparis-baguette"/>. The two origin stories told most often are both presented as legends by the sources that carry them. One says Napoleon wanted a loaf that his soldiers could carry easily. The other says knives were banned on metro building sites, so the bread had to be torn by hand rather than cut <Cite ref="sortiraparis-baguette"/>.

The explanation most often offered for the baguette's rise is more mundane. A law of 1919, applied from 1920, stopped bakers working before four in the morning, and a slim loaf bakes quickly enough to be made in the hours that remained <Cite ref="sortiraparis-baguette"/>. It is a plausible account of why the shape spread, not a settled one.

What is firmly defined is the traditional loaf. The bread decree of 1993 is the text that governs a *tradition* loaf: it may be made only from flour, water, salt and yeast, and grains, cereals, nuts, chemical additives and preservatives all take it out of that category <Cite ref="the-local-baguette"/>.
