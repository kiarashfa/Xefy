The name is a description of method rather than flavour: *char siu* is Cantonese
for "fork roasted," after the long metal forks once used to hold strips of pork
over an open flame <Cite ref="wiki-char-siu"/> <Cite ref="michelin-char-siu"/>.
The technique is old — roast
meats appear in Chinese court cooking as far back as the Zhou dynasty — but the
char siu eaten today, sweet, red-tinted and glazed to a shine, is a much more
recent dish than that lineage suggests <Cite ref="michelin-char-siu"/>.

Into the mid-twentieth century, Hong Kong's char siu was usually cut from fatty
pork belly and marinated with fermented black beans and soy, a savoury,
inexpensive cut favoured by working households and known locally as *tor dei*,
built from alternating layers of lean meat and fat <Cite ref="michelin-char-siu"/>.
The sweeter style familiar now developed alongside it, as cooks began brushing
honey or maltose onto the roasted strips for a second pass in the oven,
caramelising the surface and softening the dish's flavour for a wider audience,
children included <Cite ref="michelin-char-siu"/>. Maltose is still what gives
the finished pork its glassy sheen and its faint char at the edges, rather than
a Western-style barbecue sauce.

Char siu has been an everyday fixture of Hong Kong eating since the 1950s, sold
hanging in the windows of *siu mei* roast-meat shops alongside soy sauce
chicken and roast goose <Cite ref="michelin-char-siu"/>. As Hong Kong grew
wealthier and more health-conscious from the 1990s onward, the fattier
belly-based *tor dei* gave ground to leaner cuts such as pork collar, cooked
with the same maltose glaze but less fat to render <Cite ref="michelin-char-siu"/>.
The modern marinade — hoisin sauce, soy sauce, Shaoxing wine and five-spice —
reflects that later, sweeter tradition rather than the black-bean marinades
that came before it <Cite ref="wiki-char-siu"/> <Cite ref="michelin-char-siu"/>.
