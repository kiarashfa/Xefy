The pie's original point was thrift, not any particular meat. The earliest
documented reference is to "cottage pie": the diarist Parson Woodforde
records eating "cottage-pye" on the twenty-ninth of August 1791
<Cite ref="wiki-sp"/><Cite ref="up-sp"/>. The recognisable modern form
appears a little earlier still and may in fact predate Woodforde's dinner:
Maria Rundell's 1806 cookbook *A New System of Domestic Cookery* gives a
recipe for a dish called "Sanders," minced beef or mutton with onion and
gravy under a mashed potato topping; it is a shape any cook today would
recognise <Cite ref="wiki-sp"/><Cite ref="up-sp"/>.

The name "shepherd's pie," specific to lamb, is later still; the earliest
recorded use traced by historians is in an Edinburgh cookbook from 1849,
*The Practice of Cookery and Pastry* <Cite ref="wiki-sp"/><Cite ref="up-sp"/>.
The dish became viable at all only once the potato,
which reached Britain in the 1580s, had gone from a curiosity to an everyday
staple cheap and filling enough to stretch a small amount of meat across a
family meal <Cite ref="histcoop-sp"/>. The now-familiar distinction (lamb
for shepherd's pie, beef for cottage pie) is generally explained along
economic and regional lines, with lamb the more affordable meat in Ireland
and poorer rural Britain and beef more available where cottage pie took
hold <Cite ref="histcoop-sp"/>, though the two names were used loosely
enough, for long enough, that the split is better understood as a later
convention than a rule the dish was born with.

What has not changed is the underlying idea: meat, already cooked or
minced and gently sauced, finished under a potato crust in the same oven
heat that made the topping worth the trouble in the first place.
