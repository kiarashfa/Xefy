Sarmale belongs to a family of wrapped, stuffed dishes — dolma in Turkish —
that the food historian Priscilla Mary Işın traces in Ottoman kitchens back
to at least the early sixteenth century, when a Persian-language cookbook
recorded a recipe for *dulma-ye kalam*, stuffed cabbage, among the earliest
documented versions of the technique <Cite ref="isin-bountiful-empire"/>.
Versions of the dish are found today across the former Ottoman world, each
adapted to a different wrapping leaf and filling, and Romania's is one of
the more distinctive departures from the dish's Ottoman starting point.

Where Ottoman and Middle Eastern versions typically use fresh or brined vine
leaves and lamb, the Romanian version wraps a filling of minced pork, rice
and onion in pickled cabbage leaves and simmers the rolls with smoked bacon
— cabbage suited to a colder climate standing in for vine leaves, and pork
and bacon standing in for the lamb an Ottoman kitchen would have used
<Cite ref="wff-romania"/>. The name itself comes from the Turkish *sarma*,
"to wrap," carried into Romanian along with the technique it describes.

The dish's place in the Romanian calendar is as fixed as its recipe.
Sarmale is eaten year-round but becomes especially prominent around
Christmas, New Year and Easter, prepared as one of the standard dishes of
the holiday table <Cite ref="wff-romania"/>. A dish that arrived
by way of empire has become, in Romania, a marker of season and family
occasion rather than of any foreign origin at all.
