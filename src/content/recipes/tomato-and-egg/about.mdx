Eggs have been cooked in China for thousands of years, but the tomato has
not: as a fruit native to the Americas, it could not reach a Chinese kitchen
before contact with the wider world, which makes this dish, for all its
present-day ubiquity, considerably younger than most of what sits beside it
on a home table.

The standard account places that contact in Shanghai, where Western-style
restaurants serving tomatoes are said to have introduced the ingredient to
local cooks, who folded it into their own cooking before the pairing spread
from restaurant menus into ordinary home kitchens
<Cite ref="wiki-tomatoegg"/> <Cite ref="nyu-tomatoegg"/>. That is not
the only theory on offer: at least
one food historian argues for an older and more eastern route, tracing a
family resemblance between this dish and the egg-and-tomato cooking of the
Middle East, such as shakshuka, and suggesting the pairing may have travelled
along the same trade routes that carried other Silk Road foods into China
long before Shanghai's Western restaurants existed <Cite ref="silkroad-tomatoegg"/>.
Neither account rests on strong documentary evidence, and the dish's own
historians are candid that the record is thinner than its fame would suggest.

What is not in dispute is how completely the dish has settled into home
cooking since. It varies by region in small, telling ways — cooked saltier
in the north, sweetened with a pinch of sugar in the south — but the basic
method, egg scrambled and set aside, then folded back through tomato cooked
down to a loose sauce, is recognised everywhere as one of the first dishes a
home cook in China learns.
