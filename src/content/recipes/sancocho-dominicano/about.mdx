Sancocho's ancestry runs back through Spain before it ever reaches the
Caribbean. Culinary historians trace the stew to cocido, itself thought to
descend from adafina, a meat-and-chickpea stew Spain's Sephardic Jewish
community prepared in advance of the Sabbath, when cooking was forbidden.
One theory holds that sancocho specifically — cocido reworked with African
influence — was first cooked not in Spain but in the Canary Islands, off
the coast of northwest Africa, before Spanish colonisation carried it on to
the Americas <Cite ref="britannica-sancocho"/>.

What the dish became in the Dominican Republic owes at least as much to
Taíno and African contributions as to its Spanish base. European staples
like chickpeas gave way to indigenous ingredients — yuca, yautía, auyama
squash and other roots the Taíno already grew — while enslaved Africans
brought their own cooking knowledge and, on one telling of the dish's
history, its very origin as an everyday meal <Cite ref="britannica-sancocho"/>
<Cite ref="rosario-sancocho"/>. Williams College scholar Nelly A. Rosario
describes this bleaker account directly: a widely held belief traces
sancocho to slavery itself, when scraps thrown to livestock troughs were
little different from what was tossed to the slave quarters, and the dish
later came to mean a "plantation stew," pieced together by enslaved people
and others with little else from whatever crops a neighbour happened to
have <Cite ref="rosario-sancocho"/>.

The dish that history produced is built from a long-simmered broth, a mix
of starchy roots and whatever meat is on hand, and its logic follows from
that: the stew, not any single ingredient in it, is the point.
