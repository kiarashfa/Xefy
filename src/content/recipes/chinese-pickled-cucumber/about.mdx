The cucumber itself is a comparatively late arrival in Chinese cooking. It is
counted among the crops said to have entered China along the trade routes
opened during the Han dynasty, after the explorer and diplomat Zhang Qian's
missions into Central Asia brought Chinese courts into direct contact with
the region for the first time — alongside grapes, pomegranates and garlic,
all foods that arrived from the west rather than growing up alongside rice
and millet in the Chinese heartland <Cite ref="factsanddetails-silkroad"/>
<Cite ref="wiki-zhangqian"/>.

What happened to the cucumber once it arrived is not nearly as well
documented as its arrival, and this particular preparation has no single
attributed inventor or date worth repeating as fact. What can be said with
more confidence is why the dish is made the way it is: striking the cucumber
with the flat of a blade rather than slicing it splits the flesh unevenly
along its own grain, opening up far more surface area than a clean cut would.
That torn surface is what lets a quick salt draw water out efficiently and
lets a dressing of garlic, vinegar and chilli oil cling to and soak into the
cucumber rather than sliding off a smooth, sliced face — a piece of kitchen
logic that explains the technique's staying power better than any origin
story could.
