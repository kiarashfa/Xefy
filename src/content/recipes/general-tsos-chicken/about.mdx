The dish is named after Zuo Zongtang, a nineteenth-century Qing-dynasty
general from Hunan province, but the connection is a borrowed name rather than
a family recipe — he has no documented link to it at all
<Cite ref="natgeo-generaltso"/>. It was created in the twentieth century by
Peng Chang-kuei, a chef trained in Hunanese cooking who fled to Taiwan after
the Chinese Civil War. Peng is credited with inventing the dish in Taipei in
the 1950s for a banquet, in a form far closer to Hunan's sour, hot, salty
cooking than the version now familiar in the United States
<Cite ref="smithsonian-generaltso"/>.

Peng later brought the dish to New York, and it was there, adapting it for
American customers, that it changed into something closer to what it is now:
sweeter, less aggressively spiced, and built around a glossy, sugared glaze
rather than Hunan's sourer, hotter profile <Cite ref="smithsonian-generaltso"/>.
Peng reportedly disapproved of what other restaurants did with his recipe once
it spread beyond his own kitchens, finding the sweetened American version far
removed from what he had intended <Cite ref="natgeo-generaltso"/>.

It belongs, as a result, to American-Chinese cooking rather than to Hunan or
to China more broadly — a dish invented once, in exile, and then reinvented a
second time by the country that adopted it.
