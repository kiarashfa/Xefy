Scallion pancake belongs to *bing*, a broad Chinese category covering
almost any food built from a worked wheat dough — flatbreads, layered
pastries, even some noodles fall inside it. The category is old, and its
earliest documented member was not even originally Chinese: *hubing*, a
flatbread whose name literally marks it "foreign bing," is recorded as
early as the second century BCE, carried into China along the same Silk
Road trade routes that also brought new crops and new milling technology
from Central Asia <Cite ref="scmp-flatbread"/>. Wheat itself only reached
China from further west in the first place, so the entire family of
flatbreads scallion pancake belongs to depends, at its root, on an imported
grain worked with imported techniques.

The scallion pancake itself is a much later and more specific development
within that broad family, defined less by any single documented inventor
than by its particular method: an unleavened dough rolled thin, brushed
with oil and scattered with sliced spring onion, then rolled up and coiled
before being flattened and fried, so that what looks like a single disc of
dough is actually many oiled layers pressed together — the same layering
principle that runs through the wider bing family of flatbreads and pastries
<Cite ref="wiki-congyoubing"/> <Cite ref="scmp-flatbread"/>. That layering is
what gives the finished pancake its particular texture: crisp outside,
distinctly layered and chewy within.
