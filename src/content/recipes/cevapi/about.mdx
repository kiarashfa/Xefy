Ćevapi are told, often, as an Ottoman-era survival: small skewers or logs of
seasoned minced meat said to descend directly from Turkish köfte, carried
into the Balkans by the Ottomans and adapted along the way; *ćevapčići* is
itself a diminutive of the Turkish *kebap*, in turn from Arabic *kabāb*
<Cite ref="wiki-cevapi"/><Cite ref="jutarnji-cevapi"/>. A more specific and
more romantic version of that story credits *hajduks*, the outlaw rebels of
Ottoman-ruled Bosnia, with a precursor dish grilled over an open fire. It is a
claim the documentary record does not support.

Researching the dish's history
in the Dubrovnik archives, Bosnian Franciscan chronicles and other
period sources, the historians Slavojka Beštić-Bronza and Boro Bronza were
able to reconstruct Bosnian eating habits up through the end of the
nineteenth century, and found no trace of ćevapi in any of it. No mention
of a ćevapi vendor in Bosnia appears before 1900, and the dish is missing
from Belgrade's record too until well into the second half of the
nineteenth century <Cite ref="jutarnji-cevapi"/><Cite ref="wiki-cevapi"/>.
The historians point instead to 1878, when the southern Serbian town of
Leskovac, already known for grilling minced meat, was annexed into the
Principality of Serbia, as the pivotal year for the dish's spread; in the
1880s and 1890s, migrants from the newly annexed south filled Belgrade and
opened grill taverns there, the moment at which the minced-meat grill
became attached to Serbian cooking <Cite ref="jutarnji-cevapi"/>. The
hajduk story, in other words, looks like exactly the kind of folk
backdating that turns a nineteenth-century urban food into something that
sounds older and more defiant than the evidence allows.

What is well documented is what happened afterward. The writer Branislav
Nušić was already describing ćevapi as a Yugoslav national dish by the
1920s, and the dish spread west with the creation of Yugoslavia, taking
firm root in Bosnia and reaching as far as Maribor, where the first
recorded ćevapi grill-house opened in 1933
<Cite ref="jutarnji-cevapi"/>. It became, and remains, the everyday food
of Bosnian cities in particular, sold from dedicated grill-houses each
guarding its own recipe.
