Chili con carne's earliest history is murky — dried chillies and stewed meat
are old enough in Mexican cooking that no single point of invention holds up
— but its rise to a national dish is unusually well documented, and it runs
through one place: the plazas of San Antonio. From the 1860s until the early
1940s, women remembered as the "Chili Queens" ran food stands there after
dark, selling chili con carne, tamales and enchiladas to a crowd that
included both locals and travelers drawn by the spectacle
<Cite ref="uiw-chili-queens"/>. Visitors as well known as the writer Stephen
Crane described the scene, and Military Plaza, Alamo Plaza and later Haymarket
Plaza and Milam Park all hosted stands in turn as the city's markets moved
<Cite ref="uiw-chili-queens"/>. The stands were shut down repeatedly by the
city's health department over the decades and closed for good in the early
1940s <Cite ref="uiw-chili-queens"/>.

The dish had already left San Antonio by then. A chili stand at the 1893
World's Columbian Exposition in Chicago is credited with giving many
Americans outside Texas their first taste of it, well before the Chili
Queens' stands closed <Cite ref="utsa-texas-chili"/>. Chili con carne was named the
official state dish of Texas by the state legislature in 1977
<Cite ref="utsa-texas-chili"/>.

Whether chili should contain beans is its own long-running argument rather
than a historical question with an answer: the Texas competition body the
Chili Appreciation Society International forbids beans in its contests
outright — its own rules bar "beans, macaroni, rice, hominy, or other similar
ingredients" from judged chili — and the no-bean, no-tomato version made here
follows that competition standard rather than the beans-and-tomato chili more
commonly cooked at home across the rest of the country
<Cite ref="casi-rules"/>.
