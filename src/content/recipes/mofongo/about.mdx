Mofongo is built from three histories pressed into one mortar. The pounding
itself is a West African technique, brought to Puerto Rico by enslaved
Africans who already made fufu (starchy vegetables boiled and pounded to a
dense mass) from cassava, plantain or yam <Cite ref="wikipedia-mofongo"/>
<Cite ref="lawa-african-roots"/>.
The wooden mortar it is pounded in, the pilón, is Taíno rather than African,
continuing a tool indigenous islanders used long before either plantains or
enslaved cooks arrived. And the seasoning that finishes it (garlic and,
later, sofrito) is the Spanish contribution layered on top.

Even the dish's name carries this layering in miniature. "Mofongo" is
traced to the Kikongo phrase *mfwenge-mfwenge*, meaning roughly "a great
amount of anything at all," a term that food historians have followed back
to Central Africa with unusual confidence for a Caribbean dish name
<Cite ref="wikipedia-mofongo"/> <Cite ref="lawa-african-roots"/>.

Mofongo is also one of the few Puerto Rican dishes with an early paper
trail. It appears under the name "mofongo criollo" in *El Cocinero
Puerto-Riqueño o Formulario*, Puerto Rico's first cookbook, published in
1859 and running to some 850 recipes drawn from around the world and
adapted to Puerto Rican custom <Cite ref="wikipedia-mofongo"/>
<Cite ref="eluniversal-cocinero-puertorriqueno"/>. The dish eaten today,
pounded from fried rather than boiled plantain and studded with crisp pork
chicharrón, has moved some distance from that early recipe, but the core
act (starch pounded soft with pork fat and garlic) has stayed constant
across more than a century and a half.
