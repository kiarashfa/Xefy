Suya's roots are pastoral rather than urban. The dish is traced to the Hausa and
Fulani herding communities of Northern Nigeria, Niger and Cameroon, whose nomadic
way of life across the Sahel put a premium on preserving and cooking meat quickly
over open fires while on the move <Cite ref="wiki-suya"/><Cite ref="tastingtable-suya"/>.
Ground peanuts, dried and long-keeping, suit that context in a way a wet marinade does
not, which is a plausible if not separately documented reason the spice rub took the
form it did. Beyond crediting the Hausa, the record does not go much further: exactly
when and how the dish was first put together is, by one account, still genuinely
unclear <Cite ref="tastingtable-suya"/>.

The dish carries two names, and they track two different audiences. Among Hausa
speakers, it is still generally called *tsire*, its original name; *suya* is the
name that spread with the dish as it moved south and out of the Hausa-speaking
region, and it is now the more widely recognised term across Nigeria as a whole
<Cite ref="wiki-suya"/>. That spread turned a specifically Hausa food into
something closer to a national one — cheap, sold everywhere, and, by one telling,
a genuine unifying thread across a country otherwise divided along regional and
ethnic lines <Cite ref="wiki-suya"/>. The suya sold today, from beef to chicken and
ram, and the version of yaji that seasons it, vary from vendor to vendor, but the
groundnut base and the format of thin-sliced meat rubbed dry and grilled fast remain
constant across the country.
