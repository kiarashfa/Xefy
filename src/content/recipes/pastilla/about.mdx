Pastilla's name is the more settled part of its history. It comes from the
Spanish *pastilla*, meaning pastry, itself from the Latin *pastillum*; carried
into Moroccan Arabic, the initial *p* became a *b*, giving *bastilla*
<Cite ref="wiki-pastilla"/>. The dish it names is generally traced to
al-Andalus, on the strength of a specific comparison rather than a continuous
record: the food historian Rudolf Grewe identified *judhaba*, a chicken pie
sealed under many thin sheets of dough in an anonymous thirteenth-century
Hispano-Muslim cookbook, as a clear forerunner of the modern dish
<Cite ref="arabamerica-bastilla"/>. Scholar Anny Gaul has pointed to the same
period, describing recipes combining pigeon with cinnamon, almonds, saffron
and egg that closely resemble bastilla's filling today
<Cite ref="wiki-pastilla"/>.

The pastry itself has a separate and more contested history. Warqa, the sheet
the pie is built from, is sometimes assumed to have travelled to Morocco with
Muslims and Jews expelled from Spain at the end of the fifteenth century — but
other food historians propose a later and quite different route, arguing that
paper-thin warqa dough only reached Morocco after 1830, brought by Algerian
migrants during the period of Ottoman rule in Algeria, spreading out from
Tétouan from there <Cite ref="wiki-pastilla"/>. The two theories are not
easily reconciled, and no source consulted here settles between them.

What is not disputed is the dish's association with Fez, where pastilla
became a refined dish served at royal banquets and where a dessert variant,
*jowhara*, later developed from it <Cite ref="wiki-pastilla"/>. The sweet
dusting of cinnamon and icing sugar over a filling of chicken, egg and
saffron-spiced almonds — savoury food finished as though it were dessert — is
the detail every account of the dish's Fassi refinement returns to, whichever
account of its arrival in Morocco is believed.
