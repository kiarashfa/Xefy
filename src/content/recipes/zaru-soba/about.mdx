Soba was sold widely in Edo, the city now called Tokyo. Kitagawa
Morisada's account, quoted by Nippon.com, records soba shops in Edo from the middle of the
seventeenth century, and says that for every noodle shop in the shopping streets of Kyoto
and Osaka there would be four or five in Edo <Cite ref="nippon-soba"/>.

Cold soba was served as mori. The noodles were piled in a steaming basket called a seiro and
eaten with tsuyu, a dipping sauce, and the article contrasts that with kake, in which the
noodles sit in a bowl and hot broth is poured over them <Cite ref="nippon-soba"/>. Morisada
judged the Kantō broth to be the stronger of the two regional styles, made for dipping
soba, while the Kansai broth was for drinking <Cite ref="nippon-soba"/>.

The zaru is the bamboo tray or sieve the noodles are served on, and the sieve lets the
rinsed noodles drain without going soggy <Cite ref="food-in-japan-zaru"/>.

The tsuyu itself is built from soy sauce, mirin and dashi. One recipe in Food in Japan
makes the concentrate from sake, mirin, soy sauce and shaved bonito, and the same article
describes the base as soy, mirin and dashi <Cite ref="food-in-japan-zaru"/>.
