The name promises something the bread does not deliver: *pandesal* is
Spanish for "bread of salt," from *pan* and *sal*, but the roll eaten today
is mild and faintly sweet rather than salty
<Cite ref="wiki-pandesal"/> <Cite ref="lolainay-pandesal"/>.
That mismatch is a clue to its age — the name is older than the recipe it
now describes.

Wheat and bread both arrived with Spanish colonisation: friars grew wheat in
Cagayan Valley, the Ilocos provinces and Batangas and milled it for their own
bread and communion wafers, teaching the practice to Filipino bakers
<Cite ref="manilastandard-pandesal"/>. Pandesal's earliest form, *pan de
suelo*, is described as closer to a crusty loaf baked directly on the oven
floor than to the soft roll sold today <Cite ref="wiki-pandesal"/>
<Cite ref="manilastandard-pandesal"/>. Wheat itself was never grown at scale
in the Philippines for long, so the bread's ingredients stayed an import
rather than a local crop, which left it exposed to whatever flour happened
to be cheap and available. As cheaper, softer American wheat flour became
widely available during the American colonial period, bakers shifted toward
it, and the bread's texture softened into the fluffy, slightly sweet roll
associated with pandesal now
<Cite ref="wiki-pandesal"/> <Cite ref="lolainay-pandesal"/>.

What survives from the earlier savoury version is mostly the name. Modern
pandesal is typically rolled in fine breadcrumbs rather than salt before
baking <Cite ref="wiki-pandesal"/> <Cite ref="noseychef-pandesal"/>, and its
appeal now rests on being soft,
warm and cheap enough to eat daily — an everyday breakfast bread rather than
the salted loaf its name still describes.
