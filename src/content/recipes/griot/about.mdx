Griot's name is not settled fact but a theory, and the theory is worth stating
as one: some trace it to the griots of West Africa, the hereditary oral
historians and storytellers who held a respected, elevated place in their
societies, on the reasoning that the Haitian dish was itself originally a
food of the island's highest social class rather than everyday fare
<Cite ref="travelnoire-griyo"/>. Only over time did it move from that
restricted table to the popular dish eaten across the country today
<Cite ref="travelnoire-griyo"/>.

The technique is what carries the West African connection more reliably than
the name does: marinating meat before cooking it, in a wet paste of
aromatics and acid rather than a dry rub, is a practice many enslaved West
Africans brought with them to Haiti and adapted to what the island grew,
blending with Taíno foodways already on the island into the paste known
today as epis <Cite ref="travelnoire-griyo"/> <Cite ref="wikipedia-epis"/>. In
griot's case that marinade is a wet base of garlic, peppers, herbs and citrus
pounded or blended together, into which pork shoulder is submerged before it
is boiled tender and then fried until the surface crisps and browns. Sour
orange is the traditional souring agent, with lime standing in where sour
orange is not to be had <Cite ref="travelnoire-griyo"/>.

Griot is eaten across Haiti today and, alongside red beans and rice, is
among the dishes most closely identified with the country's cooking. It is
served with pikliz, a sharp,
vinegared cabbage and scotch bonnet slaw, whose heat and acidity cut through
the pork's fried richness — the same combination of tang against fat that
the epis marinade builds in from the start.
