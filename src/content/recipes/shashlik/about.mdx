The word gives the history away. *Shashlik* comes from Crimean Tatar *şış*, "spit,"
borrowed into Russian by the Zaporozhian Cossacks in the eighteenth century
<Cite ref="wiki-shashlik"/>. Accounts of the exact root differ — one reading gives
Crimean Tatar *шыш* as "piece," with *шашлык* as "in pieces," rather than a word for
the spit itself <Cite ref="folkways-shashlyk"/> — but they agree on the route: a
Turkic word entering Russian from the Crimea in that century. Before that borrowing,
Russian used its own term for skewered meat, cooked *на вертеле*, "on a spit," with
the dish itself called *vertelnoye* <Cite ref="wiki-shashlik"/><Cite ref="folkways-shashlyk"/>
— so the dish and its cooking method were already established across the Caucasus and
Central Asia among Turkic and Caucasian peoples well before the word, or the dish
under that name, became part of Russian cuisine.

Shashlik's spread through the Russian Empire tracks a specific piece of nineteenth-
century political history. It became widely known in Russia only after Georgia,
Azerbaijan and part of Armenia were absorbed into the empire in the mid-nineteenth
century, and it remained largely unknown in Moscow until the century's later decades
<Cite ref="wiki-shashlik"/>. From there it moved quickly: shashlik was an established
feature of St Petersburg restaurant menus by the 1910s and had become a common street
food across urban Russia within another decade <Cite ref="wiki-shashlik"/>. What
reads today as a distinctively Russian dish is, by its own name, a Caucasian and
Central Asian one that Russian cuisine adopted comparatively late.
