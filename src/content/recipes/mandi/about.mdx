Mandi's name is a description of what the pit oven does to the meat rather than a
place name: it comes from the Arabic *nada*, "dew," for the moist, almost
falling-apart texture the technique produces <Cite ref="wiki-mandi"/>. Both
sources that discuss its origin place the dish in Hadhramaut, the region of
eastern and southern Yemen that gives the wider governorate its name, and from
there its spread across the Arabian Peninsula and beyond followed the region's own
long history as a stop on the incense and spice trade routes that also carried
myrrh, frankincense and coffee <Cite ref="thenational-yemeni-cuisine"/>.

The technique behind mandi — meat suspended above rice inside a sealed pit or
clay oven, cooking by the trapped heat and dripping its juices down onto the
grains — is also among the better-documented parts of this whole family of
Arabian rice dishes. Food historian Nawal Nasrallah has pointed to a recipe for
pit-roasted whole lamb in a thirteenth-century Aleppan cookbook that closely
resembles the modern *zarb* variant of the technique, and the Palestinian
ethnographer Tawfiq Canaan described a "hermetically closed," cave-like pit oven
used for the same purpose as early as 1927 <Cite ref="wiki-mandi"/>. Neither
source claims this as mandi's own point of invention rather than a record of the
same underlying pit-cooking method being used across the region; what the
citations establish is the age and spread of the technique rather than a single
origin for the dish that carries mandi's specific name today.

From Hadhramaut, mandi travelled with Yemeni traders and migrants across the
Gulf, and in the twentieth century specifically with Yemeni communities who
settled in Hyderabad's Barkas neighbourhood, carrying the dish into a city
already known for its own biryani tradition <Cite ref="wiki-mandi"/>.
