Baozi and mantou share one origin story. Both trace back to the same
legendary steamed bun, said to have been invented filled with meat and shaped
like a human head, and both were once covered by the single term *mantou*
<Cite ref="gbc-bao"/>. The two only became separate dishes later: by the
tenth century, during the Song dynasty, *mantou* had narrowed to mean a plain,
unfilled bun, while the stuffed version took on the name *baozi* and developed
as a dish in its own right <Cite ref="wiki-baozi"/> <Cite ref="gbc-bao"/>.

That split has held ever since. Where mantou is eaten as bread, alongside a
meal, baozi is the meal — a bun built specifically to carry a filling, sealed
with a twisted pleat at the top rather than left plain, and steamed until the
wrapper turns glossy and slightly translucent over the seam. It became
especially popular across northern China, where wheat rather than rice is the
staple grain, and travelled from there into the rest of the country and, with
the Chinese diaspora, well beyond it <Cite ref="gbc-bao"/>.

The filling is where regional and household variation shows itself most: pork
with ginger and spring onion is the everyday standard, but a vegetable filling
built on cabbage, mushroom and tofu is just as long-established, particularly
in Buddhist vegetarian cooking and during periods of fasting. Both versions
keep the same soft, faintly sweet dough and the same pleated, top-knotted
shape that distinguishes a baozi from a jiaozi or a wonton at a glance.
