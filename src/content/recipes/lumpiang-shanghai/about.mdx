*Lumpia* is Hokkien before it is Filipino: the word comes from *lun*, "soft"
or "moist," and *pia*, "cake" or "pastry," carried to the islands by
generations of Hokkien Chinese immigrants
<Cite ref="wiki-lumpiang-shanghai"/> <Cite ref="scmp-lumpia"/>.
The wrapped, rolled shape traces back much further still, to a plain pancake
wrapped around spring vegetables in China's Jin dynasty, a custom that spread
across later dynasties and, carried by Fujianese migration, left its Hokkien
name on related dishes across Southeast Asia long before it had any
Philippine connection <Cite ref="scmp-lumpia"/>.

What arrived in the Philippines diversified quickly into a whole family of
lumpia, distinguished by filling and wrapper rather than by one fixed recipe.
Lumpiang Shanghai — thin, cylindrical, packed with minced pork and
vegetables and rolled small enough to eat in a bite or two — is generally
treated as the most basic and most common of these variants, the one most
likely to turn up at a party table or a fast-food counter
<Cite ref="wiki-lumpiang-shanghai"/> <Cite ref="tastingtable-lumpiashanghai"/>.

Its name is the part nobody has convincingly explained. The dish's own
lineage traces to Fujian and Hokkien-speaking migration, not to Shanghai or
Shanghainese cooking <Cite ref="scmp-lumpia"/>, and food writers covering the
dish likewise conclude the "Shanghai" naming cannot be firmly explained
<Cite ref="wiki-lumpiang-shanghai"/> <Cite ref="tastingtable-lumpiashanghai"/>.
It survives as an open question rather
than a settled fact — much like pancit canton's equally puzzling debt to a
city it has no real tie to.
