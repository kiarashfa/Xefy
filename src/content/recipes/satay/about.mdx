Satay is a Javanese street food, but the grilled, skewered meat it is built from is not
a Javanese invention. Turkish and Arab kebab traditions travelled east along Indian
Ocean trade routes, and Tamil and Gujarati Muslim traders working those same routes
brought their own seasoned, grilled meats to the port cities of Java and Sumatra
<Cite ref="wiki-satay"/>. The word itself carries that history: *satay* descends from
the Malay *satai* or *saté*, which in turn traces to the Tamil *catai*
<Cite ref="seasia-satay"/>.

Local cooks reworked what arrived rather than copying it. Small bamboo skewers and
coconut-shell charcoal replaced the larger metal skewers of a Middle Eastern kebab, cut
to a size suited to a street vendor's stall rather than a spit, and the dish was
seasoned with the spices already in the Javanese kitchen <Cite ref="wiki-satay"/>. The
dish gained ground through the 18th century alongside growing Indian and Arab
immigration, and by the 19th century colonial observers were recording it as
established festival and street food across the archipelago — Dutch documentation from
around 1870 already shows satay vendors in Java, ketupat rice cakes hanging behind
their stalls <Cite ref="seasia-satay"/>.

What most separates satay from the kebabs it descends from is the sauce, not the meat:
a thick, ground-peanut sauce that has no real equivalent in the Arab or Tamil dishes
that inspired the skewer itself. By the late 19th century the dish had crossed the
Strait of Malacca into Malaysia, Singapore and Thailand, each taking the same skewered
technique and giving it a different accompaniment — ketupat in Malaysia, cucumber salad
and sticky rice in Thailand <Cite ref="seasia-satay"/>.
