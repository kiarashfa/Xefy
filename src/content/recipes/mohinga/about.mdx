Mohinga is the closest thing Myanmar has to a national dish: a thick,
lemongrass-scented fish and rice-noodle soup eaten overwhelmingly at
breakfast <Cite ref="wiki-mohinga"/><Cite ref="gbc-mohinga"/>. Few
households make it themselves; it is bought instead from the street
vendors and cafés that compete over it in every town, each with a loyal
following for their own version <Cite ref="gbc-mohinga"/>. It is also
cooked differently almost everywhere it is eaten: the Irrawaddy Delta, Bago
and Upper Myanmar each keep their own version, thickened, seasoned and
finished slightly differently while remaining recognisably the same soup
<Cite ref="wiki-mohinga"/><Cite ref="insightmyanmar-mohinga"/>.

Its early written history, as far as it can be traced in English-language
sources, is thin. Wikipedia's account, drawing on Burmese-language
research, dates the dish's earliest mention to a verse by the poet U
Ponnya during the Konbaung dynasty, Myanmar's last royal house, and holds
that no formal mohinga recipe survives in the royal cookbooks that
recorded other, more prestigious court dishes of the same period. This is taken
by at least one Burmese historian as a sign the dish began as ordinary,
market-stall cooking rather than anything grander. That account could not
be independently confirmed against an English-language source for this
entry, so it is reported here as Wikipedia's account rather than as a
settled fact.

What is not in question is the noodle underneath it. Rice vermicelli
predates mohinga by a long way (equipment for fermenting rice and
producing noodles has been found from the much earlier Pyu city-states),
so the dish inherited its starch from a far older regional tradition
rather than inventing one <Cite ref="wiki-mohinga"/><Cite ref="gbc-mohinga"/>.
