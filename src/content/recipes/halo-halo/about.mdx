The name describes the eating, not any one ingredient: *halo-halo* comes from
the Filipino verb *halò*, "to mix," reduplicated into "mix-mix," an
instruction as much as a title — everything in the glass is meant to be
stirred together before it is eaten <Cite ref="wiki-halohalo"/>
<Cite ref="sbs-halohalo"/>.

Before it had that name, or its present crowd of toppings, it was a much
simpler snack made by the Japanese who lived in the Philippines before the
Second World War: a version of Japan's own shaved-ice *kakigōri*, sweet beans
heaped with ice <Cite ref="sbs-halohalo"/> <Cite ref="wiki-halohalo"/>. The
cold snack was called *mongo-ya*, after its main ingredient, the mung bean —
a predecessor recorded by the Japanese businessman Kiyoshi Osawa in his
memoir, *A Japanese in the Philippines* <Cite ref="gma-halohalo"/>
<Cite ref="wiki-halohalo"/>.

Filipino vendors kept building on that base rather than leaving it as it
was, substituting and adding local ingredients until the single-bean snack
had become the crowded, many-layered dessert now sold under its present name
<Cite ref="wiki-halohalo"/> <Cite ref="sbs-halohalo"/>.
