Skirt steak is a long, thin, hard-working muscle from the cow's plate, and for
most of American butchery it was treated as scrap. On the cattle ranches of
South and West Texas in the 1930s, it was among the cuts given to Mexican
vaqueros as part of their pay when an animal was slaughtered, alongside the
hide and less desirable trimmings <Cite ref="austin-chronicle-fajita"/>. Cooked
whole over an open fire and sliced into strips — *fajitas*, a diminutive of
the Spanish *faja*, a belt or sash — it became a standard ranch-hand meal long
before it was a restaurant dish, and long before the word itself reached
English print, which it did no earlier than 1968
<Cite ref="etymonline-fajita"/>.

Its move into commerce is unusually well pinned down for a dish with such
humble roots. In September 1969, Austin meat market manager Sonny Falcón ran
what is credited as the first commercial fajita stand, at a Diez y Seis
celebration in Kyle, Texas, and spent the 1970s taking it to rodeos and fairs
around the state <Cite ref="austin-chronicle-fajita"/>. The same year, Otilia
Garza took over the Round-Up Restaurant in Pharr and began giving fajitas
away to favored customers before putting them on the menu; she is generally
credited with the sizzling cast-iron platter, an idea she said she borrowed
after being served *queso flameado* on hot metal in Acapulco
<Cite ref="tm-low-steaks"/>.

The dish reached a national audience through Houston in the following decade,
when restaurateur Ninfa Rodriguez Laurenzo built her restaurant's reputation
around a version she marketed as *tacos al carbón*
<Cite ref="austin-chronicle-fajita"/>. Demand grew faster than the supply of
skirt steak could easily meet it, and by the mid-1980s many restaurants were
substituting chicken, shrimp and other fillings under the same name — a shift
the dish's own early historians have called ironic, since it was the cut of
meat, not the marinade or the sizzle, that fajitas were originally built
around <Cite ref="austin-chronicle-fajita"/>.
