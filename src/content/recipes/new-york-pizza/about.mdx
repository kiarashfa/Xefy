New York pizza descends from the wave of Neapolitan immigration into the city
around the turn of the twentieth century, brought by cooks who had grown up on
the wood-fired pizza of Naples <Cite ref="smithsonian-pizzafounder"/>. For
decades the story credited one man with starting it here: Gennaro Lombardi,
said to have taken out the country's first pizza-selling license at a grocery
on Spring Street in 1905.

That license has never actually turned up. The researcher Peter Regas spent
years working through immigration records, city directories and old
advertisements and found that Lombardi did not arrive in New York until 1904,
and that the Spring Street address had been run for years before him by other
immigrant bakers — Regas argues the real pioneer was Filippo Milone, who
quietly established several of the city's oldest pizzerias, including the
shops that later became known as Lombardi's and John's of Bleecker Street,
before selling them on to the men whose names they still carry
<Cite ref="smithsonian-pizzafounder"/>. Scott Wiener, a pizza historian who
had repeated the 1905-license story on his own tours for years, has since
dropped it, saying the claim "is now totally blown out of the water"
<Cite ref="gothamist-pizzahistory"/>.

None of this erases Lombardi's part in the story — he ran a real, influential
pizzeria that his family reopened under his name decades later, and it is
still there. It only means the tidy founding-father version of the story, the
one printed on the wall of the shop itself, is far less certain than it looks.
The dough below owes more to that whole generation of anonymous immigrant
bakers, working from memory in a city that had none of Naples's ovens or
ingredients, than to any single person's legend.
