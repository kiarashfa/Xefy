The most repeated origin story for falafel holds that it was invented in Egypt
by Coptic Christians as a meatless dish for Lent, sometimes said to be a
thousand years old. It is a tidy story and a widely told one, but it rests on
essentially no documentary evidence — no source ties the dish to Coptic fasting
practice at any specific date, and the claim functions more as folklore than
as a traceable history <Cite ref="agbi-falafel"/>.

The textual record instead points somewhere much more recent: the word
falafel does not appear in Egyptian writing until after the British occupation
began in 1882, which has led some historians to a more prosaic theory — that
British officers stationed in Alexandria, having developed a taste for fried
vegetable croquettes while serving in India, had their Egyptian cooks adapt
the idea with local ingredients <Cite ref="agbi-falafel"/>. Whether or not that
specific account is right, the documentary silence before the late nineteenth
century argues against any claim of great antiquity for the dish under this
name.

What is not in dispute is that Egypt and the Levant make genuinely different
things under related names. Egyptian ta'ameya is built on dried, skinned
broad beans, herb-heavy and often coated in sesame seed before frying, while
the Levantine version this page treats as the default is built on dried
chickpeas alone <Cite ref="es-falafel"/>. The modern argument over falafel is
mostly not about which of these came first, but about national ownership of
the Levantine chickpea version specifically — it has been claimed at various
points as Lebanese, Palestinian and Israeli, a dispute bound up with questions
of national identity that this page has no basis to settle and does not
attempt to.
