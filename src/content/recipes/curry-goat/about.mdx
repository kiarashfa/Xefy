Curry goat's ingredients name its history directly: goat, a meat common across
the Caribbean, cooked in a curry powder that has no precolonial place there at
all. Between 1834 and 1917, nearly one and a half million Indians emigrated as
indentured labourers to British colonies after the abolition of slavery left
plantations short of workers, and some thirty-six thousand of them were
brought to Jamaica specifically <Cite ref="scroll-caribbean-curry"/>. They
carried their cooking with them, and curry (the technique and the blended
spice mix behind it) is the clearest survival of that migration in Jamaican
food today <Cite ref="juggernaut-curry-goat"/>.

What reached Jamaica was not simply transplanted. Most Indo-Caribbean
labourers came from northeastern and central India, and once in the
Caribbean they found few of the ingredients their cooking assumed were
available: no curry leaves, no familiar greens, no easy substitute for the
chillies grown at home. Scotch bonnet (blisteringly hot, thin-walled and
locally abundant) took the place of the chillies used on the subcontinent,
and coconut milk, a Caribbean staple in its own right, became the braising
liquid <Cite ref="scroll-caribbean-curry"/>. Goat itself is a departure too:
curry in most of the subcontinent is built on whatever meat, pulse or
vegetable is at hand rather than on goat specifically, and its centrality
here is a Caribbean choice as much as an Indian one.

Despite people of Indian descent making up only a small fraction of Jamaica's
population, curry goat has become one of the island's most widely eaten
dishes, served at parties and family gatherings alongside rice and peas
<Cite ref="scroll-caribbean-curry"/>. It is often named, informally, as
Jamaica's second dish after ackee and saltfish <Cite ref="juggernaut-curry-goat"/>,
and versions of it (under names including goat curry) are cooked across
Guyana, Trinidad and Tobago, Suriname and Belize, each shaped by the same
history of indenture and adaptation <Cite ref="juggernaut-curry-goat"/>.
