The doughnut reached America by way of Dutch settlers in New Amsterdam, who brought a yeasted, egg-rich fried dough they called *oliekoecken*, or oil
cakes, studded with dried fruit and dropped by the spoonful into hot fat <Cite ref="smithsonian-doughnut"/><Cite ref="toriavey-doughnut"/>. That dough
had no hole in it; the shape most associated with the doughnut today is a later, and far less settled, addition.

The story usually told credits a single sea captain. Elizabeth Gregory, mother of a New England ship's captain, is said to have fried a spiced dough
using spices from her son's cargo, sometimes packing nuts into the centre where the dough was slowest to cook through <Cite ref="smithsonian-doughnut"/>.
Her son, Hanson Gregory, later claimed the credit for the hole itself, telling the *Boston Post* that he had punched it out with the top of a round tin
pepper box so the centre would cook evenly <Cite ref="smithsonian-doughnut"/>. A competing version of his own story has him skewering an unfinished
doughnut onto a spoke of his ship's wheel during a storm, the hole appearing by accident rather than design <Cite ref="toriavey-doughnut"/>.

Neither version has held up especially well under scrutiny. Some historians studying the claim have suggested more mundane motives — economising on
ingredients, or easing digestion — behind a hole Gregory dressed up as an inspired accident, and ring-shaped fried pastries already appear in cookbooks
from the 1840s, before his claimed date, as well as in fried-dough traditions well outside America entirely <Cite ref="smithsonian-doughnut"/>. Tori
Avey's own account of the ship's-wheel version calls it "probably not true, but a fun story nonetheless" <Cite ref="toriavey-doughnut"/>, and both
pieces treat the captain as the traditional face of an invention that plainly happened by more diffuse means.
