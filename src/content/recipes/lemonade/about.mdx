Lemonade's earliest known ancestor is not the clear drink familiar today but a
sweetened citrus preparation called qatarmizat, made in Egypt once lemons
reached North Africa, itself following an earlier tenth-century drink called
kashkab made from barley, mint and citron leaf <Cite ref="atlasobscura-lemonade"/>.
For centuries the drink was treated as medicine as much as refreshment: the
twelfth-century Egyptian physician Ibn Jumay recommended it for sore throats,
hangovers and general weakness, and the idea that it could restore health
followed it into sixteenth-century Britain, where doctors sometimes prescribed
it to the sick <Cite ref="atlasobscura-lemonade"/>.

That medicinal reputation had a real basis. In 1795 the British Royal Navy
ordered a daily lemonade or lemon-juice ration for sailors as a defence against
scurvy, decades before vitamin C itself was identified as the reason it worked
<Cite ref="history-lemonade"/>. Around the same period, Parisian street vendors
were selling a version of the drink from tanks worn on their backs, organised
enough by the mid-1600s to form their own guild, the Compagnie de Limonadiers
<Cite ref="atlasobscura-lemonade"/>.

The drink was already established in America by the time of George
Washington's presidency, and the carbonated version familiar from soda
fountains did not appear until the technology to carbonate water reliably
arrived in the nineteenth century <Cite ref="history-lemonade"/>. The plain,
still version made from lemon juice, sugar and water — the one treated here —
is in fact the older of the two.
