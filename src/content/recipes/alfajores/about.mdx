The alfajor's name and its first form both come from al-Andalus, the Muslim
territories of medieval Spain. The philologist Manuel Alvar López traced the
word to Andalusian Arabic, and its first recorded appearance in a Spanish
dictionary is as *alfaxor* in the grammarian Antonio de Nebrija's
Latin-Spanish dictionary, usually dated to around 1495
<Cite ref="wiki-alfajor"/><Cite ref="conversation-alfajor"/>. The word's
documented meaning shifted over the centuries that followed: Spain's Royal
Academy defined it in its *Diccionario de autoridades* of 1726–39 as a paste
of almonds, nuts, toasted breadcrumbs and fine spices bound with honey,
closer to a dense nougat than to the biscuit sold under the name in
Argentina today <Cite ref="conversation-alfajor"/>.

Spanish colonists carried the sweet to South America, where it took root in
the Río de la Plata region and was reshaped over generations by cooks of
European, Indigenous and African descent into something distinct from its
Iberian original <Cite ref="gac-alfajor"/>. Bakeries built around it in the
nineteenth century (among them Casa Chammas in Córdoba, founded in 1869)
turned it from a home confection into a commercial one, and the industry it
founded expanded through the twentieth century with mass-market producers
including the Havanna brand, launched in 1947 <Cite ref="gac-alfajor"/>.

The cornflour version eaten today (a pale, crumbly round bound with maicena
rather than wheat flour, filled with dulce de leche and finished with
coconut pressed around the rim) is a later, specifically Argentine
refinement rather than a form that travelled directly from Spain. It is also
only one of several regional doughs the country still makes: Argentina's own
tourism authority lists corn flour as the defining dough of Catamarca's
alfajor de capia and ground walnuts as a Cuyo variant, alongside fillings
that range from yerba mate in the Litoral to Malbec-spiked dulce de leche in
Mendoza <Cite ref="gobar-alfajor"/>. Exactly when the cornflour dough itself
was first adopted is not established in any of these sources, and no
reliable date for that specific innovation could be found; what the record
supports is the broader shift from Andalusian sweet to Argentine bakery
staple, made distinct region by region, rather than the precise recipe that
made any one version standard.
