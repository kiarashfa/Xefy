The sandwich depends on two separate inventions of the 1920s arriving at once. Otto
Rohwedder's automatic bread slicer made pre-sliced loaves a household staple, and
James L. Kraft's patented processed cheese, developed a few years earlier, melted
smoothly under heat instead of turning greasy and breaking the way an aged cheese
does <Cite ref="mentalfloss-americancheese"/>. Without both, the sandwich as it
now exists could not easily be made at home.

Its earliest form was not a sandwich at all in the modern sense: a single slice
of bread topped with melted cheese and eaten open-faced, a cheap and filling dish
that spread widely during the Great Depression <Cite ref="howstuffworks-grilledcheese"/>.
A second slice was added on top later, closing it into what is now recognised as
a grilled cheese, and the name itself is a relatively late arrival — the dish was
called a toasted or melted cheese sandwich for decades before "grilled cheese"
became the common term <Cite ref="howstuffworks-grilledcheese"/>.

Processed American cheese remains the diner standard for a practical reason
rather than a nostalgic one: it is formulated to melt evenly at a moderate heat
without separating, which is exactly the behaviour a pan-fried sandwich needs and
a traditional cheddar cannot reliably supply <Cite ref="mentalfloss-americancheese"/>.
The method below follows that logic — a slow, low heat that gives the cheese time
to melt through before the crust has finished browning.
