Panna cotta is cream, milk and sugar warmed just enough to dissolve gelatine, poured
into moulds and set cold. Its name means "cooked cream". The Italian dessert is
presumed to come from the Langhe, in Piedmont, and Piedmont is the region most often
given as its home <Cite ref="dissapore-panna-cotta"/> <Cite ref="wiki-panna-cotta"/>.

The Piedmont origin is a presumption rather than a documented fact
<Cite ref="wiki-panna-cotta"/> <Cite ref="dissapore-panna-cotta"/>. The best-known
story credits a woman of Hungarian origin, who is not named, with inventing it in the
Langhe. Wikipedia calls this an unverified story and dates it to the early nineteenth
century, while Dissapore dates it to the early twentieth
<Cite ref="wiki-panna-cotta"/> <Cite ref="dissapore-panna-cotta"/>.

A second account credits the chef Ettore Songia with putting the dessert on the menu
around the 1960s <Cite ref="dissapore-panna-cotta"/>. Neither account is documented,
and the dessert's history in Italy is a little over a century old, so the question of
who first made it is open.

The setting agent is the other point of dispute. Older Italian recipes use colla di
pesce, fish glue, which Dissapore takes to be animal gelatine, and which is more
probably ordinary gelatine than isinglass <Cite ref="dissapore-panna-cotta"/>
<Cite ref="wiki-panna-cotta"/>. Agar, a plant-based setting agent, gives a firmer and
less elastic set <Cite ref="dissapore-panna-cotta"/>.

Similar cream desserts appear in France, Greece and Denmark, some of them in the
Middle Ages <Cite ref="dissapore-panna-cotta"/>.
