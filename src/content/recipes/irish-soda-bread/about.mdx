Irish soda bread is a quick bread. It is raised by bicarbonate of soda rather than yeast, and in its usual form the soda reacts with the acid in buttermilk to make the dough rise <Cite ref="long-soda"/>. Nothing has to prove, so the dough is shaped and baked as soon as it is mixed.

The bread is firmly associated with Ireland, but that association has a history of its own. Lucy Long describes the process by which soda bread became a heritage food as neither uniform nor linear, and not embraced by all members of the culture it belongs to <Cite ref="long-soda"/>. The bread takes different forms in Northern Ireland, the Republic of Ireland and the United States, and each form reflects a different idea of what Irish heritage is <Cite ref="culinary-chicago-soda"/>.

Its method is as plain as its name suggests. Plain flour, buttermilk, bicarbonate of soda and salt are enough, and the dough is neither kneaded nor enriched with fat. Where a yeasted loaf needs hours of rising, the soda starts working as soon as the liquid goes in, so the loaf is in the oven within minutes of the flour being mixed. That speed is the defining difference between soda bread and the yeasted breads it is usually compared with.
