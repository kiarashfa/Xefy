Apfelstrudel's defining technique (a single sheet of dough pulled thin
enough to read through, rather than dozens of individually layered leaves)
is a Viennese refinement of a pastry that began further east. Strudel's
ancestor is generally traced to Hungarian *rétes*, itself understood to have
developed from Ottoman baklava during the period of Turkish rule in Hungary,
as the technique of building a pastry from very thin dough moved west with
Ottoman expansion into Europe in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries
<Cite ref="wiki-strudel"/><Cite ref="gng-strudel"/>.

The earliest strudel recipe anyone has produced in writing is Austrian and
specific: a handwritten cookbook held at the Wienbibliothek im Rathaus, the
Vienna city library, dated to 1696, contains a recipe for Millirahmstrudel (a
milk-cream strudel) alongside a savoury turnip version, making no mention
yet of apples <Cite ref="wiki-strudel"/><Cite ref="gng-strudel"/>. The rolled form that now defines
the pastry, one continuous stretched sheet wrapped around its filling rather
than the many separate leaves of baklava or rétes, is understood to have
taken shape in the Austrian court kitchens around the turn of the eighteenth
century <Cite ref="wiki-strudel"/><Cite ref="gng-strudel"/>. From there, strudel's popularity spread
widely through the Habsburg and later Austro-Hungarian territories, with
regional versions multiplying through the nineteenth century and into the
Austro-Hungarian Empire period as pastry chefs across Austria and Hungary
adapted the technique to different fillings <Cite ref="gng-strudel"/>.

Apple, now the strudel most people picture, is one variation among many that
grew out of that single technique; its distinction was never the filling
but the dough, stretched by hand until a Habsburg-era baker's palm could show
through it, which is still the part no shortcut fully replaces.
