Ropa vieja carries a folk legend rather than a documented inventor: a poor
man, unable to afford meat for his family, is said to have shredded his own
clothes into a pot and prayed over it, and the story goes that his prayer
turned the cloth into meat and vegetables enough to feed everyone at the
table <Cite ref="travelnoire-ropavieja"/> <Cite ref="latinpost-ropavieja"/>.
The tale explains the name rather than the technique: what the meat looks
like, pulled into fine, ragged strands, resembles a pile of old clothes far
more literally than it resembles any miracle.

The traceable history runs through Spain rather than through the legend.
Ropa vieja's roots are usually placed with the Sephardic Jewish communities
of the Iberian Peninsula, whose religious prohibition on cooking during the
Sabbath meant a stew had to be started the night before and left to cook
itself through; the technique found particular favour in the Canary Islands,
where it was popularised before Canarian emigration carried it across the
Atlantic <Cite ref="travelnoire-ropavieja"/>. The dish is first recorded as
having appeared in Cuba in 1857 <Cite ref="latinpost-ropavieja"/>, after which
it took on the sofrito of peppers, onion and tomato that distinguishes the
Cuban version from its Spanish and Canarian relatives.

The dish's place in Cuba today is more complicated than its status as a
national favourite suggests. A 1963 law restricting the sale and slaughter of
cattle, introduced to protect the country's herd, has instead made beef
scarce and tightly controlled for decades, and cattle numbers fell by around
thirty percent by 2015 <Cite ref="latinpost-ropavieja"/>. What tourists and
the Cuban diaspora eat as ropa vieja is, as a result, a considerably more
everyday meal outside Cuba than it now is for most Cubans on the island
<Cite ref="latinpost-ropavieja"/>.
