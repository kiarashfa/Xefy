A cannolo is a tube of fried pastry, filled with sweetened ricotta at the last
moment so that the shell stays crisp. Its origin is not known. The Gazzetta del Gusto
describes the origins of the dessert as still unknown, and it presents an earlier
banana-shaped sweet of ricotta, honey and almonds as a possible ancestor
<Cite ref="gazzetta-cannoli"/>.

The best-known story places the cannolo in the Arab period of Sicily, which the
Gazzetta dates to 827 to 1091, and at Caltanissetta, which some food historians name
as its birthplace <Cite ref="wiki-cannoli"/> <Cite ref="gazzetta-cannoli"/>. In the
version told there, the women of the emirs' households, the harem, made the pastry
as a tribute to the ruler. The Gazzetta labels this a legend <Cite ref="gazzetta-cannoli"/>.

A second legend, also labelled as such, has the nuns of a Caltanissetta convent
making the pastry for Carnival <Cite ref="gazzetta-cannoli"/>. Wikipedia gives the
Arab-period version as women in princes' households inventing the pastry to win
favour <Cite ref="wiki-cannoli"/> <Cite ref="gazzetta-cannoli"/>.

The name is traced by the Gazzetta to the river reeds on which the dough was rolled
before frying <Cite ref="gazzetta-cannoli"/>. The traditional shell is made with lard,
Marsala, orange peel and cinnamon, and the Gazzetta specifies a Sicilian sheep's-milk
ricotta for the filling, which it says has a stronger flavour than cow's-milk ricotta
<Cite ref="gazzetta-cannoli"/>.
