Kansas City barbecue is generally dated to Henry Perry, a pitmaster from
Tennessee who arrived in the city around 1907 and sold slow-smoked meat
wrapped in newspaper from a streetcar barn <Cite ref="tasting-table-kc"/>. His
sauce is remembered as harsh and peppery, closer to a vinegar dressing than to
the thick, sweet condiment now sold under the city's name
<Cite ref="pendergast-perry-sauce"/>. After Perry's death in 1940, the business
passed to his employee Charlie Bryant, who later sold it to his brother
Arthur, who built the restaurant now known as Arthur Bryant's
<Cite ref="pendergast-perry-sauce"/>.

Exactly who is responsible for the sweeter, tomato-and-molasses sauce now
treated as the Kansas City standard is not fully agreed. One account credits
Arthur Bryant himself with adjusting Perry's recipe and adding its
characteristic sweetness <Cite ref="tasting-table-kc"/>. Another places the
turning point decades later and outside the Bryant lineage entirely: Rich
Davis, a child psychiatrist, began selling KC Masterpiece to grocers in 1977
with a noticeably thicker, more molasses-forward sauce than the traditional
Kansas City style, and that version is widely credited with setting the
template most supermarket barbecue sauce has followed since
<Cite ref="kcur-rich-davis-sauce"/>.

Either way, the sauce eaten today — tomato base, brown sugar or molasses for
sweetness, vinegar for cut, black pepper for heat — represents a later, sweeter
turn on a tradition that started out considerably sharper. The version made
here follows that now-standard profile rather than Perry's original.
