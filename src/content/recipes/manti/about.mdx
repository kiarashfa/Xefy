The dominant account of mantı's history puts its origin well outside Turkey.
Most food historians agree that the dish travelled across Central Asia along
the Silk Road, carried west by Turkic and Mongol peoples before it ever
reached Anatolia <Cite ref="wiki-manti"/>. Under that account, the small
boiled or steamed dumpling belongs to a much wider family that also produced
Chinese jiaozi and mantou, rather than being a dish Anatolia invented on its
own. Not every historian accepts the direction of travel, though: a minority
view holds the sequence may run the other way, with a Middle Eastern dumpling
spreading eastward toward China and Korea instead <Cite ref="wiki-manti"/>.
Neither claim rests on the kind of single decisive document that would settle
the question, and both remain live positions rather than a resolved debate.

Whichever way the dish first moved, its documented presence in Ottoman
kitchens is much firmer. A fifteenth-century cookbook by Muhammed bin Mahmud
Şirvani records a version of it: a steamed dumpling filled with minced lamb
and crushed chickpeas, seasoned with cinnamon and finished with vinegar
<Cite ref="wiki-manti"/> — recognisably the same idea as today's dish, though
built differently in almost every particular of filling and sauce.

What is not in dispute is that Kayseri, in central Anatolia, has become the
form's most famous address. Kayseri mantısı is distinguished chiefly by scale:
folded small enough that dozens fit a single spoon, then served under yoghurt
and butter melted with mint or Aleppo pepper, with more of the same dried
herb and pepper scattered over the top <Cite ref="wiki-manti"/>. The size is
treated locally as a matter of skill as much as taste — a smaller mantı is
read as a more accomplished one — and it is this exacting, miniature style
that most people outside Turkey now mean when they say the name. The standard
is stated as a proverb rather than a measurement: the best mantı is the kind
small enough that forty of them fit on one spoon <Cite ref="goturkiye-manti"/>.
Kayseri mantısı now carries an official geographical indication, and the
transmission account it rests on — dough parcels of meat carried west along the
trade routes and remade with local ingredients — is the same one the Turkish
tourism authority sets out <Cite ref="goturkiye-manti"/>.
