The standard story is a clean piece of mestizo cooking: a corn dough, made
the way the indigenous tamale-like *humita* is made, laid over the kind of
minced-meat filling used in a Spanish empanada. The anthropologist Sonia
Montecino has proposed that the combination was worked out during the
colonial period by Mapuche cooks put to work in the kitchens of Spanish
colonists, fusing the two traditions they were made to straddle
<Cite ref="emol-pastel"/>.

The documentary record complicates that story rather than confirming it. A
dish answering to the same description appears in the Peruvian writer
Ricardo Palma's nineteenth-century *tradición* "Agustinos y franciscanos,"
which sets a version of it at a colonial-era banquet in Peru, and a
Peruvian recipe for the dish is separately on record from the early
twentieth century <Cite ref="infobae-pastel"/>. The earliest known written
description specifically in Chile does not appear until 1862, in the
naturalist Claudio Gay's *Historia física y política de Chile*, which
describes a filling of minced lamb, sometimes mixed with chicken, under
corn seasoned with sugar, fat and chilli
<Cite ref="wiki-pastel"/><Cite ref="bnd-gay"/>.

Even the dish's name sits awkwardly with the Mapuche-origin story.
*Choclo*, the word for corn cob used across Chile and much of the Andes,
comes from Quechua *choccllo* <Cite ref="rae-choclo"/> — the language of
the Inca rather than of the Mapuche. Taken together, the evidence points
less to a single Mapuche kitchen than to a dish with roots spread across
the wider Andean world, reaching its familiar Chilean form only later.

None of which changes what it has become: a dish now claimed unambiguously
as Chilean, eaten in high summer when corn is at its sweetest, and cooked
in kitchens across the country as a byword for home cooking.
