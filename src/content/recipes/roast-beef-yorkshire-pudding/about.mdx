Roast beef's status as a marker of English identity is old enough to have
become a joke at England's expense: the French nickname for the English,
*les rosbifs*, is taken directly from the dish, a mark of the Englishman in
French eyes memorably captured in satirist James Gillray's imagery of John
Bull tucking into his beef <Cite ref="rhc-rosbif"/>. Long before Yorkshire
pudding entered the picture, a joint of roast beef was already doing
cultural work well beyond the dinner table.

The pudding's origin is more practical than patriotic. The earliest named
recipe appears in 1747, when Hannah Glasse's *The Art of Cookery Made Plain
and Easy* gave the Yorkshire name to a batter previously cooked simply as
"dripping pudding" — set beneath a joint turning on a spit so the fat and
juices falling from the meat would flavour and cook it as it roasted
<Cite ref="hukyp"/>. Its original place at the table was not beside the beef
but before it: served first, with thick gravy, specifically to blunt
appetites and make an expensive cut of meat go further, a purpose recorded in
the regional saying "them 'at eats t'most pudding gets t'most meat"
<Cite ref="hukyp"/>.

The two dishes only became a single, simultaneous course later, as
Yorkshire pudding moved from a starter that stretched the meat to a side
dish that shares the plate with it. What survives from the original
arrangement is the method rather than the order of service: a pudding still
made from nothing more than flour, egg and milk, and still raised by nothing
but the shock of meeting fat hot enough to have come straight from
underneath a roasting joint.
