Red-braised pork — meat coloured a deep reddish-brown by caramelised sugar and
soy sauce rather than by any chilli — is cooked all over China under
different regional names and small variations in technique. The Hunanese
version is the one most widely known today, largely because of a single,
well-attested association: Mao Zedong, a Hunan native, is said to have loved
it enough to request it regularly from his personal chefs once he was living
in Beijing <Cite ref="wiki-hongshaorou"/> <Cite ref="nguyen-hongshaorou"/>.

Around that documented fondness sits a softer kind of local legend: villagers in
Shaoshan, Mao's birthplace, are said to have credited the dish with keeping
the mind sharp as well as the body fed, a claim that belongs to folklore
rather than to any medical record and is best read as regional pride rather
than fact <Cite ref="nguyen-hongshaorou"/>.

The technique itself needs no origin story to justify it. Sugar melted to a
dark caramel before the meat goes in, rather than added later as sweetening,
is what gives the pork its lacquered colour, and a long, barely-simmering
braise is what turns belly fat, which would otherwise render out chewy and
tough, into something soft enough to yield under a chopstick.
