Rice and beans arrived in Brazil from opposite directions. Beans were
already cultivated by Indigenous peoples across the Americas before
Portuguese colonisation, while rice came with the colonists themselves.
What made the pairing a national habit rather than a coincidence was price:
from the seventeenth into the eighteenth century, beans cost roughly a sixth
of what rice did in São Paulo, and their low cost and long storage life made
them standard provisions for the tropeiros — mule and cattle drivers who
carried goods between Minas Gerais and São Paulo from the seventeenth
century onward — and for expeditions travelling into Brazil's interior more
generally <Cite ref="wiki-feijaotropeiro"/><Cite ref="michelin-tropeiro"/>.
Rice and beans, cooked together or
alongside each other, became the food that stretched furthest for the least
money — a habit that outlasted the economic conditions that first produced
it.

That habit has since become close to universal. A Brazilian restaurant
industry estimate puts the number of rice-and-beans meals served daily in
the country's bars, restaurants and lunch counters above eighty million,
across every region and social class <Cite ref="itatiaia-arrozfeijao"/>.
The pairing is also nutritionally complementary in a way that predates any
awareness of amino acids: rice supplies methionine and beans supply lysine,
and together they form a more complete protein than either provides alone
<Cite ref="itatiaia-arrozfeijao"/>. The combination is so thoroughly
ordinary that the phrase "arroz com feijão" functions in everyday Brazilian
Portuguese as an idiom for whatever is plain, standard or unremarkable — the
linguistic equivalent of calling something bread and butter.

Regional versions branch off from this shared base: feijão tropeiro adds
cassava flour, egg and bacon to the beans in Minas Gerais, baião de dois
cooks rice and beans together with cheese in the Northeast, and arroz
carreteiro folds dried beef through the rice itself in the South. The
everyday version — a pot of beans
beside a pan of plain sautéed rice — remains the one eaten most often,
precisely because it asks the least of a cook and a kitchen.
