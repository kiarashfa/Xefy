Moqueca's name is generally traced to *pokeka*, a Tupi word the folklorist
Luís da Câmara Cascudo recorded for fish the Indigenous peoples of coastal
Brazil wrapped in leaves and cooked over hot coals or embers: a cooking
method rather than a fixed recipe, and regional newspaper coverage of the
dish's modern rivalry traces the same divergence to how differently that
method met local ingredients as it spread along the coast
<Cite ref="wiki-moqueca"/><Cite ref="em-moqueca"/>. What reached the stewpot
form eaten today is a layering of three culinary traditions: Indigenous
technique, a Portuguese habit of building stews around fish and aromatics,
and ingredients (dendê palm oil and coconut milk chief among them)
generally attributed to the transatlantic slave trade and credited with
giving Bahia's version its stronger Afro-Brazilian imprint
<Cite ref="wiki-moqueca"/><Cite ref="em-moqueca"/>.

Those African ingredients are also exactly what split the dish into two
rival versions, one from Bahia and one from Espírito Santo, and neither
state concedes the other's claim to be the original. Moqueca baiana keeps
the dendê oil, coconut milk and bell pepper that carry the strongest African
imprint, producing a richer, more intensely coloured stew. Moqueca capixaba
leaves all three out, colouring its sauce with annatto instead of palm oil
and building its richness from olive oil alone, and is generally read as
sitting closer to the dish's Indigenous and Portuguese roots
<Cite ref="em-moqueca"/>. The capixaba version is also inseparable from its
cookware: it is traditionally finished and served in a clay pot hand-made by
artisans in Goiabeiras, a Vitória neighbourhood whose potters were formally
registered in 2002 as bearers of Brazilian intangible cultural heritage,
with their pans named specifically for their use in moqueca capixaba
<Cite ref="wiki-moqueca"/><Cite ref="iphan-paneleiras"/>.

Neither regional claim is likely to be settled, and the dispute is treated
in Brazil less as a historical question than as a live rivalry, the kind of
argument that gets rehearsed at every table where both versions might be on
offer <Cite ref="em-moqueca"/>. The fairer reading of the record is that
moqueca was never one dish to begin with: it is a cooking method that met
different ingredients in different places and produced two legitimate,
independently evolved results.
