Moo ping's rise as a Thai street food is tied to a specific piece of urban
infrastructure rather than a kitchen. Sources place it in the early 1950s, when
carts used to transport food were redesigned into mobile vendor stalls, making it
easy for sellers to carry a charcoal grill through the city and serve pork skewers
where people already were <Cite ref="wiki-muping"/><Cite ref="thaifoodie-muping"/>.
That shift, rather than any single recipe, is what turned grilled pork into an
everyday breakfast and snack sold on Bangkok's streets from early morning.

The marinade itself now exists in two recognised styles. *Mu ping boran*, the older
style, is built on a soy-based marinade; *mu ping nom sod* — literally "fresh milk"
— replaces it with milk to tenderise the meat and is now the more commonly sold
version <Cite ref="wiki-muping"/>. Which milk is meant is not fixed: dairy milk is
the version the name literally describes, but coconut milk is a documented
variation used for the same tenderising, creamier effect, and it is coconut milk
rather than dairy that most versions cooked outside Thailand are built around
<Cite ref="wiki-muping"/>. Both styles share the same base of coriander root, garlic
and black pepper worked into the marinade before the pork goes in.
