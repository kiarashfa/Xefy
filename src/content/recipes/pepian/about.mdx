Pepián's base — a sauce of toasted chillies and seeds, thickened without
any dairy or flour — is old enough that its origin sits with the Maya
rather than with Spanish colonial cooking. Both the dish and the toasting
technique behind it are traced to the Kaqchikel Maya of Guatemala's western
highlands, where a version of the sauce is believed to have served a
ceremonial role in pre-Columbian religious and community life
<Cite ref="upavim-pepian"/> <Cite ref="latinpost-pepian"/>.

What Spanish colonisation added was mostly around the edges of an already
established dish: garlic, onion and meat joined a base that had originally
relied on indigenous chillies, tomatoes and toasted seeds
<Cite ref="upavim-pepian"/>. The core technique — toasting the seeds and
chillies before grinding and simmering them into a sauce — survived that
layering more or less intact, which is part of why pepián is described as
one of the oldest dishes still cooked in Guatemala in something like its
original form <Cite ref="latinpost-pepian"/>.

The dish's status is now a matter of official record rather than
reputation alone. In 2007, the Guatemalan government designated pepián the
country's national dish and recognised it as part of the nation's
intangible cultural heritage <Cite ref="upavim-pepian"/>
<Cite ref="latinpost-pepian"/>. That declaration formalised what was
already true in practice: a dish that had been carried, largely unbroken,
from a pre-Columbian kitchen to a modern one.
