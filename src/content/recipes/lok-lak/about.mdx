Before it was Cambodian, lok lak was Vietnamese. *Bò lúc lắc* — cubes of
beef seared hard and fast in a hot wok — is said to have developed on the
sidewalk food stalls of Saigon, where French ingredients and technique met
local, chopstick-friendly habits: whole steaks cut down into bite-sized
pieces rather than served intact. Beef itself was the unusual part of that
combination: before French colonisation, cattle in Vietnam were kept chiefly
as draft animals to plough rice fields rather than as food, and it was
French colonial cattle slaughter that made beef a regular ingredient in
Vietnamese street cooking for the first time
<Cite ref="wiki-loclac"/><Cite ref="splendidtable-pho"/>.

The name has at least three competing explanations rather than one settled
one: that the diced cubes resemble Vietnamese dice, called *hột lúc lắc*;
that *lúc lắc* simply means "shaken," describing the constant tossing
needed to sear every side of the meat — the explanation America's Test
Kitchen gives for the name — or that it describes the cubes themselves
shaking as they are thrown in the pan. None of the three has displaced the
others <Cite ref="wiki-loclac"/><Cite ref="atk-shakingbeef"/>.

Cambodia's version is a loanword as much as a loan-dish — *lok lak* or
*loc lac* borrows the Vietnamese name directly, arriving during the era of
French Indochina, which brought Cambodia under French colonial
administration alongside Vietnam and Laos after France established a
protectorate over Cambodia in 1863 and folded it into the wider Indochinese
union in 1887 <Cite ref="wiki-loclac"/><Cite ref="lumen-indochina"/>.
Cambodia's earliest version is said to have used good steak seared in
French butter; a cheaper, Chinese-influenced variant using strips of beef
and oyster sauce developed later and is closer to what is most commonly
eaten today. Food writers caution against reducing Khmer cuisine as a
whole to a simple sum of Indian, Chinese and Vietnamese borrowings, even as
they acknowledge each left a real mark on it — Indian spices arriving with
the Indianised Funan kingdom, Chinese noodle dishes from a Chinese
presence going back centuries, Vietnamese exchange running in both
directions along the shared Mekong delta
<Cite ref="usaseanypa-khmer"/>. What has stayed constant through both the
Vietnamese original and its Cambodian adaptation is the format itself:
seared beef over lettuce and tomato, a plate built more on French bistro
logic — a protein, a salad, a sharp sauce — than on any older regional
model.
