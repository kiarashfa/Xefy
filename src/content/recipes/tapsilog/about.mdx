The name is built the same way most *silog* dishes are: a shortened meat
name, plus *sinangag* for garlic fried rice, plus *itlog* for egg, spliced
into one word, *tap-si-log* from *tapa*, cured beef, being the original and
still the most common of the family
<Cite ref="wiki-silog"/> <Cite ref="esquire-tapsilog"/>.

Unusually for a dish this common, its coining is attached to one named
person rather than lost to anonymous street food. Vivian del Rosario, who
opened a small stall called Tapsi ni Vivian on a corner lot in Quezon City in
1986, says she was the first to shorten "tapa, sinangag, itlog" into
*tapsilog* <Cite ref="esquire-tapsilog"/> <Cite ref="wiki-silog"/>. Before
that, the same combination (beef tapa served with garlic rice, without a
fixed name for the egg) went by *tapsi*, and the stalls that served it were
called *tapsihan*.

Whether or not del Rosario's stall was truly first, the name spread fast and
the format faster: once "tapsilog" existed as a template, cooks began
building the same three-part shorthand around other proteins, producing
*hotsilog* with a hot dog, *hamsilog* with ham, and *longsilog* with sweet
cured sausage, all sharing the same base of garlic rice and fried egg
<Cite ref="wiki-silog"/> <Cite ref="willflyforfood-silog"/>. What began as
inexpensive, quick breakfast or
late-night food is now sold everywhere from roadside carinderias to
shopping-mall food courts.
