The Caesar salad is neither Italian nor American. It was made in Tijuana, at a
restaurant opened by the Italian immigrant Caesar Cardini to feed visitors from
across the border, and the border is the whole of the explanation: Prohibition
had made a Mexican dining room the nearest place an American could order a drink
with dinner <Cite ref="sheen-mx"/>.

The story attached to it is precise in the way that ought to invite suspicion.
On the fourth of July 1924, during an unexpected rush, the kitchen ran short and
Cardini improvised from what was left — whole romaine leaves, garlic oil,
Worcestershire sauce, egg, Parmesan — dressing them at the table
<Cite ref="salon-2024"/>. His daughter Rosa is the source of that date
<Cite ref="natgeo-2025"/>. She is not the only claimant: Cardini's brother Alex,
a First World War pilot, is credited by some accounts with a version served to
airmen, and a cook named Livio Santini maintained the recipe had been his
mother's <Cite ref="salon-2024"/>. The more interesting response to the argument
is that having it at all misses the point — the dish crossed into American
restaurant culture so completely that its Mexican birth dropped out of the
telling <Cite ref="sheen-mx"/>.

Two corrections travel with the story. The first concerns anchovy, which is now
taken to define the dressing: Cardini is said to have kept whole fillets out on
the grounds that the Worcestershire already carried enough of them
<Cite ref="salon-2024"/>, and the fillets are usually credited to his brother's
version <Cite ref="natgeo-2025"/>. The second is quieter. The original called
for limón, which in Mexico means lime, and which crossed the border as lemon
<Cite ref="natgeo-2025"/>.

The salad travelled regardless of who made it first. By 1953 the International
Society of Epicures in Paris was calling it the finest recipe to have come out
of the Americas in fifty years <Cite ref="natgeo-2025"/>.
