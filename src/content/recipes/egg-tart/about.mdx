The Cantonese egg tart is a hybrid from the start. It developed in
Guangzhou in the early twentieth century, when the city's status as
China's one treaty port open to European traders put its bakers in direct
contact with British custard tarts and, by way of neighbouring Macau,
Portuguese egg pastries — department-store pastry chefs are credited with
combining the two into a Cantonese version by the 1920s
<Cite ref="wiki-eggtart"/> <Cite ref="montecristo-eggtart"/>. The tart
reached Hong Kong after the Second World War, carried by Cantonese families
who settled there, at first as a treat served in Western-style restaurants
before spreading into dim sum service and, later, into cafés serving
ordinary diners <Cite ref="montecristo-eggtart"/> <Cite ref="wiki-eggtart"/>.

That older, shortcrust or laminated-pastry dan tat is regularly confused with
a second, unrelated tart: the caramelised, blistered-top "Portuguese egg
tart" now sold across Macau and much of Asia. That version is not, in fact,
an older import that Hong Kong's tart derived from — it was invented in
Macau in 1989, more than sixty years after the Cantonese original, by
Andrew Stow, an English pharmacist who built his own recipe around a family
custard rather than any historical Portuguese formula, and Macau's community
coined a separate name, "Portuguese egg tart," specifically to mark it as
different from the dan tat already established there
<Cite ref="taipeitimes-eggtart"/>. The two tarts now sit side by side in
Cantonese bakeries, similar enough in shape to be mistaken for variations on
one dish and different enough in origin, pastry and filling to really be two.
