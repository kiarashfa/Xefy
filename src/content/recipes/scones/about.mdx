The scone is Scottish, and older than the sweet, oven-baked version now sold
as a symbol of English afternoon tea. The word itself is documented from at
least the early sixteenth century <Cite ref="bfh-scones"/>.

For most of that history the scone was not what is baked today. Early
Scottish versions were cooked on a girdle or bakestone rather than in an
oven, made from oats, barley or peasemeal, and leavened (before chemical
raising agents were widely available in the later eighteenth century) with
sourdough <Cite ref="bfh-scones"/>. The dish had not yet reached England in
any form Isabella Beeton recognised: her exhaustive *Book of Household
Management* contains no scone recipe at all, and they only begin appearing
in English cookbooks from the 1880s and 1890s <Cite ref="bfh-scones"/>. The
now-familiar oven-baked, wheat-flour scone, risen with baking powder that
reached domestic kitchens from the 1840s, displaced the older griddle
versions through the early twentieth century
<Cite ref="wiki-scone"/><Cite ref="bfh-scones"/>.

Its role in the cream tea is later still. The earliest printed reference
anyone has traced to "cream tea" in its modern sense is a 1932 article in
the Cornish newspaper *The Cornishman*, and by that point, on the same
account, Devon had already turned to scones ahead of Cornwall, which kept
faith with its own soft, split rolls for longer, an adoption gap put down to
Cornwall's relative geographic isolation <Cite ref="bfh-creamtea"/>. Devon
and Cornwall still disagree about the order of assembly, cream first or jam
first, but neither claim is the older tradition: the scone predates the
cream tea by centuries, and arrived in the West Country from Scotland by
way of England's ordinary kitchens, not the other way round.
