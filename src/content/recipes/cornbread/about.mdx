Cornbread begins with Indigenous American agriculture, not with any settler
kitchen. Native communities were grinding corn into meal and baking it into
simple breads long before Europeans arrived, and it was this technique — corn
dough wrapped in leaves or dropped onto a hot stone or ash bed — that colonists
adapted once maize became their staple grain in the absence of familiar wheat
<Cite ref="usc-cornbread"/>. Enslaved cooks, working with the same ground corn
and few other ingredients, developed closely related breads known as hoecakes,
ashcakes and corn pone, using methods that trace directly back to Indigenous
practice <Cite ref="usc-cornbread"/>.

The cast-iron skillet version most associated with the Deep South today is
unsweetened, coarse-textured and savoury, baked hot enough in a well-seasoned
pan to form a crisp crust <Cite ref="aptv-cornbread"/>. A sweeter, cakier
cornbread — closer to a quick bread than a savoury side — developed separately
further north, where wheat flour was more available and was blended into the
batter alongside more sugar. The two are still argued over as a matter of
regional identity rather than treated as interchangeable recipes
<Cite ref="aptv-cornbread"/>.

Buttermilk, eggs and chemical leavening were folded into the batter only once
they became reliably available, turning what had been a plain corn-and-water
bread into the two lighter, richer versions eaten now <Cite ref="usc-cornbread"/>.
