Guayaquil's own historian gives encebollado a specific birthplace: not a
kitchen but a dock. Jenny Estrada traces the dish to the working riverside
culture of the Guayas, where fish hauled in from the sea met cassava dug
from the land, cooked together into a hot, substantial meal for men doing
physically demanding dockside work
<Cite ref="nanmagazine-encebollado"/>. On this account the soup was
functional before it was beloved — food built to refuel a working body,
not a delicacy designed for a table.

What began as dockworkers' food later became the city's own, and by one
account it was only in the mid-1980s that encebollado moved decisively
from riverside kitchens onto Guayaquil's streets, sold by vendors rather
than cooked mainly at home <Cite ref="nanmagazine-encebollado"/>. That move
from private kitchen to public street stall is common to a number of dishes
on this site, and it marks the point at which a local, working-class food
becomes a dish visitors are told to seek out.

The dish that resulted, and that Ecuador now treats as its national one
<Cite ref="wikipedia-encebollado"/> <Cite ref="nanmagazine-encebollado"/>,
is built from three simple elements — fish, cassava and a lime-pickled
onion — a combination plain enough that its dockside, working-food
origins are still legible in the finished bowl.

Today encebollado is eaten across Ecuador at any hour, though it keeps a
reputation, only half joking, as the country's standard remedy for a heavy
night before.
