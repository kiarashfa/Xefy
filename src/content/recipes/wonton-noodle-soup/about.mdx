The word *wonton* is far older than the noodle dish built around it. It
appears as *húntún* in Chinese sources reaching back to the Tang dynasty, long
before the specific combination of dumpling, egg noodle and broth that now
carries the name existed as a single dish <Cite ref="wiki-wontonnoodles"/>.
That combination is a Guangzhou creation, formalised during the Qing dynasty
(1644–1911), when it developed as a noodle-house dish in the city rather than
as a home-cooked one <Cite ref="scmp-wontonnoodles"/>.

Its move beyond Guangdong is comparatively well documented, and comes down to
one named figure. Mak Woon-chi, credited in Hong Kong as the wonton noodle
tradition's own legend, brought the Guangzhou style south and opened what
became Mak's Noodle — and despite how completely the dish is now identified
with Hong Kong, it had only been available there since the early 1940s
<Cite ref="scmp-wontonnoodles"/>. The dish today is Cantonese in origin by
every source consulted here, though it has since travelled on: versions
served in Singapore and Malaysia depart from the Hong Kong bowl in their
noodles, their broth and their garnishes, while keeping the same
wonton-and-noodle structure at the centre <Cite ref="scmp-wontonnoodles"/>.

What distinguishes wonton noodle soup from a dumpling served in broth is that
neither element is treated as the point on its own. The wontons are made
small and few rather than filling the bowl, the noodles are a springy, thin
egg noodle rather than a filler, and the broth carrying both is built with its
own savoury depth — traditionally from dried flatfish or shrimp — rather than
left as a plain background liquid.
