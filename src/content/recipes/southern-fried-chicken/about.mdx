Two frying traditions are usually named as the parents of Southern fried
chicken, and only one of them did much of the actual work later credited to
the dish. Scottish settlers who reached the American South from the
eighteenth century onward are said to have fried chicken in hog lard, a
practice supported by a 1747 recipe in Hannah Glasse's English cookbook and a
1773 diary entry by the Scottish writer James Boswell describing fried
chicken at table <Cite ref="foodrepublic-scottish"/>. But that version was, by
most accounts, plain and unseasoned — a secondary household food rather than
anything close to what the name now describes <Cite ref="foodthatshapedus-fried-chicken"/>.

The seasoning, the battering and the technique of frying in a well-spiced
coating are credited instead to enslaved West African cooks working in
Southern kitchens, who are said to have added cayenne, paprika, black pepper
and garlic to the flour and drawn on their own tradition of deep-frying
seasoned foods, such as the bean fritters known as akara
<Cite ref="foodthatshapedus-fried-chicken"/>. Food historians Toni
Tipton-Martin, Frederick Douglass Opie and Anne L. Bower are cited for this
account, and it is presented as the more decisive influence on the dish that
exists today <Cite ref="foodthatshapedus-fried-chicken"/>.

The Scottish half of the story is the newer and shakier claim. It was first
proposed by the food writer John F. Mariani in his 1983 encyclopedia of
American food, and even the publications that repeat it are careful to call
it "a fascinating theory rather than cold, hard fact" <Cite ref="foodrepublic-scottish"/>.
What is not contested is that the technique reached its modern form on
Southern plantations, and that fried chicken went on to become, after
emancipation, a genuine trade: Black women vendors at Gordonsville, Virginia's
train station sold it to passengers as one of the few paths open to them
toward economic independence <Cite ref="foodthatshapedus-fried-chicken"/>.
