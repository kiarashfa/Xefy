Beef was not an everyday food in Japan until the Meiji period. The formal ban on eating
meat was lifted in December 1871, and Emperor Meiji is recorded as having tried beef the
following January <Cite ref="keio-juku"/>.

Beef hot pots, called gyunabe, came first. Food in Japan dates the first gyunabe
restaurant to Yokohama in 1862 and
dates a restaurant in Asakusa, Tokyo, that still serves gyunabe to 1886
<Cite ref="food-in-japan-gyunabe"/>. Its definition of the dish, drawn from the Kōjien
dictionary, is of a Meiji-period dish of beef simmered with vegetables in a pot
<Cite ref="food-in-japan-gyunabe"/>.

What separates gyudon from that hot pot is the amount of liquid. The same article
observes that gyunabe is cooked with less liquid than gyudon, which is one reason it
suits being packed as a bento <Cite ref="food-in-japan-gyunabe"/>. Gyudon keeps the
simmered beef and onion but serves them over rice in a bowl, with enough sauce to
soak into it.
