The popular account of tempura credits Portuguese missionaries and traders with bringing
the technique of batter-frying to Japan in the sixteenth century <Cite ref="jtc-tempura"/>.
Just One Cookbook gives that account, but adds that flour-batter deep-frying already existed
in Japan, so the origin is disputable <Cite ref="jtc-tempura"/>. Food in Japan says the
Portuguese-influenced technique entered through Nagasaki during the Nanban trade, and gives no
year for it <Cite ref="fij-tempura"/>.

The name is not settled either. Food in Japan reports two Portuguese-derived theories: one
links it to *tempero*, a word for seasoning, and the other to *temporas*, the religious
fasting periods. It treats neither as confirmed <Cite ref="fij-tempura"/>.

In Edo, cooks turned battered frying into a lighter dish suited to the city's seafood, and
it spread through street stalls. Food in Japan says that frying on a larger scale became
practical as the production of edible oil grew <Cite ref="fij-tempura"/>.

The earliest recipe is also disputed. Food in Japan says that the *Shokudoki* names tenpura,
but the National Diet Library lists the publication year of the surviving edition as unknown,
so the text does not establish when the name was first used <Cite ref="fij-tempura"/>.
