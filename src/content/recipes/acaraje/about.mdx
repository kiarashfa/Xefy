The name comes from Yoruba: *àkàrà*, the fried bean fritter itself, and *jẹ*,
to eat — a street vendor's call rather than a description, closer to "come and
eat akara" than to a recipe title <Cite ref="wiki-akara"/>. The fritter it
names was brought to Brazil by enslaved Yoruba people during the transatlantic
slave trade, along with the ingredients that still define it: black-eyed peas
and palm oil, both African rather than native to Brazil <Cite ref="bahia-acaraje"/>.

In Bahia it took on two lives that still run alongside each other. As street
food, it has been sold since the nineteenth century by the Baianas de
acarajé — women, historically often enslaved or recently freed, who fried and
sold it from trays and stalls, in some cases earning enough to buy family
members' freedom <Cite ref="bahia-acaraje"/>. In Candomblé, the Afro-Brazilian
religion also carried over from West African belief, acarajé is not only
eaten but offered, prepared as a ritual food for the orixás <Cite ref="wiki-akara"/>.
The two roles were never fully separate: the same fritter sold on a street
corner in Salvador carries a devotional history most of its customers never
have reason to think about.

The dendê palm oil it fries in is not a substitutable detail. Its colour and
heavy, slightly bitter flavour are as much a part of what makes an acarajé an
acarajé as the bean paste itself, which is why other West African and diaspora
versions fried in a neutral oil are usually given a different name rather than
called the same dish.
