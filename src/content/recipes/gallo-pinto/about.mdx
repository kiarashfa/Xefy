Both Costa Rica and Nicaragua call gallo pinto their own, and neither claim
is baseless, which is exactly why the dispute has never been settled.
Scholars broadly agree that the dish's roots are Afro-Caribbean, tracing to
enslaved Africans who worked the banana plantations along Central America's
Caribbean coast <Cite ref="wikipedia-gallo-pinto"/>
<Cite ref="preston-werner-gallo-pinto"/>. From there, it spread inland with
migrant labourers who came to work the railroads and plantations of Costa
Rica's Limón province, carrying the dish with them into the country's
interior.

The clearest documentary trace of the dish's early spread comes not from a
cookbook but a novel. Carlos Luis Fallas's *Mamita Yunai* describes
Costa Rican and Nicaraguan banana workers eating gallo pinto together on
the Caribbean coast, a reference anthropologist Theresa Preston-Werner
reads as evidence the dish was already an everyday food by the early
twentieth century <Cite ref="wikipedia-gallo-pinto"/>
<Cite ref="preston-werner-gallo-pinto"/>.

Costa Rica's own foundation story, by contrast, is a twentieth-century
legend rather than a documented event: a version involving a speckled hen
in a San José neighbourhood, dated to around 1930, decades after
Preston-Werner's evidence suggests the dish was already established on the
Caribbean coast <Cite ref="wikipedia-gallo-pinto"/>
<Cite ref="preston-werner-gallo-pinto"/>. The two countries' versions still
differ today in one clear, checkable way: Costa Rica's is made with black
beans, Nicaragua's with red.
