The practice this dish belongs to is old even by the standards of Greek cuisine.
Excavations on Santorini, led by the archaeologist Christos G. Doumas, uncovered
stone skewer supports — known as *krateutai* — pre-dating the island's Bronze Age
eruption, and archaeologists working at Mycenaean sites including Pylos and Mycenae
have found fired-clay trays interpreted as portable stands for grilling skewered
meat over coals <Cite ref="wiki-souvlaki"/><Cite ref="danaos-souvlaki"/>. Homer,
Aristophanes, Xenophon and Aristotle all describe meat cooked this way, under the
Classical Greek name *obeliskos*, a small spit <Cite ref="wiki-souvlaki"/>, and the
later writer Athenaeus describes a version wrapped in bread that reads as a close
ancestor of souvlaki served in pita today <Cite ref="danaos-souvlaki"/>.

The word *souvlaki* itself is more recent, descending from the medieval Greek
*souvla* ("skewer"), which was in turn borrowed from Latin *subula*
<Cite ref="wiki-souvlaki"/>. Byzantine sources describe hot meat shops in
Constantinople selling something close to the modern dish, so the vendor-and-skewer
format also has a long run behind it <Cite ref="wiki-souvlaki"/>. But the version
sold on Greek street corners today is a twentieth-century thing: the nineteenth-
century French writer Gustave Flaubert recorded Greeks grilling meat on bamboo
sticks in 1850, and the dish did not become the ubiquitous fast food it is now until
vendors, many from Boeotia, popularised it after the Second World War, through the
1960s <Cite ref="wiki-souvlaki"/>. Regional naming still splits the country: souvlaki
in the north, *kalamaki* — "little reed" — around Athens and the south
<Cite ref="wiki-souvlaki"/>.
