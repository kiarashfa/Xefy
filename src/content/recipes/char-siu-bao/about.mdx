Char siu bao takes its name directly from its filling: diced char siu, the
Cantonese roast pork, bound in a savoury-sweet sauce and folded into a bun
served as part of dim sum <Cite ref="wiki-char-siu-bao"/>
<Cite ref="legend-char-siu-bao"/>. Unlike a plain
mantou, the dough is deliberately left loose at the crown rather than sealed
shut, so that the bun cracks open a little as it rises — the split top is
how a char siu bao is told apart from other steamed buns at a glance.

The dish exists in two distinct, equally traditional forms. The steamed
version uses a dough leavened with both yeast and baking powder, giving a
soft, fine-textured white crumb, and was for a long time the standard offering
in teahouses serving dim sum by trolley <Cite ref="wiki-char-siu-bao"/>
<Cite ref="legend-char-siu-bao"/>. The baked version uses a similar enriched
dough finished with an egg-and-sugar wash instead of steam, producing a
shinier, browner, breadier bun that behaves more like a bakery pastry than a
dumpling <Cite ref="wiki-char-siu-bao"/> <Cite ref="legend-char-siu-bao"/>.
Both are built on the same
filling, so the difference between them is entirely in the dough and the
cooking method rather than in what is inside.

The two forms are not in competition so much as suited to different settings:
the steamed bun belongs to the teahouse and the trolley, eaten fresh from the
bamboo steamer as part of a wider dim sum spread, while the baked version
suits the standalone Chinese bakery, sold individually to be eaten on the
move. Chefs have continued to vary both — pairing the filling with a
pineapple-bun crust, or using a premium cut of pork and a honey glaze — but
the underlying pairing of char siu with a bun built specifically to hold it
has stayed constant <Cite ref="legend-char-siu-bao"/>.
