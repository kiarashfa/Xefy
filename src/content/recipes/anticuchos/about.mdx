Anticuchos take their name from the Quechua *antikuchu*, generally read as a
combination of *anti* — the eastern Andean region, Antisuyu — and *kuchu*, "cut"
<Cite ref="wiki-anticucho"/>. That etymology is the basis for the commonly told
account that skewered, spiced meat has an Inca-era history in the Andes, prepared
originally with llama <Cite ref="wiki-anticucho"/>. Other accounts place the dish's
origin later and more specifically, in colonial Lima, with no pre-Hispanic
antecedent claimed at all <Cite ref="cvche-anticucho"/>. The two versions are not
easily reconciled from the sources available, and what they agree on is the period
that matters most for the dish eaten today.

That agreed history is colonial. After the Spanish arrived, beef displaced llama and
garlic entered the marinade <Cite ref="wiki-anticucho"/>. Beef heart specifically
enters the story through slavery: Spanish colonizers ate the muscle cuts and
discarded the organ meats, and enslaved Africans working in and around Lima were
given the hearts along with other offal <Cite ref="wiki-anticucho"/><Cite ref="cvche-anticucho"/>.
They marinated the heart in vinegar, garlic, cumin and ají peppers and grilled it
over open coals, by one account on sugarcane sticks in place of metal skewers
<Cite ref="cvche-anticucho"/>. What began as a way of making a discarded cut edible
became, over generations, a dish sold from carts on street corners throughout Peru
and eaten across every social class rather than remaining what it started as.
