*Kebab* itself is an old word before it names any one dish. It reached English by
way of Arabic and Persian, and the medieval Arabic and Turkish terms are themselves
thought to derive from an older Persian *kabab*, possibly rooted further back in
Aramaic words for burning or roasting <Cite ref="wiki-kebab"/>. The earliest written
description of the dish comes from a tenth-century Baghdad cookbook, which describes
kebab as meat cut up and either pan-fried or grilled <Cite ref="wiki-kebab"/>. Kebab
had reached the Indian subcontinent by at least the fourteenth century: the traveller
Ibn Battuta recorded it being served in the royal houses of the Delhi Sultanate
<Cite ref="wiki-kebab"/>. A separate account pushes that arrival back a century
further, crediting migrants and soldiers who came with the Sultanate's founder,
Qutb-ud-din Aibek, in the thirteenth century, bringing kebab with them from Turkey,
Iraq and Iran as Delhi was built into an imperial capital
<Cite ref="mangalmedia-delhikebabs"/>.

What neither record establishes is *seekh* kebab specifically — mince, rather than
cut meat, worked onto a skewer. The word *seekh* is simply Urdu and Hindi for
skewer, and the dish is documented as belonging to the Indian subcontinent's
cuisines without a separate account of when meat began to be minced for it rather
than cut into pieces <Cite ref="wiki-seekh"/>. An article devoted specifically to
Delhi's kebab history names several distinct minced and mixed kebabs from the
Mughal period — a whole chicken stuffed with minced meat, and a Levantine-derived
patty of mince bound with chickpea and egg — but does not single out seekh kebab
among them or date it separately <Cite ref="mangalmedia-delhikebabs"/>. The popular
telling that credits Mughal court kitchens with refining kebabs cut into mince is
widely repeated but not found in the sourcing available here, and is left out
rather than stated as fact.
