Ugali is named from Swahili, but the grain it is made from is not native to
East Africa. Maize arrived by way of Portuguese traders, brought initially to
feed garrisons on the coast somewhere between the sixteenth and eighteenth
centuries, well after sorghum and millet had already been established as the
region's staple cereals for a very long time <Cite ref="wiki-ugali"/>
<Cite ref="elephant-ugali"/>. For a long stretch after that, maize remained
a minor crop rather than a staple one.

What changed its status was not the plant but colonial labour policy. From
around the first two decades of the twentieth century, settler farms in
Kenya paid their African workers partly in a daily ration of maize meal,
known as *posho*, alongside beans and meat, and workers found the diet
monotonous enough that cash wages gradually replaced it by the 1930s
<Cite ref="elephant-ugali"/>. Maize suited the colonial economy for reasons
that had nothing to do with taste: it demanded less labour to grow than
sorghum or millet at a moment when men were being pulled into wage work,
warfare and schooling, and once hammermills spread through the 1920s it could
be milled into flour far faster than grains that first needed dehulling
<Cite ref="elephant-ugali"/>. Production climbed accordingly, from roughly
750,000 bags in 1920 to nearly two million by the 1940s
<Cite ref="elephant-ugali"/>, and maize meal settled into the position
sorghum and millet had held before it — a shift that, across the wider
continent, is generally understood to have completed only in the second half
of the twentieth century <Cite ref="wiki-ugali"/>.

Ugali is also only one name among many for a dish repeated, with local
variation, across most of the continent: the same stiff maize porridge is
nsima in Malawi, sadza in Zimbabwe, pap in South Africa and posho in Uganda,
among a great many other regional names for what is, at the level of
technique, the same maize meal worked to the same dense, elastic consistency
<Cite ref="wiki-ugali"/>.
