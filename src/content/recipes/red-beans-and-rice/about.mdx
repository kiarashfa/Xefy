The red kidney bean itself is not native to Louisiana. It is a variety of the
common bean domesticated in Central America, and the version New Orleans
cooks came to rely on most likely arrived with refugees of the Haitian
Revolution, who settled the city in large numbers between 1791 and 1810 and
brought with them a rice-and-bean dish of their own, *diri kolé ak pwa*
<Cite ref="parishes-2024"/>.

What New Orleans did with the bean was tie it to the calendar. Monday was
washday, an all-day task of scrubbing linen by hand, and it left no one free
to stand over a stove. A pot of dried beans, needing only an occasional stir,
could simmer for hours on its own while the household did its laundry — and
it was seasoned not with a fresh cut of meat but with whatever ham bone was
left from Sunday's dinner, making the dish as much a use for leftovers as a
meal in its own right <Cite ref="parishes-2024"/>. The trinity of onion,
green pepper and celery, and the long, low simmer that turns the beans
creamy, both follow from that same logic of a pot left to itself.

The Monday habit outlasted the washday it was built around, and became a
citywide institution: restaurants keep it on the menu specifically for
Mondays, and households that have not owned a washboard in generations still
treat the day as red beans day <Cite ref="myneworleans-ritual"/>. Louis
Armstrong, a New Orleans native, took the dish as a personal emblem of home
and is remembered for signing letters "Red beans and ricely yours"
<Cite ref="parishes-2024"/>.
