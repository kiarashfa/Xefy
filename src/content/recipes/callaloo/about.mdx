The word "callaloo" is the more stable thing about the dish; what it names
changes from island to island. The name itself is traced to West Africa,
specifically to the Mbundu people of what is now Angola, where recipes for
a dish called calalu are recorded in both Angola and neighbouring São Tomé
<Cite ref="wikipedia-callaloo"/> <Cite ref="heritagefoodstories-callaloo"/>.
It travelled to the Caribbean in the same forced migration that carried
okra and other West African vegetables into the region's cooking more
generally.

What that inherited name attaches to, though, is not one plant. In Jamaica,
Belize and several other islands, callaloo means a wild, indigenous variety
of amaranth, quickly stir-fried with aromatics into a side dish that holds
its shape. In Trinidad and neighbouring islands, callaloo instead means the
leaf of dasheen (taro, the same plant grown across the tropics for its
starchy corm) pureed into a thick, coconut-milk stew with okra
<Cite ref="wikipedia-callaloo"/> <Cite ref="heritagefoodstories-callaloo"/>.
The two dishes are cooked differently, eaten differently, and taste of two
different plants, sharing little beyond the name and their shared West
African root.

Because "callaloo" is a name rather than a fixed recipe, a reader following
one from a Jamaican source and one from a Trinidadian source should expect
two different dishes on the plate, not two versions of the same one.
