The most widely repeated origin story places chicken tikka masala's invention
at the Shish Mahal, a restaurant opened in Glasgow's west end in 1964 by Ali
Ahmed Aslam, a chef who had emigrated from what was then British India as a
child <Cite ref="wiki-aslam"/><Cite ref="euronews-ctm"/>. By his own account, a customer complained in
the 1970s that a plate of chicken tikka was too dry, and Aslam improvised a
sauce (yoghurt, cream and spices worked into tinned tomato soup) to answer
the complaint <Cite ref="wiki-aslam"/><Cite ref="euronews-ctm"/>. It is a good story, and it is the one
told most often, but neither account treats it as settled: one source
crediting Aslam with the invention still concedes that "the origin of the
dish is not certain," and the other sets the Shish Mahal story directly
beside a documented earlier recipe rather than presenting it alone
<Cite ref="wiki-aslam"/><Cite ref="euronews-ctm"/>.

The strongest documented counter-claim predates the Shish Mahal itself. A
recipe for "Shahi Chicken Masala," close enough to be treated as a forerunner,
appears in Balbir Singh's cookbook from 1961, three years before Aslam's
restaurant opened at all <Cite ref="euronews-ctm"/>. That alone does not
settle the matter, since a printed antecedent and the popular dish now served
under this name are not necessarily the same lineage, but it does mean the
Glasgow story cannot be the whole account.

The dispute was serious enough to be tested formally. A Glasgow member of
parliament sought European Union Protected Designation of Origin status for
chicken tikka masala as a specifically Glaswegian invention, comparable to the
protection held by Melton Mowbray pork pies, and the bid failed
<Cite ref="euronews-ctm"/>. What is not in question is the dish's adopted
home: it became, by one well-known 2011 survey, Britain's single most popular
restaurant dish <Cite ref="euronews-ctm"/>, an adaptation of South Asian
cooking to British taste that British curry houses, wherever the recipe
first came from, made unmistakably their own.
