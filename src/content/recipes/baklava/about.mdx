Few desserts carry as contested an origin as baklava, and the argument is not
only historical — Turkish newspapers speculated about the matter in 2012 after
a sitting American president was photographed eating baklava at a Greek
Independence Day dinner <Cite ref="smithsonian-baklava"/>. A Greek claim points
to the ancient "placenta cake" described by Roman writers — a baked,
honey-soaked layered dough the food historian Patrick Faas has read as a
direct ancestor — while a Turkish claim rests on Ottoman kitchens rather than
antiquity: the pastry as it is eaten today is generally held to have been
developed in the imperial kitchens of Topkapı Palace in Constantinople
<Cite ref="wiki-baklava"/>. A third account traces the layering technique to
the flatbreads of nomadic Turkic cooks in Central Asia, carried west long
before the Ottoman court ever served it <Cite ref="wiki-baklava"/>. None of the
three is settled, and the word itself is no help in choosing between them — the
earliest known written use of "baklava" is not a recipe at all but a line in a
poem by the 15th-century mystic Kaygusuz Abdal <Cite ref="wiki-baklava"/>.

The Ottoman court's own regard for the dish is better documented than its
invention. From 1520 the sultan marked the fifteenth day of Ramadan with the
"Baklava Procession", sending trays of it out to the Janissary Corps at a rate
of one tray for every ten soldiers, a custom that continued until the corps was
disbanded in 1826 <Cite ref="smithsonian-baklava"/>.

Gaziantep's modern claim to the pistachio version is comparatively recent and
comparatively well documented. The city sits inland from Turkey's
pistachio-growing region, and the Güllüoğlu family, who trace their baklava
back to training in Aleppo and Damascus, describe adding local pistachio to
the family recipe around 1871 <Cite ref="smithsonian-baklava"/>. Gaziantep
baklava — thinner layers, a denser pistachio filling, syrup rather than honey —
was registered as a geographical indication by Turkey's patent office in 2008
and then granted Protected Geographical Indication status by the European
Commission in 2013, the first Turkish product to receive it, a decision that
reportedly disappointed baklava producers elsewhere, Greece included
<Cite ref="wiki-baklava"/><Cite ref="smithsonian-baklava"/>. That recognition
covers the Gaziantep style specifically rather than baklava as a whole, which
is why the walnut-and-honey version associated with Greece and the ater-soaked,
orange-blossom-scented version found across the Levant are treated here as the
same family of pastry rather than the same recipe: three regional answers to
one layering technique, none of them the one true original.
