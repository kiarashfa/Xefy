The rolled, spiced sweet bun eaten across the United States today did not
start there. The earliest recorded recipes resembling a cinnamon roll come
from Germany in the sixteenth century, from where the idea spread north into
Scandinavia <Cite ref="wiki-cinnamonroll"/>. The specific form now most
closely associated with the dish in Sweden — a soft yeasted dough, rolled
around cinnamon and sugar, cut into spirals — took shape only after the First
World War: wartime restrictions on imported sugar, eggs and butter had kept it
off Swedish tables, and cinnamon buns began appearing in cafés and bakeries
once those restrictions lifted in the 1920s, where the bun became known as
the kanelbulle <Cite ref="thelocal-kanelbulle"/>.

German immigrants carried their own version of the rolled cinnamon bun to the
United States well before the Swedish kanelbulle had taken its now-familiar
shape, so the American cinnamon roll's ancestry runs through Germany rather
than through Sweden directly <Cite ref="wiki-cinnamonroll"/>. Sweden still
marks the bun's place in its food culture directly, though more recently than
the bun itself: Sweden's Home Baking Council created a dedicated Cinnamon Bun
Day, held each October, in 1999 <Cite ref="thelocal-kanelbulle"/>.

The American chain Cinnabon, founded in 1985, is often assumed to have invented
the cinnamon roll outright. It did not — the roll it sells is a late,
heavily sweetened descendant of a bun with several centuries and at least two
countries behind it already <Cite ref="wiki-cinnamonroll"/>.
