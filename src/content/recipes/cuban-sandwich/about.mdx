The Cuban sandwich did not begin as an American invention, whatever the
Tampa–Miami rivalry over it might suggest. Researchers at the University of
South Florida traced its origin to Havana, where it appeared on menus by
the 1800s under the name mixto ("mixed") rather than as a Cuban sandwich
at all <Cite ref="usf-cuban-sandwich"/>.

What turned the mixto into the sandwich known today happened in Florida, not
Cuba, once the cigar industry and its largely Cuban workforce relocated from
Key West to Tampa's Ybor City in the mid-1880s
<Cite ref="wikipedia-cuban-sandwich"/> <Cite ref="usf-cuban-sandwich"/>.
Tampa held the largest Cuban population in the United States for decades
before Miami took on that role after 1959, and it was Tampa, not Havana, that
gave the sandwich the form most identified with it in the United States
today <Cite ref="usf-cuban-sandwich"/>.

That form still carries a genuine regional disagreement, not a settled
standard: a 1934 description of Tampa's version lists ham, lean pork, Swiss
cheese, soft salami and dill pickle on Cuban bread, and Tampa kitchens have
kept the salami ever since <Cite ref="wikipedia-cuban-sandwich"/>
<Cite ref="usf-cuban-sandwich"/>. Miami's version, which spread widely
after the wave of Cuban exiles who arrived from 1959 onward, drops the
salami entirely, a difference the two cities have argued over for decades
without resolving <Cite ref="wikipedia-cuban-sandwich"/>
<Cite ref="usf-cuban-sandwich"/>.
