Risotto alla milanese is rice cooked slowly in broth and finished with butter and
cheese, and the saffron is what defines it: the threads give the rice its colour and
most of its flavour. Beef marrow appears in the earliest recipes and is optional
today <Cite ref="italymag-risotto"/>.

The best-known origin story is set at the Duomo in 1574. Valerio di Fiandra, a
master glassmaker working on the cathedral's windows, was preparing his daughter's
wedding, and an assistant nicknamed Zafferano, saffron, is said to have stirred the
spice into the rice as a joke <Cite ref="gac-risotto"/>. The story is told as a
legend <Cite ref="gac-risotto"/> <Cite ref="wiki-risotto"/>, and no mention of the dish
appears between that date and the 1800s <Cite ref="italymag-risotto"/>.

The first printed recipe is from 1809, under the name riso giallo in padella, the
yellow rice in the pan. It sautés the rice in butter with marrow and onion, and adds
hot broth in which saffron has been dissolved <Cite ref="italymag-risotto"/>. The name
risotto alla milanese came later, when a Milanese chef, Felice Luraschi, gives the
dish its present title <Cite ref="italymag-risotto"/>.
