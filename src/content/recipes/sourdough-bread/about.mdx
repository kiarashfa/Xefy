Naturally leavened bread is ancient, and nobody claims San Francisco invented
it — wild-yeast fermentation was in use in Egypt millennia before California
existed as a place name. What San Francisco supplied was the story that made
sourdough an American identity rather than just a method.

When the Gold Rush of 1848–49 drew tens of thousands of prospectors west,
sourdough starter travelled with them because it was durable, self-renewing and
did not require a fresh supply of commercial yeast in a mining camp
<Cite ref="smithsonian-sourdough"/>. A small amount of starter let a miner
stretch a little flour into bread indefinitely, and the culture became valuable
enough that miners reportedly kept it close to their bodies, even sleeping with
the pot on cold nights so the yeast and bacteria did not die <Cite ref="smithsonian-sourdough"/>.
The word "sourdough" became slang for a long-term prospector himself, a sign of
how closely the two were associated.

French and Italian immigrant bakers opened shop in San Francisco during the
same population boom, among them Isidore Boudin, whose bakery — founded in
1849 and still trading today — is generally credited as the origin of the
continuously maintained "mother dough" culture the city's bakeries still use
<Cite ref="food52-sourdough"/>. The particular tang associated with San
Francisco sourdough is usually attributed to local wild yeast and bacteria
strains, though the extent to which any given loaf still depends on a
genuinely local culture, after a century and a half of shipping starters in
and out of the city, is harder to verify than the marketing suggests.
