The name has nothing to do with flavour. A *dandan* is a bamboo carrying
pole, balanced across a vendor's shoulders with a basket hanging from each
end — one holding noodles, the other the sauce, seasonings and a small
stove — and the dish is named for that method of selling rather than for
any ingredient in the bowl <Cite ref="wiki-dandan"/> <Cite ref="michelin-dandan"/>.

The most repeated account of who first sold noodles this way names a peddler
called Chen Baobao, said to have started carrying and selling them in the
Sichuan town of Zigong in 1841 before the style spread to Chengdu, where
vendors began adding minced pork to what had been a plainer noodle. It is
presented, even in the retelling, explicitly as legend rather than settled
history — a single named figure standing in for what was more likely a wider
street-food practice among Sichuan's mobile noodle vendors
<Cite ref="michelin-dandan"/>.

The dish most non-Sichuan diners know by this name is, by most accounts, not
really the same dish. The Chengdu original is dry: noodles tossed in a small,
concentrated pool of chilli oil, doubanjiang and Sichuan pepper, with barely
any liquid beyond what clings to the strands. A soup-based version, milder
and sometimes thickened with sesame paste or peanut butter, developed later
in Hong Kong and became the more widely exported form abroad, even though it
is comparatively uncommon in Sichuan itself <Cite ref="wiki-dandan"/>
<Cite ref="michelin-dandan"/>. The
distance between the two versions is less a matter of one being more
authentic than the other than of two different cities solving the same
problem — how much sauce a bowl of wheat noodles needs — in opposite
directions.
