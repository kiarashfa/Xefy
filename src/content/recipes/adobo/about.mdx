Long before the dish had the name it carries now, cooks across the archipelago
were simmering meat and fish in vinegar, salt and garlic — an acid-and-salt
preservation method suited to a hot, humid climate with no refrigeration
<Cite ref="michelin-adobo"/>. What survives of that method is essentially
adobo's spine: vinegar, garlic, pepper and a slow simmer, with everything else
treated as a later addition.

The name itself is borrowed. Spanish colonisers, noticing that the technique
resembled their own *adobar* — to marinate or pickle — began calling it
*adobo de los naturales*, "adobo of the natives." The label is first recorded
in Pedro de San Buenaventura's 1613 *Vocabulario de la lengua tagala*, a
Tagalog dictionary compiled by a Franciscan missionary
<Cite ref="wiki-adobo"/> <Cite ref="businessmirror-adobo"/>. What the dish was
actually called before that borrowing is not settled — no native name for it
survives <Cite ref="businessmirror-adobo"/>.

Soy sauce, the ingredient most people now associate with adobo's dark colour
and savoury depth, is a later layer again, introduced through Chinese trade
and settlement rather than present from the start
<Cite ref="wiki-adobo"/> <Cite ref="businessmirror-adobo"/>. Its arrival split
the dish in two. *Adobong itim* ("black adobo"), coloured and salted with soy
sauce, is now the version most widely cooked and exported. *Adobong puti*
("white adobo") omits the soy sauce entirely, seasoning with salt instead, and
is generally held to sit closer to the dish's pre-Hispanic form
<Cite ref="wiki-adobo"/> <Cite ref="michelin-adobo"/>. Neither is more
"correct" than the other in contemporary cooking, but only one of them —
whichever a household grew up with — usually gets called adobo without
qualification.

What both versions agree on is that vinegar, not soy sauce, is the
non-negotiable ingredient: a version without it reads as a different dish,
however dark and salty the sauce <Cite ref="michelin-adobo"/>.
