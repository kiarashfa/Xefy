The word poke means "to slice" or "cut crosswise" in Hawaiian, and it names
exactly what fishermen did with the day's catch long before the dish looked
anything like it does now <Cite ref="hpr-poke"/>. The earliest preparation was
raw reef fish cut into pieces and seasoned with sea salt, limu (seaweed) and
ʻinamona — a relish of roasted, pounded kukui nut — eaten as a snack rather
than a meal <Cite ref="hawaii-magazine-poke"/> <Cite ref="wiki-poke"/>.

Almost everything that now reads as essential to poke arrived afterward.
Onions were unknown in Hawaii until Captain Cook's voyages introduced seed in
1778 <Cite ref="wiki-poke"/>. Shoyu, sesame oil and ahi tuna itself entered
through the Chinese, Japanese and Korean labourers brought to work the sugar
and pineapple plantations from the mid-nineteenth century on, and their
soy-based seasoning gradually displaced the older salt-and-ʻinamona style for
many cooks <Cite ref="hawaii-magazine-poke"/>. The dish stayed a fish-market and home-kitchen food
for most of that history, sold and eaten in small quantities as a pupu — a
snack — rather than a full plate, a habit chef Keoni Chang describes as buying
"a quarter or half-pound of two or three varieties" on the way home from work
<Cite ref="nbc-poke"/>.

The poke sold on the United States mainland today, portioned into a full bowl
over rice with a menu of optional toppings, is a distinct and recent format
rather than a transplant of that tradition. Pokéworks partner Mike Wu frames
its appeal in build-your-own terms — "it's like having your own personal sushi
chef" <Cite ref="nbc-poke"/> — which is close to the opposite of how poke was
historically bought and eaten in Hawaii. The dressed, cubed fish itself is the
continuous thread through all of this; the rice, the bowl and the topping bar
are the mainland's addition to it, not a return to an older form.
