Siu mai's name is written several ways in Chinese, and the differences are
not spelling variants of one original but the record of the dish moving
between regions. The earliest form, 捎賣, meant something closer to "sold on
the side" — a snack offered alongside tea rather than a meal in itself — and
is associated with tea houses in Hohhot, in what is now Inner Mongolia
<Cite ref="wiki-shumai"/>. As merchants from Shanxi carried the snack toward
Beijing and Tianjin, the characters shifted to their modern forms, 燒賣 and
稍美 among them, while the pronunciation stayed close to the original
<Cite ref="wiki-shumai"/>. Cantonese *siu mai* is one of those descendants,
adopted into Guangdong's tea house culture and, eventually, into dim sum
service specifically <Cite ref="scmp-siumai"/>.

The dish's presentation is old enough to be treated as settled rather than
disputed: a thin wheat wrapper gathered loosely around a filling and left open
at the top, rather than pleated shut, so the filling is visible before it is
eaten <Cite ref="wiki-shumai"/>. In today's Cantonese kitchens that filling is
built from pork and shrimp, but the open-topped wrapper — the detail that
survives across every regional version, from the mutton-filled tea house
snack of the north to the pork-and-shrimp dim sum staple of the south — is the
part of the dish that travelled furthest with its name intact
<Cite ref="scmp-siumai"/>.
