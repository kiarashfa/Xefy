No dish on this site has a more contested founding story than the hamburger, and
none of the leading claims is properly settled. Louis' Lunch in New Haven,
Connecticut credits Louis Lassen with assembling the first hamburger sandwich
in 1900 for a customer in a hurry, a claim strong enough that the Library of
Congress named the diner the sandwich's birthplace in 2000
<Cite ref="cthistory-louislunch"/>. Frank and Charles Menches are separately
said to have substituted ground beef for pork sausage at a county fair in
Hamburg, New York, in 1885 after running short of pork, and further claims
point to Seymour, Wisconsin and Athens, Texas — all resting on oral tradition
rather than surviving written record <Cite ref="tastingtable-hamburgerorigin"/>.

None of these accounts explains the two styles cooked today, which are a
twentieth-century divergence in technique rather than a difference in age. A
thick patty, seared and left alone so the juice stays inside, is the diner
standard most of the disputed founding claims describe. The smash technique —
a thin ball of mince flattened hard onto a scorching griddle the instant it
lands — trades that juiciness for a heavily browned crust across the entire
face of the patty, and has become the dominant style behind the modern wave of
American smash-burger restaurants. Both are correctly "the" hamburger; they
simply optimise for different things.
