Mantou's early history is harder to pin down than its best-known story
suggests. Its popular origin story is set in the Three Kingdoms period,
roughly seven centuries before the plain-and-filled split described below,
and credits the strategist Zhuge Liang with inventing the bun. Campaigning
south of a river
that local custom held could only be crossed after sacrificing fifty men,
Zhuge Liang is said to have refused the killing and instead had his cooks
shape meat-stuffed dough into rough approximations of human heads, offering
these to the river spirit in place of real ones, hence *mántóu*, "barbarian's
head" <Cite ref="gbc-bao"/>. It is a vivid story and, on the written record,
an unverifiable one: it survives as popular legend and folk etymology rather
than as a documented event contemporary with Zhuge Liang's own lifetime
<Cite ref="wiki-mantou"/> <Cite ref="gbc-bao"/>.

What is better attested is the later split between mantou and baozi. By the
tenth century, mantou had narrowed to mean a plain, unfilled steamed bun, while
the filled version went its own way under the name baozi <Cite ref="gbc-bao"/>.
That division still holds: mantou today is bread in the most literal sense on
a northern Chinese table, torn and dipped or eaten alongside a dish rather than
filled with one, while its stuffed relative is a dish in its own right.
