Mapo tofu's name is a description rather than a compliment: *ma* refers to
pockmarks and *po* to an old woman, so *mapo* means, plainly, "pockmarked
grandmother" <Cite ref="wiki-mapo"/> <Cite ref="harpersbazaar-mapo"/>. The
woman in question is usually identified as Mrs Chen, who ran a small eatery
with her husband near a bridge on the northern edge of Chengdu in the 1860s,
serving cheap, filling tofu to labourers and porters passing through
<Cite ref="harpersbazaar-mapo"/>. Unlike many named-after-someone dishes, this
one has an unusually direct line to its supposed source: the restaurant is
said to still trade under the Chen Mapo name in Chengdu today
<Cite ref="harpersbazaar-mapo"/>.

The dish did not arrive fully formed. Mrs Chen's own version is described as
tofu cooked simply with chilli, pork and Sichuan pepper, seasoned for
labourers who needed a cheap, filling meal rather than for refinement
<Cite ref="harpersbazaar-mapo"/>. The recipe kept changing for decades after
her: the meat, the exact seasoning and the balance of chilli to numbing
Sichuan pepper have all shifted between households, restaurants and published
recipes since, and no single version has ever been fixed as definitive.

What has stayed fixed throughout is the dish's balance of textures and
sensations rather than any single fixed ingredient list: soft tofu against a
thick, deeply savoury sauce, chilli heat against the specific tingling
numbness of Sichuan pepper, and a generous slick of red, chilli-stained oil
that is as much the point of the dish as the tofu itself.
