Crumble is usually explained as a Second World War shortcut around pastry
rationing: with butter and the quantities of flour needed for a shortcrust
lid both restricted, a loose, rubbed topping dropped straight over stewed
fruit needed far less fat and no rolling pin. The claim is taken seriously
enough to appear in the Oxford Companion to Food, a standard reference on
the subject, which states that crumble "probably originated during the
Second World War, as a quicker, easier alternative to pastry," and it fits
the wider, independently documented pattern of the wartime Ministry of Food
employing home economists to develop recipes that worked around exactly the
ingredients rationing had put out of reach
<Cite ref="gulfnews-crumble"/><Cite ref="wiki-crumble"/>.

The dish's own printed record is thinner than that story implies. Even the
Oxford Companion to Food's account points only to a recipe in the 1950
edition of Fannie Farmer's Boston Cookbook as the earliest printed mention it
can confidently identify, several years after the rationing that
supposedly produced the dish had already ended
<Cite ref="gulfnews-crumble"/>. Whatever the exact date crumble first reached
print, the gap between the tidy wartime story and the thin paper trail
behind it is itself the more honest account of how the dish is documented.
