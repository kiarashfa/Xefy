Mochi is steamed glutinous rice, pounded or kneaded into a smooth, sticky dough. Food in Japan says
that locals traditionally make it in a ceremony called *mochitsuki*. The Japan National Tourism
Organization describes the traditional method as pounding the cooked rice in a large wooden mortar
with a heavy mallet <Cite ref="fij-mochi"/> <Cite ref="jnto-mochi"/>.

Mochi has long been a ritual offering. Food in Japan says that rice cakes were believed to contain
gods and spirits and were considered divine food, and that the New Year's kagami mochi is an offering
of large and small round cakes stacked together <Cite ref="fij-mochi"/>. The Japan National Tourism
Organization calls mochi a highly propitious offering since ancient times, and notes that mochi is
added to ozoni, the soup eaten on New Year's Day <Cite ref="jnto-mochi"/>.

The earliest history is disputed. Food in Japan introduces a claim that rice cakes appeared at least in the
Kofun period, citing an earthenware steamer, with a hedge. It gives a Nara-period
account of giant rice cakes made from surplus rice in Tano, in Bungo Province, which it presents as a
legend <Cite ref="fij-mochi"/>. Its dated citation is the *Azuma Kagami* entry for
16 May 1193, which describes black, red and white rice cakes eaten in layers <Cite ref="fij-mochi"/>.

Regional customs are clearer. When the area was part of the Sendai domain, the Date clan ordered farmers,
about four hundred years ago, to offer mochi at temples and shrines on the first and fifteenth of each
month, and the obligation was later extended to at least sixty days a year. During the Edo period, a
full mochi meal called mochi honzen developed in the Date domain, and poorer farmers who could not
afford white rice made *shiina mochi* from coarser grains <Cite ref="jnto-mochi"/>.
