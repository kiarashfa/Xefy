The story told about brigadeiro is specific enough to sound documented: in
1945, supporters of the Air Force brigadier and presidential candidate
Eduardo Gomes — many of them women voting in a Brazilian national election
for the first time — sold a condensed-milk sweet at campaign rallies and
named it after him <Cite ref="atlasobscura-brigadeiro"/>. Gomes lost to
Eurico Gaspar Dutra, but the name outlived the campaign entirely
<Cite ref="wiki-brigadeiro"/><Cite ref="em-brigadeiro"/>. It is a good story, and it is presented by
those who tell it as lore rather than as settled history — a distinction
worth keeping, since the ingredients behind it were already in Brazilian
kitchens well before 1945 <Cite ref="atlasobscura-brigadeiro"/>.

Condensed milk had been sold in Brazil since Nestlé began marketing it there
in the 1920s, and it became a practical stand-in for scarcer dessert
ingredients during the rationing years of the Second World War — the exact
conditions the 1945 story assumes rather than invents <Cite ref="atlasobscura-brigadeiro"/>.
A closely related sweet from Rio Grande do Sul, "negrinho," uses the same
condensed-milk-and-cocoa base and has been traced by at least one researcher
to that same 1920s arrival of tinned condensed milk, and a regional-press
account of the sweet's history separately describes "negrinho" as an older
name than the brigadeiro story itself — evidence that the confection likely
predates the 1945 campaign, whatever its name was before Eduardo Gomes
became attached to it <Cite ref="wiki-brigadeiro"/><Cite ref="em-brigadeiro"/>.

What survived past the election, either way, is a sweet built for wartime
scarcity: condensed milk, cocoa and butter, cooked down to a fudge that
needs no oven, no eggs and no ingredient harder to find than a tin from the
cupboard — which may explain its staying power rather better than any
election slogan does.
