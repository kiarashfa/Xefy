The oldest butter tart recipe anyone has produced is dated and precise: it
appears in *The Women's Auxiliary of the Royal Victoria Hospital Cookbook*,
published in Barrie, Ontario, in 1900, submitted by a Mrs Malcolm MacLeod and
calling for nothing more than a cup of sugar, half a cup of butter, two eggs
and a cup of currants <Cite ref="wiki-buttertart"/><Cite ref="sidney-buttertart"/>.
What came before that recipe is genuinely unresolved. One theory traces the
tart to "border tarts," a dried-fruit pastry carried to Ontario by Scottish
and English settlers from the Anglo-Scottish border region; a second traces
it to *tarte au sucre*, a sugar pie possibly introduced generations earlier
by French women who settled in Quebec in the 1600s, and adapted over time
with local maple syrup, butter and dried fruit
<Cite ref="wiki-buttertart"/><Cite ref="sidney-buttertart"/>.
Neither theory has documentary proof strong enough to settle the question, and
a museum account of the dish puts it about as plainly as the evidence allows:
butter tarts "seem to have been invented in Canada, although the exact origin
is unknown" <Cite ref="sidney-buttertart"/>.

What is not in dispute is how quickly the tart spread once it reached print.
Further recipes appeared through the following decades (a pie cookbook in
1915, wider newspaper and farm-cookbook circulation soon after), and the tart
had become genuinely popular across the country by the 1920s and 1930s
<Cite ref="wiki-buttertart"/><Cite ref="sidney-buttertart"/>. It has since
been claimed as a specifically Canadian pastry rather than a borrowed one,
formal enough in that status to have been one of five desserts on a Canada
Post commemorative stamp set released in April 2019
<Cite ref="wiki-buttertart"/><Cite ref="csn-buttertart"/>, a symbolic
honour that rather overstates how settled its own history actually is.
