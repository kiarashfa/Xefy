The technique behind patacones — frying green plantain, flattening it, and
frying it again until crisp — is not itself Colombian in origin. African
agricultural and culinary traditions shaped how plantain was grown and
prepared once it reached the Americas, brought by enslaved Africans for
whom the fruit became an affordable, filling food
<Cite ref="atk-tostones-patacon"/> <Cite ref="vozrealities-patacon"/>. What
is more locally specific is the Colombian name for the result, which
follows a different history from the Caribbean's more common name for the
same food.

Where "tostón" comes straightforwardly from *tostar*, the Spanish verb for
toasting, "patacón" is argued to travel a considerably longer route: from
the Arabic *batakká*, later associated in Italy with the word *patácca*,
and into colonial Spanish American use as a name for a silver coin — a
1627 document from Santa Fe, present-day Bogotá, already records "patacón"
used with that meaning <Cite ref="vozrealities-patacon"/>. On this account,
the fried plantain disc took its name not from how it was cooked but from
what it looked like once flattened: round and flat, echoing that colonial
coin closely enough for the name to stick.

The same food travels under different names today: tostones in much of the
Caribbean, patacones in Colombia and, further south, in Peru
<Cite ref="atk-tostones-patacon"/>, a naming split that maps political and
linguistic history onto a dish whose underlying technique never actually
changed.
