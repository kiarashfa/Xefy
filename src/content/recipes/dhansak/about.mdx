Dhansak belongs to the Parsis, the Zoroastrian community that left Persia for
Gujarat after the Arab conquest of the seventh and eighth centuries
<Cite ref="wiki-dhansak"/><Cite ref="dawn-dhansak"/>. It is generally said to descend from khoresh, the
Persian family of stews that braise meat with lentils, vegetables and fruit;
an early version made with plums, lentils, spinach and meat, served over
rice, is the one most often pointed to as its likely ancestor
<Cite ref="dawn-dhansak"/>.

That lineage is repeated more confidently than it can actually be documented:
no source fixes a date or place for when a Persian meat-and-lentil stew
became the distinct, tamarind-soured dish eaten today, and both Wikipedia and
Dawn's own account hedge it as "said to be" or "believed to have roots in"
rather than settled fact <Cite ref="wiki-dhansak"/><Cite ref="dawn-dhansak"/>.
Even the name is not agreed: Wikipedia traces it to the Sanskrit *dhānya*,
grain, and *śāka*, vegetables, describing what is in the pot, while Dawn's
account instead derives it from the Gujarati *dhan*, wealth, and *sak*,
vegetables <Cite ref="wiki-dhansak"/><Cite ref="dawn-dhansak"/>.

What is better attested is the dish's more recent history. Dhansak only
became widely known outside Parsi homes in the late nineteenth century, as
Bombay and Karachi industrialised and Parsi and Irani cafés began serving it
to the local workforce alongside tea, coffee and other snacks
<Cite ref="wiki-dhansak"/><Cite ref="dawn-dhansak"/>. Parsis who went on to
work as butlers and cooks in British households carried it further still,
and it entered British Indian restaurant menus from returning colonists,
though there it was flattened into something sweeter and milder than the
sour, dal-thick stew eaten in a Parsi home, part of a broader pattern of
curry-house kitchens sweetening and simplifying dishes for a British palate
<Cite ref="wiki-dhansak"/><Cite ref="csmonitor-curry"/>.

In a Parsi household dhansak is still cooked on Sundays, when there is time
for a pot that takes hours rather than minutes, and it is also the dish
customarily served four days after a death <Cite ref="wiki-dhansak"/>
<Cite ref="dawn-dhansak"/>.
