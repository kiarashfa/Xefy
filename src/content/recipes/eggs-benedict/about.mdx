Eggs Benedict has two competing origin stories, both set in New York in the
1890s, and no surviving evidence settles which — if either — is true.

In one version, a Wall Street stockbroker named Lemuel Benedict walked into
the Waldorf Hotel nursing a hangover and ordered poached eggs, bacon, buttered
toast and a pitcher of hollandaise sauce off-menu; the maître d'hôtel, Oscar
Tschirky, is said to have liked the combination enough to put a version of it
on the menu, swapping in an English muffin for the toast <Cite ref="chow-benedict"/>.
In the other, a regular at Delmonico's named Mrs LeGrand Benedict complained to
chef Charles Ranhofer that nothing on the lunch menu appealed to her, and
Ranhofer devised the dish for her on the spot, later publishing it as "Eggs
à la Benedick" in his 1894 cookbook *The Epicurean* <Cite ref="rth-benedict"/>.

Neither story has independent confirmation. Oscar Tschirky himself never
publicly confirmed the Lemuel Benedict account attributed to him
<Cite ref="chow-benedict"/>, and Ranhofer's own published version differs
enough from the modern dish that it is not a clean match either. The more
likely explanation, on the evidence available, is that both restaurants
arrived at closely related poached-egg-and-hollandaise dishes independently
around the same period, and that a single precise moment of invention never
existed at all <Cite ref="rth-benedict"/>.
