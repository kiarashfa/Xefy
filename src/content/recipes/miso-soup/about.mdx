Miso is not originally Japanese. Its ancestor is a Chinese fermented soy preparation
recorded from the third century BC, which arrived in Japan alongside Buddhism in the
sixth century AD; by the Nara period the name had shifted towards the modern one
<Cite ref="wiki-miso"/>. The active agent throughout is koji, the mould *Aspergillus
oryzae*, whose enzymes break the soy protein down into the amino acids the paste tastes
of <Cite ref="wiki-miso"/>.

The soup is the Japanese part. Miso began as an expensive food for the elite, and what
turned it into a daily one was a meal structure: *ichiju issai*, one soup and one dish,
the eating custom that took hold among the samurai in the Kamakura period between 1185
and 1333 <Cite ref="umami-miso"/>. Buddhist monks of the Muromachi period ground the
whole soybeans into a paste, which is what made miso dissolve rather than sit in the
bowl, and home brewing spread with it; industrial production followed in the Edo period
<Cite ref="wiki-miso"/><Cite ref="umami-miso"/>.

The result is that the soup is now the overwhelming use of the ingredient. More than
eighty per cent of Japan's annual miso production goes into miso soup, and around
three-quarters of Japanese people drink it at least once a day
<Cite ref="wiki-misosoup"/>.

What is fixed about it is thin: a dashi, a miso and a small number of solid things
suspended in it. The stock is most often kombu with katsuobushi, though dried sardines
or dried shiitake do the same job, and the paste may be white, red or a blend of the two
<Cite ref="wiki-misosoup"/>. Almost everything else is regional or seasonal, which is
why a dish this standardised in structure is never quite the same bowl twice.
