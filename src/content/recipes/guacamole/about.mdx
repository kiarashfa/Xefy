The name is Nahuatl and entirely literal: *āhuacamōlli*, from *āhuacatl*, avocado, and
*mōlli*, sauce <Cite ref="wiki-guacamole"/>. It reached English through American
Spanish, where the Online Etymology Dictionary records its first appearance in 1891
<Cite ref="etymonline-guacamole"/>.

The ingredient is very much older than the word. Avocado remains from caves in the
Tehuacán Valley have been dated to nine or ten thousand years ago, and the tree was
under cultivation across Mesoamerica by around 5000 BCE <Cite ref="wiki-guacamole"/>.

What is missing from all of this is a recipe. Two sixteenth-century Spanish sources
record the food without recording its making: Alonso de Molina's Nahuatl vocabulary of
1571 notes a delicacy of avocados with ground dried chillies, and Bernardino de
Sahagún's history describes three kinds of avocado and a range of medicinal uses for
the tree — but neither says how the sauce was prepared or how it was eaten
<Cite ref="collector-2024"/>. No original recipe survives. What does survive is the
equipment: the ingredients were ground together in a *molcaxitl*, the basalt mortar
still in daily use as the molcajete <Cite ref="collector-2024"/>.

The earliest description in English belongs to a buccaneer. William Dampier's *A New
Voyage Round the World* of 1697 has avocado flesh "mixed with sugar and lime-juice and
beaten together in a plate" <Cite ref="collector-2024"/> — the same operation performed
on the same fruit, and sweet rather than salted.

The Aztec pedigree is therefore real at the level of the ingredient, the name and the
tool, and thin at the level of the dish. Guacamole as it is made now — avocado crushed
into a ground paste of onion, chilli, coriander and salt — is a modern Mexican
preparation standing on a very old foundation, not a reconstruction of anything anyone
wrote down.
