Pupusas are old enough that even their documented history reaches back
centuries, and their pre-Columbian history further still. Historians trace
the dish to the Pipil people, who are said to have called it kukumuzin
rather than pupusa, and to the broader Mesoamerican tradition of filled
corn dough that may be as much as two thousand years old
<Cite ref="solidground-pupusa"/>. Before the Spanish arrived, the filling
was built from what indigenous cooks had to hand, without meat; contact
with Spanish colonisation from the 1570s introduced pork into what had been
a vegetarian dish, a shift a Franciscan friar's own writing from that decade
appears to describe directly <Cite ref="wikipedia-pupusa"/>
<Cite ref="solidground-pupusa"/>.

The dish's written record begins later and more precisely than its
culinary one: the earliest known mention of pupusas by name is said to
come from a nineteenth-century Guatemalan traveller's account of a similar
dish encountered in Nicaragua and linked, even then, to El Salvador
specifically.

Even the name is not settled fact. The standard reference dictionary for
Spanish traces "pupusa" to a Nawat word meaning "fluffy," but at least one
Salvadoran linguist has argued against this, pointing to the Pipil's own,
different name for the dish, kukumuzin, as evidence that the word's real
origin remains an open question rather than a settled one
<Cite ref="wikipedia-pupusa"/> <Cite ref="solidground-pupusa"/>.

What is beyond dispute is the dish's official status. El Salvador's
Legislative Assembly passed Decree 655 in 2005, declaring pupusas the
national dish "by reason of its autochthonous origin and popular
acceptance" and establishing the second Sunday of November as National
Pupusa Day <Cite ref="asamblea-pupusa"/> <Cite ref="wikipedia-pupusa"/>.
