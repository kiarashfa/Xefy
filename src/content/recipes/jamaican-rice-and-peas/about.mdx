Rice and peas is Jamaica's version of a pairing repeated, with local
variations, across the whole Caribbean: rice cooked together with a legume
rather than served beside it. The dish's deepest roots trace to West Africa,
where rice cultivation and rice-and-legume cooking were established
practices before the transatlantic slave trade forced both the knowledge
and the people who held it to the Caribbean. Pigeon peas — locally called
gungo peas — travelled the same route and were, by most accounts, the
dish's original legume in Jamaica, kept in wide use today alongside kidney
beans, which have become the more common everyday choice.

The dish's nickname is the part with the least settled history. Jamaican
linguist Frederic G. Cassidy recorded, in 1961, that rice and peas was
already known as the "Jamaican coat of arms" <Cite ref="wikipedia-rice-and-peas"/>
<Cite ref="higman-jamaica-coat-of-arms"/> — a name treated seriously enough by
food historians that it has since been the subject of its own academic
chapter, though where the phrase itself first arose is not established by
either account. What both sources agree on is the dish's status: not a
throwaway side but the dish treated as edible shorthand for Jamaica itself.

Rice and peas keeps a strong association with Sunday dinner and with
Christmas in Jamaica even as it has also moved onto everyday restaurant
menus across the island.
