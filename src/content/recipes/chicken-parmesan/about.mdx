Chicken parmesan has no real equivalent in Italy, where the dish it is named
for — parmigiana, or more fully melanzane alla parmigiana — is built from fried
aubergine layered with tomato and cheese, not meat at all
<Cite ref="wiki-chickenparm"/>. It took shape among Italian immigrants in
the United States in the early twentieth century, with one of the earliest
known chicken parmesan recipes appearing in the 1940s
<Cite ref="wiki-chickenparm"/>.

The substitution is usually explained by what was on offer in the new country
rather than in the old one. In much of Italy, chicken had historically been a
comparatively expensive meat set aside for special occasions, while American
meat markets sold it cheaply and in quantity — the reverse of the situation
immigrants had left <Cite ref="wiki-chickenparm"/>. The breaded cutlet itself
is usually traced to a separate borrowing, a fusion of the aubergine
parmigiana's tomato-and-cheese construction with the breaded, fried cutlet
style — cotoletta in Italian cooking, schnitzel among the German communities
many Italian immigrants lived alongside <Cite ref="wiki-chickenparm"/>.

The result keeps the shape of its Italian namesake — a fried, breaded piece
finished under tomato and melted cheese — while being built from an
entirely different main ingredient. It belongs to American red-sauce cooking
rather than to Italy, and is treated that way here.
