The dish is built on *shahe fen*, the wide, flat rice noodle of Cantonese
cooking. Beef chow fun is the noodle stir-fried dry, in soy
alone, rather than served wet in a gravy — a distinction the dish's Cantonese
name insists on, since *gon chow ngau ho* means specifically "dry-fried beef
ho fun."

How that dry style came about is told through one particular story rather
than a documented record. In the version usually repeated, a cook named Mr
Hui, forced back to Canton from Hunan during the Japanese occupation of the
Second World War, ran out of the starch needed to thicken chow fun's usual
wet sauce one night while a demanding customer waited under curfew. Rather
than risk the man's anger with no dish at all, Hui stir-fried the noodles dry
instead, and the style is said to have stuck — a story told and retold as
folklore, with no documentary trail back to a real Mr Hui
<Cite ref="wiki-chowfun"/> <Cite ref="chatteris-daipaidong"/>. The dish may
equally have developed gradually across the dai pai dong street stalls that
fed postwar Hong Kong, rather than at any single traceable moment.

Those street stalls, known as dai pai dong, were themselves a product of the
same postwar period: the Hong Kong government began issuing large,
photograph-bearing licenses to displaced families needing a fast way to earn
a living, and the resulting stalls became the setting most associated with
dishes like this one, cooked to order over intense heat from a portable stove
<Cite ref="chatteris-daipaidong"/>. Whatever its precise origin, the dish's
identity now rests entirely on that heat: a wok hot enough to char the
noodles' edges before their moisture has time to steam them soft is what
beef chow fun is judged on, far more than any particular story behind it.
