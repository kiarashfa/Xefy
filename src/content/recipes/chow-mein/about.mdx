*Chow mein* is simply "fried noodles" (*chǎo*, fried, and *miàn*, noodle),
carried into English in the pronunciation of Taishanese-speaking immigrants
from Guangdong, who arrived on America's West Coast in large numbers during
the California Gold Rush <Cite ref="wiki-chowmein"/>
<Cite ref="etymonline-chowmein"/>. The English name is older than it might
seem: it is first recorded in American English in 1890, in a source that
oddly describes it as a Korean dish, a misattribution that says more about
how unfamiliar Asian food still was to English-speaking writers of the period
than about the dish itself <Cite ref="etymonline-chowmein"/>.

The noodles those immigrants cooked with in California were not always the
ones available to them, and the dish changed accordingly. Cooks built chow
mein around cheap, available produce (onion and celery above all), turning a
comparatively light stir-fry into something heavier and more filling, suited
to the caloric needs of labourers on the railroads and in the mines rather
than to any particular regional Chinese template. By the 1920s and 1930s,
chow mein had become what etymologists now call the
archetypal Chinese-American dish, distinct enough from anything cooked in
China that a visitor to Guangdong at the time would not necessarily have
recognised it <Cite ref="etymonline-chowmein"/>.
