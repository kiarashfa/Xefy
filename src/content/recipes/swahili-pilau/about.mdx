Pilau takes its name, and its basic method of browning whole spice and rice
together in one pot, from the Persian *polow*. It reached the Swahili coast
along the same Indian Ocean trade routes that
carried Muslim merchants from Arabia south to East Africa from the early
centuries of the first millennium CE, with Persian settlers known as the
Shirazi arriving from around the twelfth century and marrying into the
Bantu-speaking coastal communities they joined <Cite ref="worldhistory-swahili"/>.

The rice dish that resulted is Swahili rather than a transplant: the coast
already had its own maritime cuisine of fish, coconut and tropical fruit, and
pilau was built into it using whatever whole spice the trade brought through
the port at hand. Zanzibar in particular became the spice source rather than
merely a stop on the route: the Omani ruler Said ibn Sultan, who moved his own
capital to Zanzibar, introduced clove cultivation to the island in the early
nineteenth century, and the trade it created came to dominate Omani commerce
and made Zanzibar and neighbouring Pemba into the world's leading clove
producer <Cite ref="ebsco-said"/><Cite ref="wiki-swahili-coast"/>. Cloves,
along with cinnamon and black pepper grown on the same soil, are what give
Zanzibari pilau its dark colour and its particular warmth, distinct from the
saffron-forward pilafs of the Persian Gulf it descends from.

The Swahili coast's cooking as a whole carries this same layering of Indian
Ocean cultures rather than any single import: Arab influence shows in its
coffee and confectionery, and pilau sits alongside biryani-adjacent dishes as
one of the clearest results of centuries of trade between East Africa,
Arabia and the wider Indian Ocean world <Cite ref="worldhistory-swahili"/>
<Cite ref="wiki-swahili-coast"/>.
