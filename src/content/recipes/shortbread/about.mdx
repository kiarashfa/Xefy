Shortbread did not start as a biscuit. Its origins are traced to medieval
"biscuit bread" — ordinary bread dough left over from baking, sweetened and
dried out a second time into a hard rusk, a practice recorded across the
British Isles from around the twelfth century <Cite ref="bfh-shortbread"/>.
What turned it into shortbread was the gradual replacement of yeast with
butter, which made it rich enough to become an occasional luxury rather than a
way of using up dough — something baked for Christmas, Hogmanay and weddings
rather than every week <Cite ref="bfh-shortbread"/>.

The dish is popularly tied to Mary, Queen of Scots, credited in the mid
sixteenth century with a fondness for a thin, caraway-flavoured round called
petticoat tails, and the association is repeated everywhere shortbread is
written about. It should be treated with some caution: the documentary
evidence connecting her specifically to its origin is thin
<Cite ref="wiki-shortbread"/><Cite ref="bfh-shortbread"/>, and the earliest source the caraway story
usually traces to treats it more as an aside than an established fact
<Cite ref="bfh-shortbread"/>. The name "petticoat tails" itself is unsettled,
variously explained as a corruption of the French *petites galettes*, "little
cakes", or as a reference to the frilled, petticoat-like edges left by cutting
a round into wedges <Cite ref="scotchwhisky-shortbread"/>.

What can be dated with more confidence is the recipe. The first printed
version appeared in 1736, from a Scotswoman named Mrs McLintock
<Cite ref="wiki-shortbread"/><Cite ref="scotchwhisky-shortbread"/>, and eighteenth- and nineteenth-century versions
were considerably more elaborate than today's — an eighteenth-century recipe
attributed to a Mrs Frazer calls for a peck of flour, citrus peel, blanched
almonds and yeast, and an 1854 version from George Read's baking manual adds
egg and lemon essence <Cite ref="bfh-shortbread"/>. The plain three-ingredient
formula treated as classic today — butter, sugar and flour, in that
descending order of weight — is a later simplification of a dough that
started out considerably busier.
