Adana kebab takes its name from Adana, the city in southern Turkey where it is said
to have taken its present form in the bazaars of the old city in the second half of
the nineteenth century <Cite ref="wiki-adana"/>. Before the name settled, it was known
locally simply as *kıyma kebabı* — minced-meat kebab — and closely related versions
are cooked across the belt of former Ottoman territory running from Mersin through
Aleppo to Kirkuk, which points to a shared regional technique rather than a single
point of invention <Cite ref="wiki-adana"/>. One account traces the specific method —
hand-chopped meat worked with lamb tail fat — to Birecik, once part of the Ottoman
Eyalet of Aleppo, describing the kebab as a product of Turkish and Arab culinary
exchange rather than a purely Turkish one <Cite ref="wiki-adana"/>.

The dish now carries a controlled designation of origin, granted in 2005 after a
formal legal process, which sets out standards for how a kebab may be labelled Adana
<Cite ref="wiki-adana"/><Cite ref="kebabs-adana"/>. Among the standards it sets out
are that the meat be chopped by hand with a two-sided cleaver, the *zırh*, rather
than run through a machine, and that lamb tail fat make up a fixed share of the mix
<Cite ref="kebabs-adana"/>. That designation is also the source of a detail at odds with
the kebab's international reputation: the original Adana kebab was not a hot dish at
all <Cite ref="wiki-adana"/>. Maraş pepper gives it its colour and a background warmth
rather than sharp heat, and the assumption that Adana kebab is fiery appears to be a
later development as the dish spread beyond its home city — it reached Istanbul,
Baghdad and Damascus from the 1950s onward, each stop free to adjust the seasoning
<Cite ref="wiki-adana"/>.
