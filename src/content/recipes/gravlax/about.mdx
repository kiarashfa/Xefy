The name means buried salmon: Swedish *grava*, "to dig," and *lax*, "salmon"
<Cite ref="wiki-gravlax"/>. The earliest documented trace of the word is not a
recipe but a nickname, borne by a delegate to a Norwegian salmon fishery and
recorded in the *Diplomatarium Norvegicum* in 1348 <Cite ref="wiki-gravlax"/>.

What that name described in the Middle Ages was not what is eaten today.
Coastal fishermen salted salmon and buried it in the sand above the high-tide
line, where it lightly fermented rather than simply cured <Cite ref="wiki-gravlax"/>.
The technique belonged to a wider Nordic family of buried and fermented fish —
Swedish *surlax*, Norwegian rakfisk among them — developed where a short,
enormous catch met a real scarcity of salt, and producing a soft, strongly
flavoured result far from a modern cured fillet <Cite ref="nordic-food-lab"/>.

Almost none of that survives in the dish sold under the name now. Fermentation
has been dropped entirely: the salmon is no longer buried in the ground but
wrapped in a dry cure of salt, sugar and dill, and left for as little as twelve
hours <Cite ref="wiki-gravlax"/>. Nothing in the record fixes when that change
happened — the sources describe the two methods without dating the shift
between them, so the honest position is that a centuries-old name has been
carried by a considerably younger process for an unknown stretch of that
history.

What survived the change is the logic, not the microbiology: salt drawing
water out of the flesh, and time doing the rest without heat. That is also why
the modern, unfermented version leans on a safeguard of its own — fish that has
already been frozen, since a dry cure alone does nothing to the parasites that
only cooking, or freezing, reliably deals with.
