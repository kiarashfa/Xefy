Hot pot's usual origin story is set at the water's edge rather than in a
kitchen. Along Chongqing's Chaotianmen wharf, one of the busiest river
crossings in the city, boatmen and porters are said to have taken the cheap
offal cuts left over from the butcher — tripe, liver, stomach — and boiled
them in a shared pot of chilli and Sichuan pepper, a broth spiced heavily
enough to disguise the strong flavour of the meat and warm workers through a
damp, cold winter on the river <Cite ref="ichongqing-hotpot"/>
<Cite ref="wiki-hotpot"/>.

The practice moved off the docks and into the city gradually. Street vendors
known as "stick-stick men" carried broth and ingredients through Chongqing's
streets on bamboo poles, selling hot pot to pedestrians directly over open
flames rather than at a fixed stall <Cite ref="ichongqing-hotpot"/>. By the
1920s the trade had settled into Chongqing's Jiangbei district, where
dockworkers bought and boiled beef tripe together, splitting the cost of the
broth and paying only for what they personally ate out of it. A restaurant
is said to have later formalised the dish, turning a workers' meal into
something eaten by choice rather than necessity, though no precise date for
that step is well attested.

What survives from that origin is less the exact cuts of meat than the
broth's whole reason for being so aggressively seasoned: chilli and Sichuan
pepper were practical before they were a matter of taste, covering the flavour
of poor-quality meat and offal and giving workers something to warm them
against a damp climate. A modern pot, built on better cuts and richer stock,
is still seasoned to the same *málà* — numbing and hot — formula that started
as a way of making do.
