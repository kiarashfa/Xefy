Ajiaco santafereño has a documentary history unusually long for a soup:
its ingredients (corn, potato and guascas) are described as Muisca staples
from before the Spanish arrived in the Cundinamarca-Boyacá highlands, and
the dish itself was written down within decades of colonisation. Spanish
soldier Bernardo Vargas Machuca, based in Santafé de Bogotá, recorded its
consumption in his account of the Indies in the mid-sixteenth century, and
a further description survives from 1660, when the friar Joaquín de
Finestrad wrote in *El Vasallo* of ajiaco and corn porridge as part of the
Muisca diet, noting that indigenous cooks kept it meatless while Spanish
colonists added meat to their own <Cite ref="infobae-ajiaco"/>.

That colonial addition is what shaped the dish eaten today: chicken, three
varieties of potato and guascas, finished with cream and capers layered
onto what began as an indigenous dish
<Cite ref="infobae-ajiaco"/> <Cite ref="wikipedia-ajiaco"/>, producing what
is often described as a mestizo dish rather than a purely indigenous or
purely European one. It moved from rural and indigenous kitchens into the
city's wider culinary record over the centuries that followed, appearing
among Bogotá's principal dishes in Timoteo González's 1893 culinary
treatise <Cite ref="infobae-ajiaco"/>.

The dish's local specificity is worth stressing precisely because its name
is shared, confusingly, with unrelated soups elsewhere. Cuban ajiaco is a
beef, pork and chicken stew built around tropical tubers, and Peruvian
ajiaco is a chilli-and-herb dish served with rice. They are different foods that
happen to carry the same word, with only the loosest family resemblance to
Bogotá's potato-and-guascas soup.

Bogotá's own government has since made the dish's local identity official:
in 2004, the city council's Agreement 212 declared ajiaco santafereño part
of the municipal cultural heritage <Cite ref="infobae-ajiaco"/>.
