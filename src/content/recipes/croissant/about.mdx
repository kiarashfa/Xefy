The croissant is one of the most French things a bakery sells, and it began as a crescent made in Austria. Austrian bakers were making the *kipferl* by the thirteenth century, and most food historians date it to that period <Cite ref="foodrepublic-croissant"/>, <Cite ref="vinepair-croissant"/>. The popular story that it was invented in Vienna to celebrate the defeat of the Ottomans in 1683 is told as a story, and the sources find no real evidence for it <Cite ref="foodrepublic-croissant"/>, <Cite ref="vinepair-croissant"/>.

The kipferl came to Paris through an Austrian artillery officer, August Zang, who opened the Boulangerie Viennoise in 1839 <Cite ref="foodrepublic-croissant"/>. He sold Vienna bread and kipferl, and other Paris bakers began making their own. Parisians renamed the pastry the croissant, and the French version was flakier than the Austrian one <Cite ref="vinepair-croissant"/>. The early French versions were brioche-like buns, closer to a sweet bread than to the layered pastry now sold <Cite ref="foodrepublic-croissant"/>.

The laminated croissant, built from many thin layers of butter and dough, came later. The recipe usually credited with establishing it was written in 1915 by the baker Sylvain Claudius Goy <Cite ref="foodrepublic-croissant"/>.

The popular claim that Marie Antoinette brought the pastry to France is a legend. The sources describe it as a common but unsubstantiated story. The queen was killed in 1793, and no reference to croissants in France has been found before the middle of the nineteenth century <Cite ref="vinepair-croissant"/>, <Cite ref="foodrepublic-croissant"/>.
