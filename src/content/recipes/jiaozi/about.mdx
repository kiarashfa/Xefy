Jiaozi are old enough that their history is written in fragments rather than in
one continuous record. The character *jiaozi* appears in the *Guangya*, a
dictionary compiled during the Three Kingdoms period, and a century later the
scholar Yan Zhitui described a dumpling "shaped like a crescent moon" as
already common food across the land <Cite ref="wiki-jiaozi"/>. The
archaeological record agrees with the texts: dumplings recovered from a Tang
dynasty tomb near Turpan, over a thousand years old, are close enough to
today's jiaozi to be identified on sight <Cite ref="wiki-jiaozi"/>.

The dish's popular origin story is a different kind of claim. It credits the
Eastern Han physician Zhang Zhongjing with inventing jiaozi as medicine —
stewing lamb, black pepper and warming herbs, wrapping the mixture in dough
shaped like an ear, and distributing it to treat the frostbitten poor one
winter. The shape is said to be why jiaozi are still eaten at the new year for
luck, and why the fold is meant to resemble an ear as much as it resembles the
crescent moon of the earlier written record. It is a well-loved story and an
unverified one: it rests on tradition rather than on any documentary or
archaeological source, and it sits alongside a written record for the dumpling
itself that predates the Han physician's lifetime by less than the legend
implies <Cite ref="wiki-jiaozi"/>.

What the sources do establish without dispute is jiaozi's role as a food that
travelled. Its most direct descendants carry that history in their names:
Japanese gyoza, adapted from jiaozi recipes brought home after the Second
Sino-Japanese War, and Cantonese guotie, whose pan-fried bottom and steamed top
are treated in the north as a dumpling in their own right and in the south as
simply how jiaozi are cooked when boiling is not the plan. The wrapper, the
fold and the ratio of meat to cabbage travelled outward from the same
long-attested dish; the cooking method did not have to.
