Braising a tough cut of meat slowly in a covered pot is not an American
invention; the technique reaches back to French dishes such as boeuf à la
mode, a marinated, pot-braised beef recorded in François Pierre de la
Varenne's French cookbook as early as 1651 <Cite ref="cooksinfo-alamode"/>.
What became "Yankee pot roast" is that method adapted to New England kitchens
that mostly lacked proper ovens, where a version of the same dish was cooked
over an open hearth in a large covered pot rather than roasted
<Cite ref="cravenhall-hearthcooking"/>.

A separate strand arrived later and independently. Jewish immigrants from
Hungary, Austria and Russia brought their own slow-braised beef dishes, known
in Yiddish as gedempte fleisch, and these traditions folded into the same
category of long-cooked, pot-braised beef once established in American home
cooking <Cite ref="forward-potroast"/>. The dish now called pot roast in the
United States is better understood as several separate European braising
traditions converging on the same technique — meat seared, then simmered
slowly in a covered pot with vegetables and a little liquid until it
surrenders — than as a single dish with one lineage.

That convergence is also why there is no single fixed recipe for it. The
vegetables, the liquid and the seasoning vary by household and by which
tradition a family's pot roast happens to descend from; the searing and the
long, low braise are the only parts everyone agrees on.
