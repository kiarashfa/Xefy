Ramen is a Japanese wheat noodle in a hot broth, and the most accepted account of its
origin is that it grew from wheat-noodle dishes brought to Japan by immigrants from Fujian
and Guangdong in the early twentieth century. Nippon.com says the dish spread in port towns
such as Yokohama and Hakata in Fukuoka, which had long histories of trade with the outside
world <Cite ref="nippon-ramen"/>. Japanese Food Guide traces the route to the opening of
Japan's ports, through which Chinese noodle dishes arrived, and says the dish was once called
*chūka soba*, Chinese soba <Cite ref="jfg-ramen"/>.

The early shops are dated in more than one way. Japanese Food Guide gives 1910 for a
Chinese restaurant in Asakusa, opened by Kanichi Ozaki, which invited twelve Chinese chefs
from Yokohama's Chinatown <Cite ref="jfg-ramen"/>. Nippon.com describes Asakusa's Rairaiken
as considered to be Japan's oldest ramen shop, but gives no founding year for it
<Cite ref="nippon-ramen"/>. Which shop came first is not settled.

After the war, returnees from China sold the dish from market stalls called yatai, according
to Japanese Food Guide <Cite ref="jfg-ramen"/>.

Tonkotsu, the long-boiled pork-bone broth of Hakata and Kurume, is credited by Nippon.com to
the Kurume stall Sankyū in 1947, which let a batch of pork bone steep too long and produced
the broth <Cite ref="nippon-ramen"/>. That single origin is stated without a second source,
and it is reported here as the article's account.
