Moros y Cristianos wears its name as a piece of medieval Spanish history
rather than a description of its ingredients. Arab Muslim rule of the
Iberian Peninsula, which began in the early eighth century, and the
centuries-long Reconquista that ended it in the fifteenth, are what the name
refers to: the black beans standing for the Moors, the white rice for the
Christian Spaniards who eventually retook the peninsula
<Cite ref="wikipedia-moros"/> <Cite ref="marcacanaria-moros"/>.

That naming convention travelled with Spanish colonists to Cuba, where it
was applied to a dish of rice and beans cooked together that the settlers'
descendants continued to make. In eastern Cuba the same basic dish is more
often called congrí, made with red beans rather than black: a regional
difference in the legume rather than in the technique, and one that leaves
moros y cristianos as the name most associated with black beans and the
western half of the island <Cite ref="wikipedia-moros"/>
<Cite ref="marcacanaria-moros"/>. Cuban anthropologist Fernando Ortiz traced
"congrí" itself to Haitian Creole, from *congo* for the bean and the French
*riz* for rice, pointing to a separate African and Haitian root for that
name rather than the Spanish one behind moros y cristianos
<Cite ref="marcacanaria-moros"/>.

Unlike food origin stories built on a single disputed event, moros y
cristianos names something broadly agreed upon: a centuries-old conflict
memorialised, half-seriously, on a dinner plate two continents away.
