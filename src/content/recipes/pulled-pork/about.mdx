North Carolina's barbecue divide is usually described as a rivalry, but the
two styles share a base and diverge only where the finishing sauce is
concerned. In the east, the tradition holds closest to its early roots: a
whole hog cooked over wood and dressed only with vinegar, pepper and salt, a
sauce substituted early on for the lemon juice used to acid-cure meat
elsewhere in the Caribbean-influenced barbecue tradition that reached the
Carolinas by the early nineteenth century <Cite ref="ourstate-bbq"/>. Whole-hog barbecue itself is old enough in the region to predate the
sauce: George Washington's own diary records him attending "a Barbecue" in
Virginia in 1769, and it was not until the early nineteenth century that the
vinegar-and-cayenne finish became the standard <Cite ref="sfa-nc-bbq"/>. Food
writer John Shelton Reed, who has traced the region's barbecue history at
length, calls the eastern style "the original, that's for sure" <Cite ref="ourstate-bbq"/>.

The Piedmont version usually credited to Lexington and Salisbury took shape
later, during the First World War, when barbecue began to be sold from street
stands rather than at the whole-hog pig-pickings of the east
<Cite ref="ourstate-bbq"/>. Cooking a whole hog for a cart-based stand was
impractical, so Piedmont pitmasters — several accounts trace the style to a
handful of cooks of German descent — worked with the pork shoulder alone, and
lightened the vinegar base with a little tomato, producing the sauce known
locally as "dip" <Cite ref="ourstate-bbq"/>. It is thinner and less sweet than
the sauces associated with Kansas City or Memphis, and Reed argues that the
eastern and Piedmont sauces "are much more like each other than either one is
like what you'd find" in those other regions <Cite ref="ourstate-bbq"/>
— a reminder that "Carolina barbecue" is closer to one tradition split in two
than two unrelated cuisines that happen to share a state.

The rivalry has outlasted the sauce pot: North Carolina's first sit-down
barbecue restaurant, Bob Melton's in Rocky Mount, opened serving the eastern
style in 1924, and by the time Lexington pitmaster Wayne Monk was catering
the Reagan administration's 1983 economic summit, both traditions were
established enough that neither had displaced the other
<Cite ref="sfa-nc-bbq"/>. The version made here is the eastern one — a whole
shoulder standing in for the whole hog most home cooks cannot manage,
shredded and dressed in the sharp, tomato-free vinegar sauce that predates
the Piedmont's sweeter dip.
