Shawarma is a name, not a separate invention. It is the Levantine Arabic rendering of
the Turkish *çevirme*, "turning", the same word that gave Greek its *gyros* — both are
regional names for the vertical spit developed in the 19th-century Ottoman Empire and
carried, along with the technique, wherever Ottoman territory reached
<Cite ref="wiki-shawarma"/>. The word is attested in English by 1895, when an
Egyptian Arabic–English dictionary translated it as "rich stew, roast meat"
<Cite ref="wiki-shawarma"/>.

What distinguishes shawarma from döner is where it settled rather than how it began:
Levantine cooks built their own version around lamb or chicken layered with a spice
mix of cumin, cardamom, cinnamon and allspice rather than döner's paprika and oregano,
and it became, and remains, the everyday street food of Damascus, Beirut and the
cities around them.

Shawarma's migration did not stop at the Mediterranean. Beginning in the late 19th
century, tens of thousands of people left Ottoman Lebanon and Syria for Mexico,
escaping conscription and searching for work, and by the 1930s their restaurants were
serving shawarma there too <Cite ref="prx-shawarma"/>. Their Mexican-born children
later adapted it: the food historian Jeffrey Pilcher describes how, in the 1960s,
"they take the technology that they grew up with in these Lebanese restaurants, the
vertical rotisserie — but instead of using lamb, they use pork"
<Cite ref="prx-shawarma"/>, seasoning it with chillies and achiote and serving it on
corn tortillas instead of pita. That dish became tacos al pastor — a taco whose
cooking method is a shawarma spit wearing a different name for the third time.
