Pão de queijo is older than any precise date attached to it, and the two
best-documented accounts of its early history do not fully agree. Both trace
the dish to Minas Gerais during Brazil's colonial gold-mining boom of the
eighteenth century, which drew a huge workforce, enslaved and free, into
the region <Cite ref="wiki-paodequeijo"/><Cite ref="rede98-paodequeijo"/>.
Cassava, already grown in the area before Portuguese arrival, filled the gap
left by wheat, which the region's distance from the coast and its soil made
impractical to grow or import affordably
<Cite ref="wiki-paodequeijo"/><Cite ref="rede98-paodequeijo"/>.

Where the accounts diverge is timing, and specifically cheese. Wikipedia's
history, drawing on the journalist Xavier Graziano's 2014 account in the
newspaper *Estadão*, ties cheese's entry into the recipe to the later,
post-abolition growth of Minas Gerais's dairy industry, well over a century
after the gold rush that started the cassava-starch habit in the first
place. It is one version of the story, offered without a document precise enough
to fully confirm it. A separate account from the
Brazilian broadcaster Rede 98, citing the food researcher Vani Fonseca, dates
the dish closer to 1750 and describes cheese entering the recipe far
earlier, as leftover scraps saved from artisanal cheesemaking on Minas
Gerais farms, folded into polvilho dough on weekends, well before the
timeline the abolition-era story assumes <Cite ref="rede98-paodequeijo"/>.
Neither account cites a document precise enough to settle the question, and
readers should treat pão de queijo's exact date, and the moment cheese
joined the starch, as genuinely unresolved rather than fixed to either
telling.

What both versions agree on is the shape of the story: a cassava-starch
staple, born of a region that could not easily grow wheat, later enriched
with the region's own dairy into the roll eaten today (crisp-shelled, chewy
inside, and leavened by nothing but egg and steam rather than yeast).

The dish's second life, as a commercial product sold well beyond Minas
Gerais, is better dated. The engineer Mário Carneiro founded Casa do Pão de
Queijo in São Paulo in 1967, selling rolls made to his mother Dona
Arthêmia's recipe; the chain's later franchised growth carried the roll into
the rest of Brazil and eventually abroad, where it is now sold as "Brazilian
cheese bread" <Cite ref="bloomberg-paodequeijo"/>.
