The name describes a method rather than one fixed dish: *sinigáng* means
"stewed," from the Tagalog verb *sigáng*, "to stew"
<Cite ref="wiki-sinigang"/> <Cite ref="bestinmanila-sinigang"/>.
Sour broth is built around meat, fish or seafood and whatever vegetables were
to hand. Tamarind now dominates as the souring agent, but the dish draws on a
wide range of other acidic fruits and leaves besides — unripe mango,
calamansi, bilimbi, santol, guava and more, chosen by what is available
<Cite ref="wiki-sinigang"/> <Cite ref="angsarap-sinigang"/>. The food
historian Doreen Fernandez, writing in
1975, argued that this adaptability is the point: a technique built to use
whatever sour fruit is at hand, workable across every class and budget, which
in her view makes sinigang more representative of Filipino taste than adobo
<Cite ref="fernandez-sinigang"/>.

The dish's reputation has outgrown that quiet, undocumented history. In 2021
the food and travel review site TasteAtlas ranked sinigang na baboy, the pork
version, as the world's best-rated soup, ahead of Scottish cullen skink and
Japanese ramen, citing its sourness as a foil for tropical heat
<Cite ref="gma-sinigang"/> <Cite ref="wiki-sinigang"/>. It is now commonly
named, alongside adobo, as one of the dishes most likely to stand in for a
national dish the Philippines has never officially designated.
