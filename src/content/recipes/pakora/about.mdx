Both names are old and both describe the technique rather than any one
ingredient. Pakora comes from the Sanskrit *pakvavaṭa*, combining *pakva*,
cooked, with *vaṭa*, a small lump — literally a cooked lump, fried in oil.
Bhaji comes from a different Sanskrit word, *bharjita*, meaning simply fried
<Cite ref="wiki-pakora"/>. Neither word was ever specific to onion, or to gram
flour, or to any single vegetable; they named a method that could be applied
to more or less anything.

Something recognisable under this method turns up early. Sanskrit and Tamil
Sangam-era literature both mention fried lentil or vegetable cakes in verses
over a thousand years old, though without recipes precise enough to say
exactly what they tasted like <Cite ref="satj-pakora"/>. Actual written
recipes appear later and are considerably more specific: the *Lokopakara*, an
eleventh-century Kannada text, describes gram flour pressed into fish-shaped
moulds and fried in mustard oil, and the twelfth-century *Manasollasa*
describes a preparation called *parika*, made with vegetables and gram flour,
close enough to a modern pakora that the line of descent is not really in
doubt <Cite ref="wiki-pakora"/>.

What has never settled into one fixed form is the filling. Regional versions
differ by what is locally abundant and by household habit rather than by any
canonical recipe — besan-battered onion in Punjab, potato in Gujarat, spinach
and other greens elsewhere, aubergine in Bengal under its own name, beguni
<Cite ref="satj-pakora"/>. The onion version this page describes, sold in
Britain and much of the wider world as onion bhaji, is one branch of that same
very old and very unfixed family rather than the original form of it.
