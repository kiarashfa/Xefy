The word "flapjack" for a griddle cake is recorded in English by the early
seventeenth century, well before it crossed the Atlantic <Cite ref="smithsonian-pancakes"/>.
What the English colonists found in New England, though, was not their own
wheat batter but a corn-based one: the Narragansett and neighbouring peoples
showed early settlers how to grind maize and cook it on a hot stone or griddle,
a technique the colonists adapted into what they called journey cake or
johnnycake <Cite ref="natgeo-pancakes"/>.

Amelia Simmons's 1796 *American Cookery*, generally treated as the first
cookbook written by an American, includes both a johnnycake recipe made with
cornmeal and molasses and a separate "Indian slapjack" leavened with eggs
rather than a rising agent, showing the two traditions — cornmeal griddle bread
and egg-risen batter — already sitting side by side at the end of the
eighteenth century <Cite ref="natgeo-pancakes"/>.

The wheat-flour, chemically leavened pancake familiar today is a later
development, arriving once baking soda and baking powder became household
staples in the nineteenth century. Buttermilk earned its place in the batter
for the same reason it did in biscuits and cornbread: it was the acid a farm
kitchen already had on hand to react against the soda, and it left the
pancake with a characteristic tang that a plain milk batter does not have
<Cite ref="smithsonian-pancakes"/>.
