*Pancit* is a borrowed word before it is a dish: it comes from Hokkien
Chinese, most plausibly from a phrase meaning "something conveniently cooked"
(fast food, in the original sense) carried to the islands by Chinese
immigrant traders and merchants <Cite ref="wiki-pancit"/>
<Cite ref="pepper-pancit"/>. Noodles themselves were their introduction: rice
flour stood in for wheat early on, and the dishes built from it were
nativized quickly, gaining Spanish-era touches as they spread rather than
staying a purely Chinese import.

That spread had an economic engine. Chinese *panciteros* sold noodles as a
fast, portable meal to factory workers during the Spanish colonial period,
whose limited time for cooking made a quick noodle dish attractive; the
roadside stalls that grew out of this trade are described as among the
country's first covered eateries <Cite ref="pepper-pancit"/>. Pancit's
association with long life (noodles left uncut, eaten whole to avoid
"cutting short" a life) comes from the same Chinese lineage and is why
pancit still appears on Filipino birthday tables today
<Cite ref="wiki-pancit"/> <Cite ref="pepper-pancit"/>.

Pancit canton, the stir-fried egg-noodle version most familiar outside the
Philippines, is a puzzle in one specific respect: nothing about it traces to
Canton, the old name for Guangdong province. No source has settled why the
name stuck: the likeliest explanation on offer is that an enterprising
pancitero attached it to suggest an authentically Chinese dish to customers,
but this is speculation rather than a documented account
<Cite ref="wiki-pancit"/> <Cite ref="pepper-pancit"/>.
