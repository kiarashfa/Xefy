Plantains reached the Caribbean with Spanish colonisers, but the knowledge
that turned them into a staple food — and the techniques for cooking
them — travelled with enslaved Africans, who brought both agricultural
expertise and a tradition of making the most of an abundant starch
<Cite ref="atk-tostones"/>. Food historian Von Diaz frames the logic behind
dishes like tostones plainly: faced with an abundance of one ingredient,
cooks find every possible way to use it <Cite ref="atk-tostones"/>.

Even the dish's name is not settled. "Tostón" is documented as the name of
a colonial Spanish silver coin, itself descended from the Italian "testone,"
a coin struck in Milan from 1470 bearing a ruler's head
<Cite ref="mexicodesconocido-toston"/>; the fried plantain disc, roughly
coin-shaped once flattened, is often said to take its name from that
resemblance. A second, more literal candidate ties the word instead to the
Spanish verb *tostar*, "to toast" — a plausible rival that the documented
coin sense does not rule out.

What is not in dispute is the dish's reach. The same twice-fried plantain,
softened by an initial fry, flattened, and crisped by a second, appears
under different names wherever the fruit itself does: tostones in Puerto
Rico and Cuba, bannann peze in Haiti, patacones in Peru and much of northern
South America <Cite ref="atk-tostones"/>. The technique is constant even as
the name, the dipping sauce and the accompaniment change from island to
island and coast to coast.
