Youtiao's two names describe two different things about it. In Mandarin it is
simply *yóutiáo*, "oil strip", a plain description of a stick of dough fried
in oil. In Cantonese it is *yàuh ja gwái*, "oil-fried devil," a name that
carries a story rather than a description <Cite ref="wiki-youtiao"/>
<Cite ref="snackstack-youtiao"/>.

The story is set in the Southern Song dynasty and centres on the general Yue
Fei, a celebrated defender against the invading Jurchens, and the chancellor
Qin Hui, who is said to have had Yue Fei falsely accused of treason and
executed <Cite ref="snackstack-youtiao"/>. According to the legend, popular
anger at the execution found an outlet in a street snack: two strips of dough,
representing Qin Hui and his wife, pressed together and dropped into boiling
oil to be punished in effigy every morning <Cite ref="wiki-youtiao"/>
<Cite ref="snackstack-youtiao"/>. It is a satisfying story and, on the
historical record, a contested one: even Qin Hui's own reputation as a
traitor rather than a pragmatic peacemaker is a matter scholars still argue
over, which makes a specific popular protest against him harder still to pin
to a date or a place <Cite ref="snackstack-youtiao"/>.

Whatever its origin, the shape the legend explains (two strips joined along
one edge before frying) is the same shape that makes the dish work. The
seam is what lets the two pieces puff apart from each other in the hot oil
rather than separately, giving youtiao its characteristic hollow centre and
long, airy crumb. It is eaten today mostly at breakfast, torn and dipped into
congee or soy milk rather than on its own.
