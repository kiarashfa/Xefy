Ohn no khao swè translates plainly as "coconut milk noodles," and the name
is a fair description of the whole dish: egg noodles in a broth thickened
with both coconut milk and gram flour, ladled over the noodles rather than
served on the side. Its early history in Myanmar is thin, though: there
are not many surviving records to fix where or when it began, and local
tradition pointing to Shwedaung, a town near Pyay, is not confirmed by
anything more solid than tradition itself <Cite ref="wiki-ohn-no"/><Cite ref="dailystar-khowsuey"/>.

Its clearer history starts with a Muslim trading community rather than a
kitchen. Memons, a Gujarati Muslim community with a long-standing trading
presence in British-ruled Myanmar, ate and adopted the dish while living
there, some settling as far as Akyab, present-day Sittwe
<Cite ref="dailystar-khowsuey"/>. That presence ended abruptly: after the
military coup of 2 March 1962 brought Ne Win to power, rising hostility
toward Myanmar's Indian-descended communities pushed a large number of
Memons to leave the country for India and Pakistan, many settling in
Karachi <Cite ref="wiki-ohn-no"/><Cite ref="dailystar-khowsuey"/>.

They took the dish with them and changed it as they went. In Pakistan it
became "khow suey," rebuilt around what was available and what suited a
Memon table: spaghetti in place of egg noodles in some households, a
yoghurt-and-gram-flour sauce standing in for coconut milk in others, and
South Asian spicing layered over the Burmese base
<Cite ref="wiki-ohn-no"/><Cite ref="dailystar-khowsuey"/>. Its social role
shifted along with its recipe: from an everyday Rangoon street food to a
centrepiece dish for weddings, Eid and other gatherings in the Memon
community, served with the same array of textural garnishes and left for
each guest to customise their own bowl <Cite ref="dailystar-khowsuey"/>.
