The best-known story behind guotie is a kitchen accident. As it is usually
told, a cook in the Song dynasty (960–1279) let the water boil away from a pan
of ordinary boiled jiaozi, and rather than start again he added more water and
let the dumplings finish with their bottoms already caramelised to the pan.
The dish that resulted — a crisp, glassy-based dumpling with a soft, sealed
top — kept the name of the accident: *guōtiē* is usually glossed as "stuck to
the pot" <Cite ref="vwk-guotie"/>. Nobody claims a documentary source for the
story, and it belongs with the wider family of Chinese food legends that
explain a dish by the mishap that supposedly produced it rather than by any
recorded kitchen.

What is better established is guotie's relationship to jiaozi, and it is not
quite the simple one the accident story implies. In northern China, guotie is
usually treated as a distinct dumpling from jiaozi — smaller, pleated along one
seam rather than sealed all around, and shaped to sit flat rather than stand
upright — while in the south the same word is often used simply to mean
"pan-fried jiaozi," with no distinction intended at all <Cite ref="wiki-jiaozi-guotie"/>.
Both usages are current; neither is a mistake. The English name most cooks
outside China now reach for, "potsticker," has its own recorded starting
point: the writer Buwei Yang Chao introduced it in her 1949 cookbook *How to
Cook and Eat in Chinese*, and it stuck for the same reason the Chinese name did
<Cite ref="wiki-jiaozi-guotie"/>.
