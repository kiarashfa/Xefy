A cooked plate of eggs and cured pork is recorded early: the
seventeenth-century writer Sir Kenelm Digby praised poached eggs with fried
bacon as breakfast fare, and by 1773 the travel writer Patrick Brydone was
contrasting a hearty English breakfast at a lord's house with the lighter
continental version <Cite ref="historyhit-breakfast"/>. But a large cooked
breakfast eaten as a matter of course did not appear in English life
generally until the nineteenth century; before that, breakfast across
Britain was typically bread, porridge or pottage
<Cite ref="wiki-full-breakfast"/><Cite ref="boroughmarket-breakfast"/>.

The meal owes its fixed shape in large part to Victorian country-house
hospitality and to Isabella Beeton, whose *Book of Household Management*
(1861) included fried ham and eggs among its breakfast dishes and helped set
the pattern that spread from the aristocratic estate to the emerging middle
class <Cite ref="wiki-full-breakfast"/><Cite ref="boroughmarket-breakfast"/>. What is now sold as a "full
English" took longer still to assemble: fried potatoes were a post-war
addition, and mushrooms, grilled tomatoes, baked beans and black pudding
are all credited to the 1960s and 1970s <Cite ref="historyhit-breakfast"/>.
The cooked breakfast became a working-class staple only once wartime
rationing had ended and domestic food storage improved enough to make it an
everyday meal rather than an occasional one <Cite ref="wiki-full-breakfast"/><Cite ref="boroughmarket-breakfast"/>.

So the plate now treated as a fixed national institution is a composite,
built up over roughly three centuries and only completed within living
memory. What holds it together is not tradition so much as a working logic:
a frying pan doing several jobs at once, and every element chosen to
survive being cooked well ahead of the egg and held warm beside it.
