Lavash is baked directly against the wall of a *tonir*, a conical clay oven
sunk into the ground: a ball of wheat dough is rolled paper-thin, stretched
over a cushioned pad, and slapped onto the hot inner wall, where it cooks in
well under a minute and is pulled off before it can burn
<Cite ref="unesco-lavash"/>. Made this thin, it keeps for months once dried,
and is torn or rehydrated as needed rather than baked fresh for every meal
<Cite ref="unesco-lavash"/>.

Exactly whose bread it is depends on who is asked. Armenia, Azerbaijan,
Turkey and Iran all lay some claim to it
<Cite ref="wiki-lavash"/><Cite ref="dailysabah-lavash"/>,
and even researchers making the case for Armenian lavash specifically
concede that closely similar thin flatbreads exist across the wider region
and beyond, distinguishing the Armenian version by details of technique —
a deeper *tonir*, dug to suit a fuel-poor terrain — rather than by any claim
to sole invention <Cite ref="horizon-lavash"/>. None of these claims can be
fixed to a single point of origin — thin, clay-oven flatbread of this
general kind appears to be an old, broadly shared solution across the
region rather than the invention of any one kitchen
<Cite ref="wiki-lavash"/><Cite ref="horizon-lavash"/>.

That contest became public in 2014, when Armenia's nomination of lavash to
UNESCO's Intangible Cultural Heritage list, submitted under a title that
called it "traditional Armenian bread," drew a formal objection from
Azerbaijan, which lobbied UNESCO through multiple channels to have the
decision reversed after the fact
<Cite ref="wiki-lavash"/><Cite ref="horizon-lavash"/>. UNESCO inscribed it
that year under the more careful title "an expression of culture in
Armenia" rather than "Armenian bread," and its committee explicitly noted
that lavash "is shared by communities in the region and beyond,"
discouraging language like "unique" or "original," and a joint nomination
from Azerbaijan, Iran, Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan and Turkey covering the wider
regional tradition of flatbread-making followed two years later
<Cite ref="wiki-lavash"/><Cite ref="unesco-lavash"/>.

What is not in dispute is the bread's place in Armenian ritual life
specifically: it is baked communally, largely by women passing the skill
down to younger girls as they help, and a sheet of it is laid across the
shoulders of a newly married couple as a wish for fertility and prosperity
<Cite ref="unesco-lavash"/>.
