Leche flan belongs to a family of caramel-topped custards that Spanish
colonisation carried around the world. It is a denser, sweeter descendant of
the Spanish dish, made with condensed milk and more egg yolk than a
crème caramel takes, using canned milk that reached Filipino kitchens with
American wartime supplies rather than the whole eggs and plain milk of its
European relatives <Cite ref="wiki-cremecaramel"/>
<Cite ref="noseychef-lecheflan"/>.

The dessert's popular origin story is architectural rather than culinary.
Spanish-era builders mixed egg whites with lime and other binders as mortar
for church walls and domes: the sealing of Manila Cathedral's dome in 1780
and expense records for duck eggs at the Bacoor, Cavite church in the 1820s
are both documented instances of the practice
<Cite ref="sbs-eggs"/>. That construction method left enormous quantities of
egg yolks with no stated use, and oral tradition holds that convent and
household cooks began working them into rich custards and candies rather
than discard them, with leche flan, yema and tocino del cielo all credited to
the same surplus <Cite ref="sbs-eggs"/> <Cite ref="wiki-cremecaramel"/>.

That story is repeated constantly and confirmed by nobody with documentary
evidence. The Philippine food historian Felice Prudente Sta. Maria has said
directly that the yolk-surplus account "hasn't yet found written evidence,"
even as she allows that a culinary legend told this consistently may
eventually be borne out <Cite ref="sbs-eggs"/>. The mortar-building itself is
well documented; the leap from surplus yolks to a specific dessert is a
plausible, widely told story rather than a proven one.
