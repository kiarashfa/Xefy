Carrot cake's defining ingredient began as a way around a shortage rather than a virtue in its own right. Sugar was scarce and expensive through much
of the Middle Ages, and bakers who wanted a sweet pudding without it turned to carrots, whose own sugars and moisture could do some of sugar's work
<Cite ref="dailymeal-carrotcake"/>. An English recipe titled "pudding in a Carret root", dated to 1591, already combines carrot with several elements
that survive in the dessert today, though it is closer to a stuffed vegetable dish than to a cake <Cite ref="wiki-carrotcake"/>. The cake form as such
came later, once household ovens made baking rather than boiling or steaming the easier method — the French chef Antoine Beauvilliers published a
"Gâteau de Carottes" in his 1814 cookbook *L'Art du Cuisinier*, popular enough to be copied by rival cooks, and carried it into an English edition of
the same book a decade later <Cite ref="wiki-carrotcake"/><Cite ref="dailymeal-carrotcake"/>.

The cake's second wave of popularity again traces back to a shortage of sugar rather than an appetite for carrots for their own sake. Wartime rationing
in Britain during the Second World War pushed the Ministry of Food to promote carrots as a sweetener, and carrot cake became established as a staple
of British baking in that period <Cite ref="wiki-carrotcake"/>. Its rise in the United States came later still and for a different reason: American
carrot cake spread widely from the 1960s on, helped along by carrots' reputation for healthiness — a reputation the finished cake, layered thick with
sugar and cream cheese icing, does not entirely earn <Cite ref="dailymeal-carrotcake"/>. That icing is itself the American layer on an older dish:
modern British and American recipes both now finish the cake with a white cream cheese icing, though neither source dates precisely when the pairing
became standard rather than one option among several <Cite ref="wiki-carrotcake"/>.
