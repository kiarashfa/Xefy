The plain dosa this dish is built on is old, and exactly how old is argued
between two states. The food historian P. Thankappan Nair points to a
recipe called *dosaka* in the *Manasollasa*, a Sanskrit encyclopedia compiled
around 1130 for the Karnataka king Someshvara III, as evidence for a Karnataka
origin <Cite ref="homegrown-dosa"/>. K. T. Achaya argued instead that dosa, as
*dosai*, belonged to Tamil culture as far back as the first century AD, citing
its presence in early Tamil Sangam-era writing <Cite ref="homegrown-dosa"/>.
Neither claim is settled, and both states still consider it theirs.

The potato-filled masala version is a different matter, and its history is
much better anchored. One popular story credits Someshvara III's own cooks
with inventing it, which cannot be literally true: potatoes did not reach
India until Portuguese traders brought them in the sixteenth century, several
hundred years after his reign <Cite ref="wiki-masala-dosa"/>. The version
actually eaten today is generally credited to K. Krishna Rao, a restaurateur
who popularised Udupi-style cooking in 1930s Madras through his Sri Krishna
Vilas and New Woodlands hotels — a dish with a documented commercial birth
barely a century old, dressed in a much older dosa's reputation
<Cite ref="wiki-masala-dosa"/>.

Mysore's contribution came later still: spreading a fried red chilli-and-garlic
chutney directly onto the dosa before the potato goes in, which is the whole
difference between a plain masala dosa and a Mysore one. The chutney seasons
the crepe itself rather than sitting alongside it, which is why it goes on
first, closest to the griddled surface, rather than on top of the potato.
