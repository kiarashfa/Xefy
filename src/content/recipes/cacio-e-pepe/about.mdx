Cacio e pepe is cheese and pepper, and the phrase is dialect for exactly the two
things in it <Cite ref="wiki-cacio"/>. Pasta, salt and the water the pasta cooked in
are the whole of the rest.

The received account gives the dish to the shepherds of Lazio, Abruzzo, Tuscany and
Umbria, moving flocks between pastures with hard sheep's cheese, dried pasta and
pepper in their bags <Cite ref="wiki-cacio"/>. It is a good story and the pepper is
the hole in it: black pepper was never a cheap commodity, and dried pasta became an
everyday staple far more recently than the story needs it to have
<Cite ref="wiki-cacio"/><Cite ref="lat-2021"/>. The likelier reading is that pepper
joined an older and plainer cheese-and-pasta dish long after the fact.

The written record shows it Roman and domestic well before it was famous. Ada Boni's
*La cucina romana* of 1929 already lists spaghetti a cacio e pepe, and remarks that
its piquancy was valued for the wine it encouraged <Cite ref="boni-1929"/>. Its
career as a restaurant dish is much younger — placed by one account in the osterie of
Rome in the 1950s and 1960s, and credited by another to the Slow Food movement of the
late 1980s <Cite ref="lat-2021"/>.

Its reputation for being harder than it looks is deserved, and has now been measured.
A group of physicists published a phase diagram of the sauce in *Physics of Fluids* in
2025 and found that everything turns on starch: below roughly one per cent of starch
relative to the mass of the cheese, the proteins aggregate on heating into what the
authors named the Mozzarella Phase — clots of cheese suspended in water rather than a
sauce <Cite ref="pof-2025"/>. The paper was given an Ig Nobel Prize the same year
<Cite ref="ista-2025"/>. It is an unusually exact account of why the traditional
instruction is the instruction: the cooking water, never fresh water, and never the
flame.
