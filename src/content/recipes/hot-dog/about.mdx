The sausage predates the sandwich by centuries and its birthplace is itself
disputed between two German-speaking cities: Frankfurt, which gives the
frankfurter its name, and Vienna, source of the alternative name wiener
<Cite ref="npr-hotdogorigin"/>. German immigrants brought it to New York in the
mid-nineteenth century, where street vendors sold it as a "dachshund sausage"
<Cite ref="smithsonian-coneydog"/>.

Its American identity was made at Coney Island. Charles Feltman is widely
credited with putting the sausage in a milk roll and selling it from a
Coney Island stand from the 1870s, and the format spread from there
<Cite ref="smithsonian-coneydog"/>. The best-known rival stand exists because of
a defection rather than an independent invention: Nathan Handwerker, once one of
Feltman's own employees, opened Nathan's Famous nearby in 1916, and the two
stands' competing claims to the original Coney Island hot dog have never
really been settled <Cite ref="smithsonian-coneydog"/>.

What travelled out from Coney Island split into regional dressings rather than
a single standard — mustard and onion in New York, chili in the Midwest, a
version wrapped in a sweeter bun and known by the Coney name itself in
Michigan — and each region treats its own version as the correct one. The
frankfurter and the bun are the constant; everything on top of them is a local
argument.
