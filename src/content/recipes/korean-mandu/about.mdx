The earliest documented mention of a dumpling in Korea sits in the *Goryeosa*,
the official history of the Goryeo dynasty, in an anecdote about a naturalised
Khitan man who mixed meat with other ingredients to make dumplings for his
sick father, recorded under the reign of King Myeongjong in the twelfth
century <Cite ref="wiki-mandu"/>. The dish most people mean by mandu today,
however, is usually traced to a later and more specific moment: the Mongol
domination of the Goryeo court in the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries,
when Mongol influence and intermarriage with Korean royalty are credited with
relaxing Buddhism's restriction on eating meat and bringing meat-filled
dumplings into everyday cooking <Cite ref="wiki-mandu"/><Cite ref="tkf-mandu"/>.

That account is not the only one on offer. Dumplings and noodles built on
wheat dough are argued to trace back much further, to Mesopotamia, spreading
east over centuries along the Silk Road and leaving related dumplings —
mandu's own cousins include Turkish mantı and Central Asian manti — across a
wide belt of Asia well before the Mongol period specifically <Cite ref="wiki-mandu"/>.
A Goryeo-era folk song called *Ssanghwajeom*, "the dumpling shop," which
describes a stall run by a foreigner thought to be Central Asian, is read by
some as evidence that dumpling vendors were already established in Korea
independent of the Mongol court connection <Cite ref="wiki-mandu"/>. Which
account explains mandu's arrival is not settled.

By the Joseon dynasty, mandu had spread out into regional and seasonal forms
rather than staying a single dish: kimchi mandu work fermented cabbage into
the pork-and-tofu filling, wang mandu are enlarged into a bun-like snack
favoured in the north, and mandu-guk, dumplings simmered in a clear beef
broth, became the customary dish for the Lunar New Year, its dumplings
traditionally shaped to recall old brass coins <Cite ref="tkf-mandu"/>.
