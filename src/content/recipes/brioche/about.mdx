Brioche is an enriched bread. Its dough carries a large amount of butter and egg, which is what separates it from a plain loaf: the crumb is soft and fine, the crust colours quickly, and the bread stays tender for a day or two after baking. Because the butter is worked into the dough a little at a time, the dough is slack for a long while before it turns elastic, and it is chilled before it is shaped so that the butter firms enough to be handled.

The word is much older than most of the bread it names. Its first recorded use in French is from 1404, in a Rouen record that lists "deux pains, quatre brioches" (two loaves, four brioches) <Cite ref="cnrtl-brioche"/>, <Cite ref="wiki-brioche"/>. The dictionary gives its formation as the stem of *brier* with the suffix *-oche* <Cite ref="cnrtl-brioche"/>.

Two shapes dominate. The brioche à tête is baked in a fluted round tin, with a large ball of dough topped by a smaller one that forms the head. The brioche de Nanterre is baked in a plain loaf tin, with small pieces of dough set side by side so that they fuse as they rise and show a pattern on the crust. The two are made from the same dough. The difference lies in the mould and the shaping, and that difference is what this page records as its two versions.

Brioche belongs with the other rich doughs of French baking. A pastry chef will often treat it as a bread and a pâtisserie at once: it is served with coffee or chocolate as readily as it is toasted, and it is at its best within a day of baking.
