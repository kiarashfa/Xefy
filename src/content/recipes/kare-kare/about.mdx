The name is the more settled part: *kare-kare* is generally traced to
*kari*, the Malay word for curry, adapted into Filipino usage
<Cite ref="wiki-karekare"/> <Cite ref="scholastic-karekare"/>. The dish
itself contains none of the spices a
curry would suggest, drawing its colour from annatto and its body from ground
peanuts and toasted rice.

Where the dish came from is genuinely unsettled. The account repeated most
often makes it a Kapampangan invention, from cooks of Pampanga said to have
run the first eateries serving *kari* in Manila; a rival account credits the
South Indian sepoys brought by the British during their occupation of Manila
in 1762–1764, some of whom stayed on after the British withdrew and sold
curry from what became *carinderias* <Cite ref="wiki-karekare"/>
<Cite ref="stylish-karekare"/>.

The culinary historian Ruston Banal — himself from Pampanga — set out to
confirm the Kapampangan claim and came back instead with the sepoy account,
presenting it at the 2024 KainCon culinary-heritage conference
<Cite ref="stylish-karekare"/> <Cite ref="wiki-karekare"/>. Neither story is
settled by documentary evidence, and the dish is better described as
unresolved than as definitively Kapampangan.

What is not disputed is the dish's shape: oxtail — often alongside tripe or
pork hock — simmered with aubergine, long beans and other vegetables in a
peanut sauce coloured with annatto, and served with a side of sautéed shrimp
paste to cut the sauce's richness.
