Idli has one of the older food-word trails in South Indian writing. The
tenth-century Kannada text *Vaddaradhane* describes "iddalige" made only from a
black gram batter, and the eleventh-century *Lokopakara* of Chavundaraya II
gives a preparation of black gram soaked in buttermilk, ground to a paste and
mixed with the clear water of curd and spices <Cite ref="wiki-idli"/>. A recipe
for something called *iḍḍarikā* appears again in the *Manasollasa*, a
twelfth-century encyclopedia compiled for the Western Chalukya king
Someshwara III <Cite ref="wiki-idli"/>.

None of these early versions is quite the dish made today. They leave out rice,
the long overnight fermentation, and steaming — the three things that define
modern idli — and texts describing the complete recipe do not appear until
after the thirteenth century <Cite ref="wiki-idli"/>. What changed in between is
disputed. The food historian K. T. Achaya proposed that the steamed, fermented
idli was developed in Indonesia, where fermented foods have a long history, and
carried back to India by cooks attached to Hindu courts trading with the
archipelago between the ninth and thirteenth centuries; other historians argue
that fermentation was just as plausibly worked out independently in India,
since nearly every food culture arrives at it in some form <Cite ref="wiki-idli"/>.
Tamil Nadu and Karnataka both claim the dish as their own, and the argument is
old enough that it predates any surviving record precise enough to settle it
<Cite ref="swiggy-idli"/>.

What is not in question is the technique, which has stayed remarkably stable
once it appears fully formed: rice and dehusked black gram, ground separately,
combined, and left to rise on their own wild fermentation rather than any added
raising agent, then steamed in small moulds. It remains a genuinely
lentil-heavy batter next to its griddled relative dosa, which is part of why it
steams into a cake rather than spreading into a pancake.
