Daifuku is a soft mochi wrapped round a filling of sweet red bean paste. Its ancestor is *uzura mochi*,
a quail-shaped rice cake that was popular in the early Edo period (1603–1867) <Cite ref="jocsk-daifuku"/>.
Food in Japan calls quail mochi the ancestor, named for its plump oval shape, and says that commercial
sale began in the Edo period <Cite ref="fij-daifuku"/>.

The early filling was salted bean paste rather than sweet anko. Food in Japan says that sugar became
affordable only in the Edo period, and that sweetened paste gradually replaced the salty kind
<Cite ref="fij-daifuku"/>. Just One Cookbook says that salted red bean was still a common filling in the
Meiji period (1868–1912), because sugar was a luxury <Cite ref="jocsk-daifuku"/>.

The smaller, sugar-filled version is credited to a particular person in Koishikawa, in Edo, and the
sources give different accounts. Food in Japan names a woman called Otama, living around Koishikawa in
what is now Bunkyō Ward, whose smaller sugar-filled version is commonly dated to 1771. It treats those
details as tradition rather than settled fact <Cite ref="fij-daifuku"/>. Just One Cookbook instead gives
a widow in Koishikawa who, it is said, made the cake smaller and added sugar to the red bean paste in
1875 <Cite ref="jocsk-daifuku"/>.

The name changed to suit the cake. The early version was called *habotai mochi*, a thick-bellied rice
cake <Cite ref="jocsk-daifuku"/>. *Fuku* can mean both belly and luck, and the name was later changed to
*daifuku mochi*, meaning great luck rice cake, with the more auspicious character for luck
<Cite ref="fij-daifuku"/>.

Daifuku was sold on the streets of Tokyo from the Edo period. Mochi hardens as it cools, so vendors sold
it warm, often grilled over braziers. Food in Japan says that grilled daifuku became a street-food
craze in the Kansei era, in the 1790s <Cite ref="fij-daifuku"/>.
