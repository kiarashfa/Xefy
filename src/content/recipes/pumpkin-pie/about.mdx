No pumpkin pie was served at the 1621 harvest gathering now remembered as the
first Thanksgiving. The two surviving written accounts of that meal list
Indian corn, waterfowl, venison and a range of vegetables, but no pumpkin
dessert of any kind <Cite ref="history-pumpkinpie"/>. What the earliest
colonists did do with pumpkins and other winter squash was stew them into a
thick, sweetened porridge, or occasionally fill a hollowed pumpkin shell with
milk and spices and bake it directly in hot ashes — a pudding, not yet a pie
in a crust.

The pie as a category took root over the following century. Amelia Simmons's
*American Cookery*, published in 1796 and widely regarded as the first
cookbook written and published by an American, remained in print for 35 years
and is believed to be the first cookbook to include a precursor to pumpkin
pie <Cite ref="nyalmanack-americancookery"/>: two recipes for "pompkin"
pudding baked in a crust, made with stewed pumpkin, eggs, cream or milk,
sugar and spices — the direct ancestor of the pie eaten today
<Cite ref="wiki-americancookery"/>. Pumpkin desserts had already found a place
at New England's harvest table by then in less formal ways: Colchester,
Connecticut is said to have postponed its Thanksgiving observance by a week
in 1705 because the town lacked the molasses needed to make one
<Cite ref="history-pumpkinpie"/>.

The pie's status as the holiday's default dessert nationwide is thus a
nineteenth-century consolidation of a much older colonial habit, not a fixture
of the meal it is now nostalgically attached to.
