Congee's Chinese name, *zhou*, is attested in writing under the Zhou dynasty,
where the *Book of Rites* lists rice porridge among everyday foods and
recommends it particularly for the elderly and the sick
<Cite ref="eatshistory-congee"/> <Cite ref="wiki-congee"/>.

Long before it was an everyday breakfast, congee was treated as medicine. The
Han dynasty physician Zhang Zhongjing, later called the father of Chinese
clinical medicine, included rice porridge in his medical writing as a way to
warm the stomach and help patients recover their strength after illness
<Cite ref="eatshistory-congee"/>. It served a more practical role too: a
small quantity of rice, stretched with a large volume of water, could feed far
more people than the same rice cooked whole, which made congee a standard
resort in famine and wartime as well as in the sickroom.

The plain porridge is a base as much as a dish in its own right, and the range
of things cooked into or spooned over it reflects that. Century egg and pork
is the best-known topped version today, particularly in Cantonese cooking,
where it is known as *pei dan sau yuk juk*.
