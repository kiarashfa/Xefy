The most repeated origin story credits Saint Hyacinth of Poland with bringing
pierogi home after a visit to Kievan Rus' in the thirteenth century, in one
telling teaching famine-struck Poles to make the dish from whatever they had
to hand <Cite ref="wiki-pierogi"/><Cite ref="delicious-poland-pierogi"/>.
Poland's first documented mention of pierogi does date to the thirteenth
century, but the saint's role in it is treated as legend rather than
established fact — the dish's own name argues against a single dramatic
introduction, since it derives from the Proto-Slavic root *pirŭ*, "feast",
a root also behind the Russian *pirog* and *pirozhki*, which points to a
history shared across the Slavic-speaking world rather than one particular
saint's journey <Cite ref="wiki-pierogi"/>.

Where the dumpling form itself came from before that is less settled still.
One theory has it arriving with Marco Polo's Silk Road contact with Chinese
dumplings; another credits Turkic cooking, pointing to düş-vara as a parallel
dish, and a more recent hypothesis proposes the Turkic word *bureg* as the
actual root of "pierogi" rather than the Slavic feast-word <Cite ref="wiki-pierogi"/>.
None of these accounts is treated as conclusive.

The name of the default filling here is its own piece of etymology, and one
regularly misread: "pierogi ruskie" means Ruthenian pierogi, after the East
Slavic Ruthenian lands that now sit across parts of Ukraine, Belarus and
south-eastern Poland — not Russian, despite how the word looks to an English
speaker <Cite ref="wiki-pierogi"/>. It is this potato-and-farmer's-cheese
filling, rather than the meat or cabbage versions, that is generally treated
as the classic pierogi <Cite ref="delicious-poland-pierogi"/>.
