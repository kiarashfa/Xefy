The earliest written trace of lahmacun is a 13th-century Aleppo cookbook, *Kitab
al-Wusla ila al-Habib*, which instructs a cook to "cut up meat small, then spread it
on a round piece of dough and bake it in the oven" — recognisably the dish eaten
today <Cite ref="wiki-lahmacun"/>. The Ottoman traveller Evliya Çelebi later recorded
a dish called *lahm-ı acîn* among the foods of Damascus in his 17th-century
*Seyahatname*, which the linguist Sevan Nişanyan identifies as the earliest known use
of the word in Turkish <Cite ref="wiki-lahmacun"/>. The name itself is Arabic: *laḥm
bi ʿajīn*, "meat with dough."

Who cooked it in Aleppo is where the record runs out and the dispute begins. Turkey's
claim centres on Gaziantep and Şanlıurfa, cities in the country's southeast, and
Gaziantep lahmacun was registered as a protected EU geographical indication in 2025
<Cite ref="wiki-lahmacun"/>. But Urfa and Gaziantep both held substantial Armenian
communities until the end of the 19th century, and Urfa's Armenian quarter was
destroyed, along with the people who lived there, in 1915 — which makes any claim
about who was cooking what there before that date very hard to settle either way
<Cite ref="armkitchen-lahmacun"/>. What is documented is that lahmajoun reached
present-day Armenia only in the 1960s, brought by Aleppo-Armenian repatriates, which
argues for a shared Aleppo-region dish carried onward by both communities rather than
an invention either can claim outright <Cite ref="armkitchen-lahmacun"/>.

Within Turkey itself, the dish stayed regional for centuries: it was essentially
unknown in Istanbul until the middle of the 20th century, remaining a specialty of
Arab-influenced southern cities before spreading nationwide after the 1950s
<Cite ref="wiki-lahmacun"/>.
