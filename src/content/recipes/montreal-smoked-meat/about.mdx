Montreal smoked meat descends from a Romanian Jewish curing and smoking
tradition, carried to the city by immigrant families who settled, from the
close of the nineteenth century onward, along St Lawrence Street (known
locally as The Main) after fleeing rising antisemitism in Eastern Europe
<Cite ref="qahn-schwartz"/>. The city's most famous version of the dish
belongs to the delicatessen opened by Reuben Schwartz, a Romanian immigrant,
on The Main on the thirty-first of December 1928 <Cite ref="qahn-schwartz"/>.

Schwartz's is rarely credited as the first, however, and the question of who
actually was is genuinely unsettled. Benjamin Kravitz, a Lithuanian
immigrant, opened the rival deli Bens around 1908 after customer demand for
his mother's smoked meat recipe outgrew the fruit and candy store he had
started with, and even a museum retrospective of Bens is careful to say he
was "certainly one of the most successful" at the dish rather than its
inventor, explicitly calling him "not the first"
<Cite ref="mccord-bens"/><Cite ref="wiki-schwartz"/>. Several delis operated
in the same few blocks through the twentieth century (Schwartz's and Bens
chief among a list that also included Reuben's, Dunn's, Lester's, Snowdon
Deli and Main), and rival claims to the superior sandwich remain a live
argument among Montrealers rather than a settled matter of record
<Cite ref="mccord-bens"/><Cite ref="wiki-schwartz"/>.

What is better documented is the method that has kept Schwartz's version
distinct: a whole brisket cured for ten to fourteen days, then hung and
smoked in a brick smokehouse for at least a further day before being
hand-sliced without mechanical tenderising or added preservatives
<Cite ref="qahn-schwartz"/>. Bens itself, once a business large enough to
serve eight thousand customers a day at its 1960s peak, closed for good in
December 2006, after a summer strike over pay and conditions and years of
pressure from rising downtown rents and chain-restaurant competition
<Cite ref="mccord-bens"/>, a reminder that a deli's fame is no guarantee of
its survival, whatever claim it made to having started the tradition in the
first place.
