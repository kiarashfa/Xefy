Xiaolongbao's name is straightforward — "small basket bun", for the bamboo
steamers it is cooked and served in — but the dumpling's origin is told as two
separate, competing stories, and neither is treated as settled fact by the
sources here. The better-known version credits Huang Mingxian, who ran a
restaurant called Ri Hua Xuan in Nanxiang, near Shanghai, with inventing the
dish in the 1870s by folding aspic into pork mince: as the dumpling steamed,
the aspic melted and filled it with soup <Cite ref="scmp-xlb-legends"/>. A
second story sets the dumpling's fame further west, in Wuxi, where the
Qianlong Emperor is said to have tasted a local version during his travels and
praised it so warmly that some accounts trace the "long" in xiaolongbao to
"dragon" — a reference to the emperor's own nickname — rather than to the
steaming basket <Cite ref="scmp-xlb-legends"/>. Wikipedia's account adds a
third strand, tracing a Changzhou lineage back further still, to the Northern
Song dynasty's *guantangbao*, a soup-filled bun made in Kaifeng
<Cite ref="wiki-xlb"/>.

What both principal legends and the Changzhou lineage agree on is the
mechanism, which is the part of the dish that actually matters at the stove:
solid aspic, made by chilling a gelatine-rich stock until firm, is diced and
folded into the raw filling exactly like any other ingredient. Only under the
steamer's heat does it melt back into liquid, so the soup inside the dumpling
was never poured in — it was pleated in solid and cooked into being
<Cite ref="wiki-xlb"/>. Before refrigeration, that trick depended on the
season: making aspic reliably required weather cold enough to set a stock,
which is part of why the technique is remembered as a patient, hours-long
process rather than a quick one <Cite ref="scmp-xlb-legends"/>.
