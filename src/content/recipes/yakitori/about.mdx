The word *yakitori* is old, but what it named at first was not quite today's dish.
It appears in *Ryori Monogatari*, published in 1643 and generally reckoned Japan's
oldest surviving cookbook, though the food it describes under that name was most
likely grilled poultry without a skewer at all — a related but separate term,
*kushiyaki*, covered skewered grilling in that period
<Cite ref="shungate-yakitori"/>. A closer ancestor of the modern skewer turns up
later, in the 1785 text *Manpo Ryori Himitsubako*, under the name *Nagasaki
Toridengaku* <Cite ref="shungate-yakitori"/>.

Chicken specifically, and the street-stall format recognisable today, arrived later
still. Cultural resistance to eating meat had kept poultry a marginal food in Japan
for centuries, and yakitori's popularisation followed the Meiji Restoration, when
that resistance eased and inexpensive off-cuts and offal from newly common poultry
slaughter gave stallholders cheap, quick-cooking food to sell <Cite ref="shungate-yakitori"/>.
The dish kept growing through hardship rather than plenty: it is described as
becoming an everyday food after the 1923 Great Kanto Earthquake, when offal became a
valued source of protein, and again after the Second World War, when yakitori stalls
proliferated in the black markets around stations such as Shinbashi and Shinjuku
<Cite ref="shungate-yakitori"/>. Chicken did not become the standard yakitori meat
until the 1960s, once mass-produced broiler chickens made it the cheapest bird on
the market <Cite ref="shungate-yakitori"/>. The two seasoning styles served today —
*tare*, the sweet soy glaze, and *shio*, salt alone — are both sold side by side at
the same stalls, with the choice between them left to the customer rather than fixed
by the dish itself <Cite ref="wiki-yakitori"/>.
