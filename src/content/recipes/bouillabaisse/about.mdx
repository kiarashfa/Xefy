Bouillabaisse is a fish soup from Marseille, and it is usually said to have begun as a meal for fishermen, who cooked the bony rockfish they could not sell <Cite ref="ng-bouillabaisse"/>, <Cite ref="cyp-bouillabaisse"/>. The name combines the Provençal verbs for boiling and for lowering the heat to simmer <Cite ref="wiki-bouillabaisse"/>, <Cite ref="ng-bouillabaisse"/>.

The origin is not settled. One account has fishermen inventing it at sea by boiling part of their catch in seawater, and another credits fishmongers with using up unsold fish <Cite ref="ng-bouillabaisse"/>.

In 1980 a group of Marseille restaurants drew up a charter setting out the ingredients and method <Cite ref="ng-bouillabaisse"/>, <Cite ref="cyp-bouillabaisse"/>. National Geographic Traveller says it set a minimum of four kinds of fish <Cite ref="ng-bouillabaisse"/>.

The history and the rules are both contested. National Geographic Traveller calls the history "a matter of fierce debate, even among the Marseillais", and Cyprus Mail says the rules for an authentic bouillabaisse are "fiercely debated" <Cite ref="ng-bouillabaisse"/>, <Cite ref="cyp-bouillabaisse"/>.

Saffron is always included, according to National Geographic Traveller <Cite ref="ng-bouillabaisse"/>, and Cyprus Mail says traders from the Middle East introduced it <Cite ref="cyp-bouillabaisse"/>. Tomatoes and potatoes arrived later, through the Columbian Exchange <Cite ref="cyp-bouillabaisse"/>.

The rouille served with it began, according to National Geographic Traveller, as a sauce of pounded monkfish liver, garlic, oil and tomato, and later became a mayonnaise-like emulsion made with potato <Cite ref="ng-bouillabaisse"/>. Cyprus Mail describes it as a garlicky, paprika-spiced sauce served with toasted bread under the broth <Cite ref="cyp-bouillabaisse"/>.
