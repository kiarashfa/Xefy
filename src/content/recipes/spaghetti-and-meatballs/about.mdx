Spaghetti and meatballs was built in the United States by Italian immigrants,
most of whom arrived between 1880 and 1920, rather than carried over from
Italy as a finished dish <Cite ref="gambero-spaghettimeatballs"/>. Meat had
been a rare, occasional food for many of these families before they left;
American meat markets sold it far more cheaply and in far greater quantity,
and that new abundance is what put meatballs on the table regularly enough to
become a habit <Cite ref="gambero-spaghettimeatballs"/>.

The combination itself would look unusual in most of Italy. Italian meals are
conventionally structured around a primo, a plate of pasta treated as a
starter rather than a main course, with meat or fish served separately as a
secondo — the dish as served here, a large bowl of spaghetti carrying a
generous helping of meatballs as the meal's centre, is not a plate that Italy
itself produced <Cite ref="smithsonian-spaghettimeatballs"/>. It reflects an
American portion size and an American idea of what a main course looks like
more than it reflects Italian practice <Cite ref="gambero-spaghettimeatballs"/>.

None of which makes it any less a real dish with its own history. It belongs
fully to Italian-American cooking, built by people working with Italian
techniques and American ingredients and quantities, and it is treated as its
own dish here rather than as a corruption of something it was never trying to
be.
