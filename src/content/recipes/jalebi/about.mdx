Jalebi is a later stop on a much older sweet's journey. The earliest known
recipe for the fried, syrup-soaked coil appears in a tenth-century Arabic
cookbook, described there as latticed fritters dipped in syrup scented with
rose water, musk or camphor <Cite ref="wiki-jalebi"/><Cite ref="betterindia-jalebi"/>. The
name itself is inherited rather than coined in India: the *Hobson-Jobson*
dictionary of Anglo-Indian terms traces *jalebi* to the Arabic *zulabiya* or
the Persian *zolbiya* <Cite ref="wiki-jalebi"/><Cite ref="betterindia-jalebi"/>.

The dish reached India along the same routes as much of the era's Persian
and Central Asian cooking, carried by Turkish and Persian traders and
artisans, and took on its Indian name once it arrived
<Cite ref="betterindia-jalebi"/>. Its presence there is unusually well dated
for a medieval sweet: a Jain text of around 1450 lists jalebi on a
merchant's dinner menu, and a Sanskrit text from before 1600 sets out a
recipe close enough to the modern one that food historians treat it as
essentially the same dish <Cite ref="wiki-jalebi"/><Cite ref="betterindia-jalebi"/>.

What travelled was a technique and a name more than a fixed recipe. Once in
India, jalebi diverged by region rather than staying uniform: thicker and
chewier in some states, thinner and crisper in others, sold plain or stuffed
elsewhere in the world under names as different as *zlabia* in North Africa
and *jerry* in Nepal <Cite ref="wiki-jalebi"/><Cite ref="betterindia-jalebi"/>.
The fermented batter and the hot-oil coil are the constant; almost
everything else about how a given region eats it was worked out locally,
long after the sweet itself had arrived.
