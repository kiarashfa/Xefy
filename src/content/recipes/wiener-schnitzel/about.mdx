The most repeated story about Wiener Schnitzel credits Field Marshal Joseph
Radetzky with bringing the recipe back to Vienna from Habsburg territory in
Italy in 1857, supposedly noting a "deliciously breaded veal cutlet" in a
report to the emperor <Cite ref="natgeo-ws"/><Cite ref="wiki-ws"/>. It is a
tidy story about a real historical figure, and Austria's own federal
ministry treats it as implausible: breaded cutlets, *panierte Speisen*, were
already an established part of Viennese cooking well before Radetzky's time,
with references reaching back to 1798, and the specific term "Wiener
Schnitzel" is recorded in a cookbook from 1831, decades before the
1857 date the legend depends on
<Cite ref="bmluk-ws"/><Cite ref="wiki-ws"/>.

What the dish does have is a legally settled identity, which is more than
most historic recipes can claim. Austrian food law defines Wiener Schnitzel
as consisting only of veal; a version made from pork must be labelled
"Wiener Schnitzel vom Schwein" or "Schnitzel Wiener Art" so that a customer
is never sold the cheaper meat under the protected name
<Cite ref="bmluk-ws"/><Cite ref="wiki-ws"/>. That legal precision sits oddly
next to how loosely the dish's own history is usually told: a popular
legend about a field marshal and an Italian campaign has done more to shape
its reputation than the eighteenth- and nineteenth-century Viennese kitchens
that actually produced it.
