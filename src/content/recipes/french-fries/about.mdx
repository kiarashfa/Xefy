The popular Belgian version of this story holds that villagers along the River
Meuse were already frying sliced potatoes in the winter of 1680, when the
river froze and their usual small fried fish were unavailable. The story does
not survive contact with the potato's own history: researchers have pointed
out that potatoes were not yet grown in that part of the Meuse valley in 1680,
arriving only decades later, around 1735, which would make a potato dish from
1680 there impossible <Cite ref="historycoop-fries"/>.

A rival claim, argued in detail by the Belgian food historian Pierre
Leclercq, places the dish instead among the street vendors of Paris's Pont
Neuf bridge, who Leclercq traces to the 1780s selling sliced fried potatoes —
the "pomme Pont-Neuf" — as a portable street food, with deep-frying becoming
established there by around 1800 <Cite ref="uliege-frenchfries"/>. Leclercq
has also revisited the manuscript behind the Belgian story directly: the 1781
document once read as describing potatoes deep-fried "in the shape of small
fish" instead describes thin slices pan-fried in a little fat, a different
and much older technique that is not the dish in question
<Cite ref="uliege-frenchfries"/>. Neither claim has the kind of unambiguous
documentary evidence that would close the question outright, and the dispute
between French and Belgian historians and officials continues without a clear
resolution.

The English name adds a separate confusion rather than settling anything.
American soldiers stationed in French-speaking Belgium during the First World
War are generally credited with the name, assuming the fried potatoes they
were served there were French because French was the language they heard
<Cite ref="historycoop-fries"/>. The story is complicated by the fact that
"French fried potatoes" already appears in American cookbooks and magazines
in the 1890s, before that war — so the wartime soldiers may have popularised
a name that already existed rather than coined it outright
<Cite ref="historycoop-fries"/>.
