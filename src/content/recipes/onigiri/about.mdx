Onigiri are rice balls, pressed by hand into a triangle or oval around a filling. The earliest
traces are archaeological. At the Sugitani Chanobatake site in Ishikawa Prefecture, a carbonised lump
of rice from the Yayoi period was unearthed in 1987 <Cite ref="fij-onigiri"/>. Scholars class it as
a chimaki-style rice mass, so whether it was a rice ball is debated <Cite ref="fij-onigiri"/>.
Nippon.com says it was steamed in a wrapping, like chimaki, rather than shaped after cooking
<Cite ref="nippon-onigiri"/>.

The Hitachi no kuni fudoki, compiled around 721, describes hand-formed rice called *nigiri ii*
<Cite ref="nippon-onigiri"/>. Nippon.com places the first
recognisable onigiri in *The Tale of Genji*, written in the eleventh century, which describes the
small rice bundles called *tonjiki* that were eaten while travelling <Cite ref="nippon-onigiri"/>.
Food in Japan gives no date for the tonjiki, and says the evidence for them is thin
<Cite ref="fij-onigiri"/>.

The name changed over time. *Nigiri ii* became *nigiri meshi*, both written with the same characters,
and *meshi* was later dropped and an honorific *o-* added to produce onigiri <Cite ref="nippon-onigiri"/>.
The verb *nigiru* means to grip or press. The folk etymology *oni-giri*, cutting demons, is a
charming story that Food in Japan says no reliable source confirms <Cite ref="fij-onigiri"/>. The
word *omusubi* comes from *musubu*, to tie or bind. *Musubi* came into wide use in the Edo period,
and *omusubi* later became common alongside onigiri <Cite ref="nippon-onigiri"/>.

Popular accounts credit warriors in the Kamakura and Sengoku periods with carrying pressed rice on
campaign, with salt and umeboshi keeping it, and with spreading pickled plum across the country.
Food in Japan says that is hard to verify from primary sources <Cite ref="fij-onigiri"/>.

Until the 1970s most families made rice balls at home, according to Food in Japan. The page
attributes to Seven & i Holdings the claim that its chain began selling onigiri in 1978
<Cite ref="fij-onigiri"/>. Umeboshi, grilled salmon, salted kelp and bonito flakes are the standard
fillings, a set that Nippon.com describes as representing the land and the sea, without giving dates
for any of them <Cite ref="nippon-onigiri"/>.
