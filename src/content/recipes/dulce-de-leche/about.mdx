Dulce de leche is milk and sugar cooked down together until the milk sugars
caramelise, turning the mixture a deep brown and thickening it to a spread.
Versions of the same technique exist across Latin America under other
names (manjar in Chile, arequipe in Colombia, doce de leite in Brazil), and
that spread across so many kitchens and borders is a large part of why no
single origin story has ever taken hold as settled fact
<Cite ref="wiki-ddl"/><Cite ref="lechero-ddl"/>.

The most repeated account places its invention in 1829, at the country estate
of the Argentine politician Juan Manuel de Rosas near Cañuelas, Buenos Aires
province. A maid preparing sweetened milk for mate is said to have left the
pot over the fire and returned to find it transformed into a thick, brown
paste, which Rosas then had made deliberately <Cite ref="cuyo-ddl"/>. The
story is told matter-of-factly in Argentina, where the dish holds an official
designation as part of the country's culinary patrimony, but Uruguay has
long disputed the claim on the same story, with some tellings placing the
maid herself as Uruguayan <Cite ref="cuyo-ddl"/>.

Historians treat the Rosas kitchen-accident story as apocryphal rather than
documented fact, and the documentary record points to something older on the
Chilean side of the Andes. Account books kept by the Colegio de Mendoza
record the arrival of bottled "manjar" (described there as the celebrated
dulce de leche of Chilean origin) between 1693 and 1712, carried over the
mountains long before Rosas's estate existed
<Cite ref="lechero-ddl"/><Cite ref="wiki-ddl"/>. Tradition credits José de
San Martín with carrying the confection back across the Andes into the Río de
la Plata region after his army reached Chile in 1817, taking jars of it with
him on the Peruvian campaign that followed <Cite ref="lechero-ddl"/>.

That older, cross-border history is also why the dispute over which country
owns dulce de leche has never really been settled. In 2003, an Argentine
push to register the confection as an exclusively national product provoked
a sharp diplomatic response from Uruguay's Ministry of Education and Culture,
led by the librarian Raúl Vallarino; after weeks of pointed threats on both
sides, the two governments agreed instead to register dulce de leche, asado
and empanadas jointly as shared cultural and gastronomic heritage of the Río
de la Plata region, rather than the property of either country alone
<Cite ref="a24-ddl"/>.

What is not in dispute is the technique: simmering sweetened milk until it
caramelises is a preservation method as much as a dessert, since cooking the
water out and the sugar down leaves a spread that keeps far longer than fresh
milk. That practical origin, rather than any one kitchen accident, is likely
closer to how the dish actually spread across a region with milk, sugar and
long distances between towns.
