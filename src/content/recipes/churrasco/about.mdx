Churrasco's roots sit in the cattle economy that grew up around Spanish and
Portuguese colonisation of what is now Rio Grande do Sul, where descendants
of cattle introduced to the region multiplied across the grasslands much as
they did on the Argentine side of the Pampas. The gaúchos who worked that
land grilled meat over open fire as a practical way to eat on the range,
seasoned with little beyond salt, because beef was abundant and there was
rarely anything else on hand <Cite ref="wiki-churrascaria"/><Cite ref="enc-churrasco"/>.

What is well established is the shared ancestry churrasco holds with
Argentine asado: both grew from the same gaúcho fireside cooking practised
across the connected grasslands of southern Brazil, Uruguay and Argentina,
and the same reference tradition that describes asado explicitly names
churrasco as that open-fire practice carried into Brazil under a different
name <Cite ref="wiki-churrascaria"/><Cite ref="enc-churrasco"/>. National
borders and separate culinary identities pulled the two apart only later,
as each country built its own restaurant and household traditions on top of
a fireside habit neither one invented alone. Brazil's version distinguishes
itself today by cut and format rather than by seasoning alone — picanha,
ribs and sausage threaded onto skewers or set whole over the coals, salted
plainly and carved as each piece finishes, rather than the parrilla grate
and ordered courses of an Argentine asado. The commercial rodízio
restaurant, where skewered meats are carried table to table and carved to
order, is a twentieth-century restaurant invention built on top of this
older, practical fireside habit rather than a continuation of it.
