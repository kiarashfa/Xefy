The fish and the chips arrived in Britain by separate routes, and only joined
forces well over a century apart. Frying fish in a floured coating, then
eating it cold, was a Sephardic Jewish practice brought to England by refugees
fleeing the Portuguese Inquisition from the sixteenth century onward, useful
precisely because oil-fried fish, unlike fish cooked in hard fat, keeps well
enough to serve at room temperature — a practical answer to the prohibition on
cooking on the Sabbath <Cite ref="toi-fac"/>.

The practice was well established in London by the early nineteenth century.
Charles Dickens mentions a "fried fish warehouse" in *Oliver Twist*
<Cite ref="wiki-fac"/><Cite ref="historicuk-fac"/>, and in 1845 the French
chef Alexis Soyer published a recipe titled "Fried Fish, Jewish Fashion"
<Cite ref="wiki-fac"/><Cite ref="toi-fac"/>. Fried chipped potatoes appear in
the English record at almost the same moment and independently of the fish.
The pairing of the two into a single dish is usually dated to the 1860s,
credited variously to Joseph Malin, an Ashkenazi Jewish immigrant who opened
a fish and chip shop in London's East End, and to John Lees, who is
separately said to have sold the combination from a stall in Mossley,
Lancashire, by 1863 <Cite ref="wiki-fac"/><Cite ref="toi-fac"/><Cite ref="historicuk-fac"/>.
Both claims rest on oral tradition and trade-body recognition rather than
contemporary documentation, and there is no way to settle which came first.

What is well documented is the dish's later status as a fixture of British
life: it was kept exempt from rationing through both World Wars, a choice
remembered as a matter of civilian morale as much as nutrition
<Cite ref="wiki-fac"/><Cite ref="toi-fac"/><Cite ref="historicuk-fac"/>. A
dish built from two unrelated immigrant and industrial food trends, joined
in shops with no fixed single founder, ended up as close to an official
national dish as Britain has.
