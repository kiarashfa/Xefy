Har gow belongs to a specific place and a specific kind of kitchen: the
teahouses of Guangzhou in the early twentieth century, where dim sum developed
as a genre of small, shareable dishes built to be judged one plate at a time
<Cite ref="rimping-hargow"/>. Within that world, credit for har gow itself
usually goes to the Yi Zhen teahouse in Wufeng village, on the edge of the
city, where a chef with a river of fresh shrimp on his doorstep worked out a
dumpling to show it off with nothing standing in the way <Cite ref="wiki-hargow"/>.
As with a great many teahouse-dumpling stories, the restaurant credit is more
solidly attested than any precise date — no one publishes the year Yi Zhen
first served it, and the dish is better understood as an emergent product of
Guangzhou's teahouse dim sum culture than as a single afternoon's invention.

The dumpling's defining feature is not the shrimp but the wrapper around it.
Har gow's skin is wheat starch and tapioca starch scalded with boiling water —
no wheat flour, and so no gluten — which is what gives it a wrapper that turns
glossy and faintly see-through once steamed, showing the pink of the shrimp
through the dough instead of hiding it. That translucency is the whole design
brief: a dumpling built to prove, at a glance, that the kitchen is not
skimping on the filling. Cantonese dim sum menus still treat it as a
benchmark dish — the wrapper thin enough to see through, robust enough not to
split, and pleated by hand into a tight fan along one edge, a piece of
handwork that separates a competent dim sum kitchen from an ordinary one.
