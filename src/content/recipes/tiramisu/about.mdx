Tiramisù is a dessert of mascarpone, egg yolk, sugar, coffee, ladyfingers and cocoa,
and its origin is disputed <Cite ref="wiki-tiramisu"/> <Cite ref="dissapore-tiramisu"/>.
The Dissapore survey concludes that none of the accounts is certain
<Cite ref="dissapore-tiramisu"/>.

The Veneto claim places it at Le Beccherie in Treviso, the restaurant of Ado and Alba
Campeol, in the late 1960s <Cite ref="wiki-tiramisu"/> <Cite ref="dissapore-tiramisu"/>.
The recipe does not appear in cookbooks before the 1960s, and the name is first
recorded in an Italian dictionary in 1980 <Cite ref="wiki-tiramisu"/>
<Cite ref="dissapore-tiramisu"/>.

The Friulian claim is older. Around 1940 Mario Cosolo, at Il Vetturino in Pieris, near
Gorizia, served a dessert first called Coppa Vetturino, and the name tirame su appears
in that account <Cite ref="wiki-tiramisu"/> <Cite ref="dissapore-tiramisu"/>. Dissapore
also gives a Friulian version of the 1950s, at a hotel in Tolmezzo
<Cite ref="dissapore-tiramisu"/>.

Two older stories are told and are not documented. One gives the dessert to Sienese
pastry chefs and Grand Duke Cosimo III in the late seventeenth century
<Cite ref="wiki-tiramisu"/> <Cite ref="dissapore-tiramisu"/>. Another, repeated by the
Accademia del Tiramisù, has a Treviso brothel madam making it as a restorative for her
clients, and Wikipedia gives it as a claim rather than a fact
<Cite ref="wiki-tiramisu"/> <Cite ref="dissapore-tiramisu"/>.

Dissapore places the dessert in the post-war economic boom, drawing on the podcast by
Alberto Grandi and Daniele Soffiati, and notes that the first recipes used pavesini
biscuits rather than ladyfingers <Cite ref="dissapore-tiramisu"/>.
