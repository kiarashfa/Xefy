Roti is an unleavened flatbread of whole-wheat atta, mixed with water and salt, rolled thin and cooked on a dry, heavy pan. It has no leavening at all, so it sits at the unleavened end of the range, alongside the puri, which is also made without a rise.

Roti comes from the Indian subcontinent <Cite ref="wikipedia-chapati"/> <Cite ref="sapiens-roti"/>. Durrani and Biswas place the earliest versions with the peoples of the Indus Valley and the cultivation of ancient grains <Cite ref="sapiens-roti"/>. The name, they write, comes from the Urdu-Hindi word for a slap, for the slapping that flattens the dough <Cite ref="sapiens-roti"/>.

The essay's account of roti is also one of daily work. It describes girls, women, daily wagers and homemakers across India making roti day in and day out, and argues that for many of them it was an economic necessity or unpaid domestic labour <Cite ref="sapiens-roti"/>.

The bread travelled with the people who ate it. Durrani and Biswas write that the British moved about two million indentured workers from colonial India to Asia, the Americas and Africa between the 1830s and 1917, and that this spread roti-making across those regions <Cite ref="sapiens-roti"/>.
