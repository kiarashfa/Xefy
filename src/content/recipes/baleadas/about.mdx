Unlike most dishes on this site, the baleada has a founding story with a
name, a place and a date attached to it, rather than one lost to
generations of home cooks. It is credited to Doña Tere, a single mother
running a food stand near the railway that carried workers to the Standard
Fruit Company's banana plantations in La Ceiba, on Honduras's northern
coast, around 1964 <Cite ref="wsav-baleadas"/> <Cite ref="nuestrostories-baleadas"/>.
She is said to have folded flour tortillas around mashed beans and cheese
to feed plantation and railway workers passing her stand.

Even a dish this recently invented has more than one story attached to its
name. "Baleada" plays on *bala*, Spanish for bullet, and Doña Tere's own
account frames it as a joke: the beans are the bullets, the cheese is the
gunpowder, and the tortilla is the cartridge that holds them
<Cite ref="wsav-baleadas"/> <Cite ref="nuestrostories-baleadas"/>. A second
story circulating in La Ceiba instead holds that a vendor selling the dish
survived a shooting and kept working, and that the dish took her name from
the incident. This is a grimmer account that both sources treat as unconfirmed
local legend rather than established fact
<Cite ref="wsav-baleadas"/> <Cite ref="nuestrostories-baleadas"/>.

The flour tortilla itself marks the dish as a product of the Caribbean
coast rather than Honduras's interior, where maize tortillas are the norm;
wheat flour reached La Ceiba through the same banana-trade infrastructure
that built the railway Doña Tere's stand stood beside.
