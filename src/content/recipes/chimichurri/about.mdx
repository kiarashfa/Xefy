Chimichurri is closely bound up with the Argentine grill, and popular
accounts trace it to the gauchos of the nineteenth-century Pampas, who are
imagined seasoning open-fire beef with whatever herbs and vinegar were at
hand. The documentary record does not support a history that old: the sauce
does not appear in print cookbooks before the late 1950s, a gap of a century
or more between the supposed gaucho origin and its first written trace
<Cite ref="britannica-chimichurri"/>.

The name is the more genuinely contested part of the story. The leading
theory holds it a corruption of the Basque word *tximitxurri*, meaning
roughly "a hodgepodge" or "a mixture of several things in no particular
order", plausible given the substantial Basque immigration to Argentina and
Uruguay, which brought roughly six million arrivals to Argentina alone
between 1870 and 1930 and had already begun building organised communities
there from the 1850s onward <Cite ref="wiki-chimichurri"/><Cite ref="eke-euskadi"/>.
A rival family of stories credits English speakers instead: that the word
garbles "Jimmy's curry" or the name of an Irish or English soldier, sometimes
tied to the failed British invasions of the Río de la Plata in 1806–1807.
Wikipedia, Britannica and Argentine food media covering the claim all treat
these English-language stories the same way, as colourful and widely
repeated legend rather than documented history, with no contemporary source
behind any version of them
<Cite ref="wiki-chimichurri"/><Cite ref="elgourmet-chimichurri"/>.

What is not in dispute is the sauce itself: parsley, garlic, oregano, chilli
and vinegar, built to cut through the richness of grilled beef rather than to
cook alongside it. Its wide reach today, from Uruguay to southern Brazil,
tracks the reach of the Argentine parrilla and the tradition it seasons,
which is a steadier guide to how chimichurri actually spread than any of the
stories told about where its name came from.
