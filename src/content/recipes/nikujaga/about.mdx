The most-told account of nikujaga, the stew of beef and potatoes in a sweet soy broth,
places its origin in the Imperial Japanese Navy in the late nineteenth century. Just One
Cookbook gives the account in its history section: an admiral who had studied in England
asked a naval cook to make the beef stew the British navy served, and the cook, who had
never tried one, made his own version with soy sauce and sugar, which the article compares
to sukiyaki <Cite ref="joc-nikujaga"/>. The dish was first called *amani* and was given to
sailors as a nutritious food <Cite ref="joc-nikujaga"/>.

Food in Japan gives the same figure of Togo Heihachiro and the ship's meal, but
attributes the soy-and-sugar version only in general terms, and says that some hold it to be
an urban legend <Cite ref="fij-nikujaga"/>.

The birthplace is contested in print. Food in Japan reports that Maizuru, in Kyoto Prefecture, declared
itself the birthplace in October 1995 and that Kure, in Hiroshima Prefecture,
followed in March 1998, and that the argument has run for about twenty years
<Cite ref="fij-nikujaga"/>.

The dish stayed out of most home kitchens for longer than its navy origin would suggest.
Just One Cookbook says it did not appear on the table at home until the 1970s, when beef
and potatoes became more widely available <Cite ref="joc-nikujaga"/>.
