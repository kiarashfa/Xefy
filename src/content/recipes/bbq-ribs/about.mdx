Memphis's dry rib is tied to one restaurant. Charlie Vergos opened the
Rendezvous in a Memphis basement in 1948, serving sandwiches until he
converted an old coal chute into a charcoal pit in the mid-1950s and turned
his attention to pork ribs, a cut most of the city's barbecue restaurants had
been ignoring <Cite ref="sfa-rendezvous"/>. His method — a spice rub built on
his family's Greek cooking, a vinegar spritz, and no sauce at the table — is
still credited as the origin of the Memphis dry rib, and the restaurant is
still run by his descendants <Cite ref="sfa-rendezvous"/>.

Kansas City's sauce has a longer chain of hands and a less settled one.
Henry Perry, a pitmaster from Shelby County, Tennessee — practically Memphis's
back yard — brought his smoking to Kansas City in 1908, selling meat wrapped
in newspaper from an alley stand before settling into an old trolley barn,
dressed in a sauce remembered as harsh and peppery rather than sweet
<Cite ref="pendergast-perry"/>. After Perry's death in 1940 the business
passed to his employee Charlie Bryant, who later sold it to his brother
Arthur, and it was Arthur who reworked the sauce recipe at the restaurant
that still carries the Bryant name <Cite ref="pendergast-perry"/>. Accounts
disagree on exactly when the sauce became the thick, tomato-and-molasses
style now associated with the city: some credit Arthur Bryant himself with
the sweetening, while others place the decisive change decades later, with
Rich Davis, whose KC Masterpiece sauce — first sold to grocers in 1977 —
pushed Kansas City barbecue further toward the sweet, thick style most
Americans now picture when they hear the word "barbecue"
<Cite ref="kcur-rich-davis"/>.

The two styles are usually presented as opposites — dry against sauced,
Tennessee against Missouri — but the line runs directly through Perry, a
Tennessean who founded the Kansas City tradition, which makes the rivalry more
a matter of what happened to the sauce after it left Memphis than of two
unconnected cuisines. The St. Louis cut sits alongside both: it names a trim
of the spare rib rack, tips and cartilage removed to a neat rectangle, rather
than a flavour of its own, and it is finished either way — dry, wet, or
somewhere between the two — depending on whose smoker it is in.
