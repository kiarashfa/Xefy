Nachos have an origin story unusual for how precisely it is dated and how little
it is disputed. In 1943, in the border town of Piedras Negras, Coahuila, a group
of American visitors from Eagle Pass, Texas arrived at the Victory Club after the
kitchen had already closed. The maître d', Ignacio "Nacho" Anaya, put together
what he had on hand — fried tortilla pieces topped with melted cheese and sliced
pickled jalapeño — and the diners named the dish after him
<Cite ref="texashighways-nachos"/>.

The name stuck on both sides of the border. Anaya later opened his own
restaurant, and the dish spread north into Texas and the rest of the United
States through the second half of the twentieth century, gaining new toppings
with every kitchen it passed through <Cite ref="time-nachos"/>.

What travelled with it was only the basic idea — a fried corn chip, melted
cheese, sliced chilli — and almost every American version now built on top of
that idea, from stadium concession trays to loaded restaurant platters, is a
long way from Anaya's simple plate. This version keeps to that original
proportion: chips and cheese in alternating layers rather than one dense mound,
so the cheese reaches every chip rather than only the ones on top.
