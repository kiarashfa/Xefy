Soup joumou carries a history that is unusually well documented for a
dish's founding story, because the history in question is not a kitchen
anecdote but a nation's. During French colonial rule, the pumpkin, or
giraumon, that gives the soup its name and its body was grown by enslaved
Africans, and the soup made from it was a delicacy reserved for the
colonists who enslaved them — the people who grew it and cooked it were
forbidden from eating it themselves <Cite ref="unesco-joumou"/>
<Cite ref="pbs-joumou"/>.

That prohibition ended when Haiti's independence did. Haiti declared
itself independent from France on January 1, 1804, following the uprising
that began in 1791 and became the only successful slave revolt in history
to found a nation, and Haitians have marked that date every year since by
eating the soup that had been denied them
<Cite ref="pbs-joumou"/> <Cite ref="unesco-joumou"/>. The dish's meaning
was built into the act of eating it: a food withheld under slavery, claimed
as the first thing eaten in freedom, and repeated every New Year's Day
since as a direct, edible memory of that claim <Cite ref="unesco-joumou"/>.

The soup's significance has since been formally recognised beyond Haiti
itself. In 2021, UNESCO added joumou soup to its Representative List of
Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity, Haiti's first inscription on that
list, citing not only the dish itself but the communal way it is
made — women managing the cooking, children preparing ingredients,
artisans making the pots it is cooked in, and farmers growing the
vegetables that go into it <Cite ref="unesco-joumou"/>.

Soup joumou is still, above all, a dish tied to a specific day rather than
a general one: it constitutes the first meal of the year, eaten on the
anniversary of independence more than as an everyday soup
<Cite ref="unesco-joumou"/> <Cite ref="pbs-joumou"/>. Haitian families
living outside Haiti keep the same custom; PBS Food describes one such
family, in the United States, preparing the soup every January the first
as a way of staying connected to their roots <Cite ref="pbs-joumou"/>.
