Al pastor's trompo — the vertical spit its pork is stacked on — is a shawarma spit
that travelled twice. Beginning in the late 19th century, tens of thousands of people
left Ottoman Lebanon and Syria for Mexico, escaping conscription and searching for
work, and by the 1930s their restaurants were serving shawarma there, in pita, as
lamb <Cite ref="prx-alpastor"/>. An intermediate dish, tacos árabes, kept that pita
bread but had already appeared in Puebla by the 1930s <Cite ref="wiki-alpastor"/>.

The dish now called al pastor is a later, Mexican-born reworking of it. The food
historian Jeffrey Pilcher describes the 1960s shift plainly: cooks "take the
technology that they grew up with in these Lebanese restaurants, the vertical
rotisserie — but instead of using lamb, they use pork" <Cite ref="prx-alpastor"/>.
Pork was marinated in a paste of dried chillies and achiote rather than the original
spice mix, and corn tortillas replaced pita <Cite ref="wiki-alpastor"/>. The name
itself is a fossil of the dish it replaced: *al pastor* means "in the style of the
shepherd", a label that stuck even after the shepherd's lamb was gone
<Cite ref="prx-alpastor"/>.

Exactly when the pineapple arrived — now the dish's most recognisable feature — is
not firmly dated in either account, and Puebla and Mexico City both claim the
version now eaten worldwide. What both sources agree on is the sequence: an Ottoman
technique, carried south by Levantine migration, adapted first into shawarma-in-pita
and only later, a generation on, into the pork-and-pineapple taco known today
<Cite ref="wiki-alpastor"/>.
