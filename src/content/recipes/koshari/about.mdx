The earliest written record of anything resembling koshari comes from the
English explorer Richard Burton, who described Suez residents eating a dish
of rice, lentils and onions in an account from the 1850s
<Cite ref="smithsonian-koshary"/> <Cite ref="wiki-koshary"/>. What it was
called then, and how directly it connects to the layered dish sold today, is
not settled.

The popular story traces koshari to Indian *khichdi*, a rice-and-lentil dish,
supposedly carried to Egypt by Indian soldiers and merchants. Food historians
are split on how seriously to take it. The food archaeologist Mennat-Allah
El Dorry allows that the resemblance is plausible, since rice-and-lentil
dishes turn up in several countries along historic pilgrimage routes, India
among them <Cite ref="smithsonian-koshary"/>. The anthropologist Rania Hilal
disputes the connection more directly, arguing that khichdi is closer to a
spiced, curried pudding in texture and seasoning, while koshari keeps its
components — rice, lentils, pasta — distinct and leans on a tomato-based
sauce that has no real equivalent in the Indian dish
<Cite ref="smithsonian-koshary"/>. Pasta itself is a later addition again,
by one dated account added only after Egypt's monarchy ended in 1953, when
rice grew expensive and macaroni offered a cheaper way to bulk out the same
bowl <Cite ref="smithsonian-koshary"/>.

What is not disputed is koshari's present standing. Sold from street carts
and dedicated koshari houses across the country, it is treated as Egypt's
national dish regardless of which account of its past — Indian import,
homegrown layering tradition, or something worked out between the two —
turns out to be right.
