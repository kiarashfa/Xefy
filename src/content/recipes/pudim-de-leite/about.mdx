Pudim de leite condensado belongs to the same family as the French crème
caramel: an egg custard baked over a layer of caramelised sugar and turned
out so the caramel runs down over the top, a technique Brazilian culinary
teaching places squarely within the same wider custard family as flan and
crème caramel <Cite ref="cozinhatecnica-pudim"/>. What separates the
Brazilian version from its European relatives is a single ingredient,
sweetened condensed milk, which replaces most of the sugar and some of the
milk in the base recipe and gives the finished custard its particular
density and sweetness.

That ingredient's dominance in Brazilian kitchens is not incidental. Nestlé
opened its first Brazilian condensed-milk factory in Araras, São Paulo, in
1921, and the product took its enduring name (Leite Moça, "the young
woman's milk") from the maid pictured on the tin, after customers began
asking for it by that description rather than by its formal name
<Cite ref="joio-pudim"/>. Condensed milk became popular as a dessert
ingredient through the 1940s, and Nestlé's own advertising for pudim and
similar recipes ran continuously from the 1950s into the 1980s
<Cite ref="cozinhatecnica-pudim"/>, distributed through packaging, pamphlets
and a dedicated home-economics service that systematically rebuilt
traditional Brazilian sweets around condensed milk in place of more
laborious techniques; a former head of that service later acknowledged the
company had formulated its own version of recipes that were already
established <Cite ref="joio-pudim"/>. Pudim, along with brigadeiro and
beijinho, was among the sweets reshaped this way.

The result is a case where a company's commercial interest and a country's
culinary habit became difficult to separate: pudim made with condensed milk
is now treated in Brazil as the traditional recipe in its own right, rather
than as one branded version of an older French dessert.
