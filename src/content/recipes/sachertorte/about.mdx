The story goes that in 1832 Prince Metternich, the Austrian state
chancellor, asked his court kitchen to devise a special cake for a banquet.
The chef in charge was ill, and the task fell to a sixteen-year-old
apprentice pastry cook named Franz Sacher, who improvised the dense
chocolate sponge that still carries his name
<Cite ref="wiki-sacher"/><Cite ref="fc-sacher"/>. His son Eduard trained at
Demel, Vienna's rival pastry shop, before opening his own establishment,
the Hotel Sacher, in 1876, where the torte went on the menu
<Cite ref="wiki-sacher"/><Cite ref="fc-sacher"/>.

Decades later, financial pressure on the hotel led Eduard's own son to sell
the recipe on to Demel, and both houses ended up selling their own version
of the same cake under competing claims to authenticity
<Cite ref="wiki-sacher"/><Cite ref="fc-sacher"/>. The dispute reached court
after the Second World War and turned on specifics rather than vague claims
of tradition: Demel's cake carried a single layer of apricot jam beneath
its chocolate glaze, while Hotel Sacher insisted the authentic recipe used
two. The writer Friedrich Torberg, a regular at both establishments,
testified that Hotel Sacher itself had used only one layer before the First
World War, evidence that told against the hotel's own claim
<Cite ref="fc-sacher"/>. The case ended in a settlement giving Hotel Sacher
the exclusive right to sell its cake as the "Original Sachertorte," while
Demel kept the right to sell its own as the "Eduard-Sacher-Torte," marked
with a triangular seal rather than the hotel's round one
<Cite ref="fc-sacher"/>.

Both houses still sell their own version today under those separate
names. This is a rare case of a dessert's authenticity being settled, however
imperfectly, by courtroom testimony rather than by consensus.
