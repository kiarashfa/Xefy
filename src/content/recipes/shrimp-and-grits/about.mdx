Shrimp and grits began as a breakfast, not a dinner. It comes from the Gullah
Geechee people of the South Carolina Lowcountry, descendants of enslaved West
and Central Africans who paired the corn grits ground in the Indigenous manner
with the shrimp running thick in the coastal creeks each autumn — a dish eaten
by fishing families at the start of a working day, long before any restaurant
served it <Cite ref="levine-museum"/>. Shrimp itself carried a particular
meaning in that kitchen: it had been part of West African diets before the
Middle Passage, and its presence on a Lowcountry table has been read as a
thread of memory as much as a convenient local catch <Cite ref="levine-museum"/>.

For generations the dish stayed a home and dockside meal. That changed in the
early 1980s, when chef Bill Neal put a version built on cheddar-and-parmesan
grits, jumbo shrimp, mushrooms and bacon on the dinner menu at Crook's Corner
in Chapel Hill, North Carolina, after joining the restaurant in 1982
<Cite ref="deepsouthmag-2014"/>. The turning point for the dish's wider fame
came in 1985, when *New York Times* food editor Craig Claiborne wrote up
Neal's recipe, giving a regional breakfast a national readership almost
overnight <Cite ref="deepsouthmag-2014"/>. Restaurants across the South
followed with their own versions through the 1990s — Cajun-spiced in
Charleston, finished with barbecue sauce in New Orleans — and the dish now
carries a range of regional treatments far removed from its plainer origin as
a fisherman's morning meal <Cite ref="deepsouthmag-2014"/>.
