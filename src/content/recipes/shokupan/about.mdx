Shokupan is a soft, sliced white loaf, baked in a tin. Bread reached Japan with Portuguese traders
in 1543, and the Japanese word for bread, *pan*, comes from the Portuguese *pão*. Bread was not
widely eaten for centuries after that <Cite ref="jocsk-shokupan"/>.

The Western loaves that shokupan grew from came later. Just One Cookbook says that in the late
1800s the British introduced a round-topped white loaf, and that after the Second World War
American forces brought the flat-topped Pullman loaf. Japanese bakers adapted both styles to local
tastes <Cite ref="jocsk-shokupan"/>.

Nippon.com gives the first Japanese baking of bread as Egawa Tarōzaemon in 1842, a claim that the
article describes as purported <Cite ref="nippon-shokupan"/>. The Meiji-era bakery in that article is
Kimuraya in Ginza, Tokyo, founded in 1869 by former samurai Kimura Yasubē and his son. The
article says that the snack-bread style got its start there in 1874, and that early customers found
the bread hard and unappealing. Yasubē added sweet bean paste to his rolls, which gave rise to the
anpan <Cite ref="nippon-shokupan"/>.

Bread made real inroads into the Japanese diet after the Second World War. It was adapted to local
tastes, and it became much softer than European and American breads. Long, bun-like loaves became
regular school lunch fare, and as urban households grew more Western, sliced white bread called
shokupan became a breakfast staple <Cite ref="nippon-shokupan"/>. Outside Japan the loaf is sometimes
called Hokkaido milk bread, a name that Just One Cookbook attributes to the region's dairy
ingredients <Cite ref="jocsk-shokupan"/>.
