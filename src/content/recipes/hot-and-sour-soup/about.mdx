Few Chinese dishes carry a more disputed regional reputation than this one.
The soup's Chinese name, *suan la tang*, means literally "sour spicy soup,"
and it is served in Western Chinese restaurants as a byword for Sichuan
cooking. That is a claim some writers repeat as settled fact
<Cite ref="196flavors-hotsour"/> and others treat as a popular myth. On that
second account, the soup eaten under this name is barely found in Sichuan at
all, where the region's own genuinely Sichuanese "sour and spicy" dish is a
bowl of noodles rather than a soup, and the thickened, vinegar-and-pepper
soup familiar from restaurant menus is more often traced to Beijing instead.
This is a genuinely disputed attribution that at least one other account rejects
outright, insisting on the Sichuan version instead
<Cite ref="wiki-hotsour"/> <Cite ref="196flavors-hotsour"/>. Neither claim is
easy to verify from outside China, and the disagreement itself is a fair
warning against trusting any single confident attribution for this dish.

The sour-and-spicy combination itself is much older and more widely spread
than any single city's claim to it. Cooking of this general character
(built on vinegar and pepper heat rather than chilli alone) is traced back
as far as the Warring States period, showing up across a broad swathe of
northern and central China rather than starting in one province
<Cite ref="196flavors-hotsour"/>. The pairing is not uniquely Chinese either:
closely related sour-and-hot soups turn up independently across South East
Asia, from Vietnamese *canh chua* to Thai *tom yum*, suggesting the appeal
of balancing sourness against heat in a hot broth was reached more than once,
by more than one cuisine, rather than travelling outward from a single point
of origin <Cite ref="196flavors-hotsour"/>.

What stays constant wherever the soup is claimed from is its structure
rather than its geography: a thickened broth built for contrast, sharp with
vinegar and hot with pepper, carrying enough texture (tofu, mushroom, wood
ear, a ribbon of egg) to be a meal in a bowl rather than a prelude to one.
