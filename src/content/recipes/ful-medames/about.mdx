The name is a description of a cooking method rather than an ingredient
list. *Ful* is simply Arabic for beans; *medames* comes from Coptic and means
"buried," recording how the dish was once cooked — a pot of fava beans
sunk into hot ash or embers and left to simmer for hours, sealed rather than
watched <Cite ref="wiki-fulmedames"/> <Cite ref="rk-fulmedames"/>.

That method had a specific home in medieval and early-modern Cairo: for
generations, the trade in cooked ful was centred on the Princess Baths, a
public bathhouse near the old Bab Zuweila gate, where enormous amphora-shaped
pots of bathwater, called *qidras*, were kept boiling over fires stoked all
day. When the baths closed for the night, rather than let the embers go to
waste, more qidras were filled with beans and buried in the coals to cook
slowly until morning, supplying cookshops across the city with wholesale ful
for breakfast <Cite ref="rk-fulmedames"/>. The nineteenth-century orientalist
Edward William Lane described the same practice directly in 1860: earthenware
pots "buried, all but the neck," in the hot ash of an oven or bathhouse,
their contents eaten with oil and lime juice
<Cite ref="wiki-fulmedames"/> <Cite ref="rk-fulmedames"/>.

Whether the dish itself reaches back to ancient Egypt, as is often claimed,
is considerably less certain than the bathhouse history. The claim rests on
fava beans found at a late-Neolithic site and on the Coptic root of
*medames* — circumstantial evidence at best, which is why Wikipedia
qualifies the pharaonic-era origin as something "some writers have
suggested" rather than established, and Roads & Kingdoms allows only that
the popular legend "jibes, somewhat" with the documented facts
<Cite ref="wiki-fulmedames"/> <Cite ref="rk-fulmedames"/>. The
claim is best treated as a popular attribution rather than an established
fact. What is not in doubt is the dish's staying power: cooked slowly,
dressed simply, and eaten daily, ful medames remains Egypt's most common
breakfast and is widely regarded as its national dish.
