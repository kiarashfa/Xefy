Pernil's name states its cut before anything else: it comes from the
Spanish *pierna*, leg, and the dish traces to Spain's Extremadura region,
where a whole pork leg was traditionally spit-roasted over an open fire as a
prized, rather than everyday, piece of the animal <Cite ref="sanjuan-pernil"/>.
The technique that seasons it travelled the same route. Adobo began on the
Iberian Peninsula as a way of preserving meat rather than simply flavouring
it, and only over time, as refrigeration made preservation unnecessary, did
it settle into its present role as a wet or dry seasoning paste rubbed into
the meat before cooking <Cite ref="196flavors-pernil"/>.

In Puerto Rico that paste became adobo mojado: garlic, oregano, salt and oil
worked into deep cuts across the surface of a bone-in shoulder or leg, left
to penetrate overnight, and then roasted slowly until the skin cracks and
the meat pulls apart with a fork <Cite ref="sanjuan-pernil"/>. Pork itself
reached the island with Spanish colonisation, and a whole leg fed a
gathering more efficiently than almost any other cut available, which is
part of why pernil became fixed to the island's largest family occasion
rather than staying an everyday dish <Cite ref="sanjuan-pernil"/>.

Pernil is now the dish most closely associated with Puerto Rican Nochebuena,
and the same wet-adobo roast pork tradition, under the same name, extends
across much of Latin America, appearing at Christmas, New Year and other
family celebrations well beyond the island <Cite ref="196flavors-pernil"/>.
