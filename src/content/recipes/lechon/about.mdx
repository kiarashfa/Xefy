The word is Spanish before it is Filipino. *Lechón* is built from *leche*,
"milk," and named a milk-fed suckling pig before it named anything
Philippine; Spanish colonisation carried the word wherever the empire went,
which is why lechón also names roast-pig dishes in Cuba, Puerto Rico and
across the former Spanish Caribbean and South America
<Cite ref="wiki-lechon"/> <Cite ref="latinpost-lechon"/>.

What the word was attached to in the Philippines was not a Spanish
invention. Archaeological evidence gathered by the National Museum shows
Filipinos eating pork as far back as seven thousand years ago, and the
Italian chronicler Antonio Pigafetta recorded a ritual pig roasting, led by a
babaylan priestess, during his 1521 voyage with Magellan's expedition — years
before sustained Spanish colonisation began
<Cite ref="tribune-lechon"/>. The Filipino version also diverged in its
seasoning: Philippine lechón is typically stuffed with native aromatics such
as lemongrass, garlic and citrus, rather than the salt-and-water seasoning of
the Iberian dish it takes its name from
<Cite ref="wiki-lechon"/> <Cite ref="philstar-lechon"/>. The label arrived
from Spain; the roasting tradition and its seasoning did not.

The best-documented chapter of that history is comparatively recent and
regional. Cebu's particular style of lechon, now internationally the most
famous <Cite ref="wiki-lechon"/> <Cite ref="gma-lechon"/>, is traced to
lechoneros working in Talisay City from the 1920s onward, who at the time
called their pork *inasal* — from *asal*, to cook over live coal — rather
than lechon <Cite ref="gma-lechon"/>. Multi-generation
family operations from that period, including the Garces family's recipe,
are still trading in Talisay today, alongside rival lechon towns such as
Carcar that built their own reputations on the same technique
<Cite ref="gma-lechon"/>.
