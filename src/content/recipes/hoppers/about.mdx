The name almost certainly comes from the Sanskrit *apupa*, a fried dainty, and
the dish it names is old enough to appear in Sangam-era Tamil literature. The
*Perumpāṇāṟṟuppaṭai* describes vendors selling appams "like honeycombs" alongside
another sweet called modakam, placing something recognisably like this pancake
in Tamil regions well over a thousand years before it took its current form
<Cite ref="wiki-appam"/>.

How it reached Sri Lanka specifically is not precisely documented, but the
general route is well understood: trade, colonial administration and
migration between South India and Sri Lanka carried the dish across the strait
along with a great deal of the rest of Tamil and South Indian cooking
<Cite ref="slurrp-hoppers"/>. The English name is a later, separate layer —
"hopper" is generally taken to be a British colonial-era anglicisation of
*appa*, the word settling into English usage rather than being translated
<Cite ref="slurrp-hoppers"/>.

What varies most across the dish's range is how the batter is leavened.
Fermentation methods differ from place to place and household to household —
some Kerala cooks, notably in the Cochin Jewish community, have historically
used fresh toddy in place of yeast, leaning on the same wild fermentation that
gives the batter its rise and faint sourness by other means <Cite ref="wiki-appam"/>.
The constant across all of them is the shape: a batter swirled up the sides of
a small curved pan so it sets thin and lacy at the rim and soft in the middle,
which is what separates a hopper from a flat-cooked dosa made from a similar
kind of batter.
