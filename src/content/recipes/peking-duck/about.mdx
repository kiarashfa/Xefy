The name is less straightforward than it sounds. Roast duck is recorded in
Chinese sources as far back as the Yuan dynasty, but food historians treat
that early dish as an ancestor of the modern one rather than the same thing
<Cite ref="scmp-pekingduck"/>. The specific duck now called Peking duck
develops later and further south, as a Nanjing specialty that travelled north
only after the Yongle Emperor moved the capital to Beijing in the early
fifteenth century, carrying the dish and the cooks who made it along with the
rest of the court <Cite ref="natgeo-pekingduck"/>.

Two distinct cooking methods grew up around it, and both still exist today.
Nanjing cooks had developed the *menlu*, a squared, closed brick oven that
roasted several ducks together over an internal fire; the *gualu*, an open
hanging oven fired by fruitwood, developed later, inside the kitchens of the
Qing court, and let a duck be roasted individually and to order rather than
in a batch <Cite ref="natgeo-pekingduck"/>. It is the hanging-oven method that
defines the dish as it is known internationally today.

That international identity is largely the work of one restaurant. In 1864,
a former poultry trader named Yang Quanren opened Quanjude in Beijing and
staffed it with cooks trained in the former imperial kitchens, bringing the
hanging-oven technique out of the palace and into a restaurant the public
could actually eat at <Cite ref="natgeo-pekingduck"/>. Quanjude's duck found
favour first with Beijing's own upper classes, and much later found a
different kind of fame: Peking duck was the dish Premier Zhou Enlai served
to Henry Kissinger and Richard Nixon during the diplomatic visits of the
1970s, cementing its status as China's best-known dish abroad
<Cite ref="natgeo-pekingduck"/>.
