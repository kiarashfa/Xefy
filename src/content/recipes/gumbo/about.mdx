Gumbo draws on at least three culinary traditions at once, and even its name
is disputed. The word is most often traced to *ki-ngombo* or *gombo*, a Bantu
term for okra carried to Louisiana by enslaved people from Central Africa,
since early written gumbos were thickened with the vegetable the word itself
names <Cite ref="parishes-choctaw-gumbo"/>. A rival claim holds that the name
instead comes from the Choctaw word for filé, *kombo* — but that word does not
appear in Choctaw dictionaries before around 1909, more than a century after
"gombo" was already being recorded in Louisiana in the 1760s and 1780s, and
Choctaw has no /g/ sound to begin with. The likelier reading is that Choctaw
vendors adopted a version of the existing word to market ground sassafras
leaves to non-Choctaw customers in New Orleans markets, rather than the word
originating with them <Cite ref="parishes-choctaw-gumbo"/>.

What is not in dispute is that Choctaw foodways shaped the dish itself: filé,
made from dried and ground sassafras leaves, was their contribution as both a
thickener and a seasoning, used as an alternative to okra rather than
alongside it <Cite ref="sfa-gumbo"/>. The dark roux that anchors most modern
gumbos is a French technique, pushed far past the pale stage a French kitchen
would normally stop at <Cite ref="sfa-gumbo"/>. The historian Carl A.
Brasseaux places the earliest documented references to gumbo in Louisiana
around 1800, including its appearance at a New Orleans gubernatorial reception
in 1803 and at an Acadian Coast gathering the following year, with the dish
well enough established by 1885 to appear in multiple published Creole
cookbooks <Cite ref="sfa-gumbo"/>. The result is a dish that different
households have always finished differently — with okra, with filé, or with
neither — depending on which of its several ancestries a given cook leaned on.
