Collard greens are Eurasian, not African, in origin — a leafy, non-heading
brassica the Romans and Greeks already knew. The long-standing account of how
they reached the American South has been that European settlers brought them
over in the sixteenth century as a garden crop, which enslaved Africans then
took up and cooked in their own way <Cite ref="conversation-2025"/>.

That account is now being questioned rather than overturned. Researchers
Bronwen Powell and Abderrahim Ouarghidi found collards growing among African
diaspora communities in oases of southern Morocco and traced supporting
evidence to a tenth-century Baghdad cookbook recipe for "kurunb Nabati" that
closely matches the way collards are still cooked there today, alongside a
nineteenth-century explorer's account and a surviving classical Arabic name for
the plant in the region <Cite ref="conversation-2025"/>. On that reading,
collards may have travelled with Muslim traders from Iraq and Oman to North
Africa centuries before any European ship carried them west. The researchers
themselves describe the case as one for revisiting the traditional narrative,
not replacing it outright, since no direct evidence yet links Morocco to the
plant's arrival in America <Cite ref="conversation-2025"/>.

What is not in dispute is what enslaved cooks did with the plant once it was
in the South. Cooking the tough leaves down slowly in a pot of water, rather
than serving them fresh, and treasuring the broth left behind as "pot likker"
is a practice with African roots, carried into a Southern dish built around a
smoked pork bone for seasoning <Cite ref="tastingtable-2025"/>. The dish also
carries its New Year's Day symbolism from that same community: the flat green
leaves are said to resemble folded paper money, and collards eaten alongside
black-eyed peas on the first day of the year are supposed to bring
prosperity for the months that follow <Cite ref="tastingtable-2025"/>.
