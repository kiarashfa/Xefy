Asado is older than the backyard grill it is now cooked on. Its roots are with
the gauchos, the horsemen of the Argentine Pampas who worked and lived among
the descendants of cattle brought to the region by Spanish colonisation, and
whose numbers had multiplied into vast, unmanaged herds that roamed the
grasslands from at least the eighteenth century until the mid-nineteenth
<Cite ref="wiki-gaucho"/><Cite ref="enc-asado"/>. Beef was
abundant and land was not fenced, so a gaucho diet built almost entirely
around it, cooked over an open fire, was as much a matter of what was
available as of taste <Cite ref="enc-asado"/>.

The technique that developed for it is largely the one still used: a side of
ribs skewered on a metal frame and set beside, rather than over, a
slow-burning fire, so the meat cooks through gently instead of scorching
<Cite ref="enc-asado"/>. Quebracho, a dense regional hardwood, was the
preferred fuel because it burns down to coals with very little smoke, leaving
the meat's own flavour rather than the wood's. That fireside version (meat
staked upright beside the coals rather than laid on a grate) survives today
as *asado a la cruz*, still used for whole animals at large gatherings, while
the smaller, everyday version has moved onto a raised grill, the parrilla,
as Argentina urbanised.

Two things distinguish an asado from simply grilling beef. The first is
sequence: chorizo and other achuras go on first and are eaten as the fire
gets going, well before the main cuts are ready, so the meal unfolds in
courses rather than arriving at once. The second is restraint: salt is
usually the only seasoning, on the reasoning that a slow fire and a good cut
need nothing else to taste of themselves. The same open-fire tradition spread
under other names as it crossed borders, surfacing in Brazil as churrasco
<Cite ref="enc-asado"/>, though the two developed into recognisably different
practices rather than remaining one dish under two words.
