The name states the technique: *gyros* is Greek for "turn", a direct calque of the
Turkish *döner*, itself from *dönmek*, "to turn" <Cite ref="wiki-gyros"/>. Both names
describe the same vertical spit, developed in Bursa in the 19th-century Ottoman Empire
<Cite ref="wiki-gyros"/>. Gyros is not an independent invention but that Ottoman
technique arriving in Greece with the people who cooked it.

It arrived specifically in 1922, when hundreds of thousands of Greek and Armenian
refugees from Asia Minor — most from Constantinople and Smyrna — were resettled in
Greece under the population exchange that followed the Greco-Turkish War
<Cite ref="kochilas-gyros"/>. Armenian cooks among them had a particular reputation for
the dish, and as refugees opened small shops across Greek cities, the spit-roasted
meat they sold became a fixture of Greek street food <Cite ref="kochilas-gyros"/>.
In its new setting the meat shifted from the lamb and beef of a Turkish döner toward
pork, the cut that came to define gyros as distinct from its parent dish.

Greek immigration carried gyros onward after the Second World War, and it reached the
United States alongside that migration, taking hold in particular in Chicago
<Cite ref="kochilas-gyros"/>. By 1970 gyros wraps were already established fast food in
both Athens and Chicago, and the Chicago area became the centre of the machinery and
pre-formed meat that turned gyros into a mass-produced product across America in the
years that followed <Cite ref="wiki-gyros"/>.
