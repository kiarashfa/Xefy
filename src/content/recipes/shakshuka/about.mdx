The name is the dish cooking. *Shakshuka* comes from a Maghrebi Arabic verb,
*shakshaka*, which the Oxford English Dictionary glosses as to bubble, to sizzle, to be
mixed up or beaten together; in Tunisian and Algerian usage the noun simply means a
mixture <Cite ref="wiki-shakshouka"/>. English took it in the nineteenth century through
French, as *chakchouka* <Cite ref="wiki-shakshouka"/>.

Whatever its age, the dish cannot be very old. Neither tomatoes nor peppers existed in
North Africa before Spanish and Portuguese ships brought them from the Americas, and
they were not established around the Mediterranean until the sixteenth and seventeenth
centuries <Cite ref="eats-shakshuka"/>. What preceded them was a much older Amazigh
habit of stewing whatever vegetables were in season, into which eggs were eventually
broken <Cite ref="eats-shakshuka"/>.

Where it belongs is contested, and the earliest European notices are contested in
themselves: a French source calls chakchouka Tunisian in 1894, a newspaper lists it
among Egyptian foods in 1896, and a European account calls it Turkish in 1899
<Cite ref="eats-shakshuka"/>. Gil Marks derives it from the Turkish *şakşuka* and has it
travelling west with the Ottomans; Anthony Buccini and Noah Sienna instead place
shakshuka, menemen, piperade and ratatouille in one family of western Mediterranean
vegetable stews with a common ancestry rather than a single point of origin
<Cite ref="wiki-shakshouka"/>. The food historian Rafram Chaddad credits it most
directly to Tunisia and to the Amazigh <Cite ref="eats-shakshuka"/>.

The last part of the story is better documented than the first. Shakshuka arrived in
Israel with Maghrebi Jewish migration in the 1950s — it had no prior place in Palestinian
or Levantine cooking — reached Israeli restaurant menus in the 1990s, and travelled from
there onto brunch menus everywhere <Cite ref="wiki-shakshouka"/>. The dish is North
African. Its fame is not.
