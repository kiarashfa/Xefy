The name is a plain description rather than a recipe: *nyama* is meat, *choma*
is to roast or burn, and together they simply mean grilled meat. What that
description covers is the closest thing Kenya has to a national dish: most
often goat, sometimes beef, salted and grilled slowly over charcoal or wood
until the outside is charred and the inside still juicy
<Cite ref="wiki-nyama-choma"/><Cite ref="si-kenya"/>.

Accounts of how it began do not agree on much beyond an open fire. One
tradition traces it to hunters who grilled game over the fire on hunting
expeditions before the practice moved into everyday and celebratory cooking
<Cite ref="si-kenya"/>; a separate and more commonly repeated account
attributes it to pastoralist communities grilling livestock while herding.
Neither is documented with the kind of written record that would settle the
question, and the honest position is that outdoor grilling of meat over a
wood or charcoal fire has deep and probably multiple roots across East
Africa's herding and hunting communities rather than one traceable origin.

What is better documented is nyama choma's modern social role. It is
traditionally a men's occasion, grilled and tended outdoors by men even in
households where cooking is otherwise women's work
<Cite ref="si-kenya"/>, and it is built around gathering rather than a
sit-down meal: eaten standing or seated around the fire, with the hands,
alongside ugali and kachumbari, and washed down with beer, Tusker lager
by long-standing custom <Cite ref="si-kenya"/>. Roadside "choma joints" and
dedicated grill restaurants across Kenya and Tanzania have formalised what
began as an informal, communal way of cooking, but the fire and the
gathering around it remain the point as much as the meat itself.
