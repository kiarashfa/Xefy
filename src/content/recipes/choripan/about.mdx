Choripán's name needs no decoding: *chori*, for chorizo, and *pan*, bread.
Beyond that, the documented history is thinner than the sandwich's
ubiquity suggests. Wikipedia's account of the dish includes a period
photograph, dated 1925, of vendors selling something described as choripán
on a Buenos Aires street — the earliest visual trace anyone has produced —
though the setting most associated with the sandwich today, the grill carts
along the city's Costanera riverside, is independently dated only to the
early 1960s <Cite ref="wiki-choripan"/><Cite ref="canal26-choripan"/>. That
gap of several decades between the earliest image and the earliest
well-documented everyday setting is itself telling: the sandwich in some
form is older than its modern street-food identity. The historian Daniel
Balmaceda argues, separately, that the choripán eaten today — the sausage
split, grilled and dressed rather than simply pushed whole into bread — was
not fixed in that form until it
came together at Parque Sarmiento in Córdoba in the 1950s, before spreading
outward from there <Cite ref="lanacion-choripan"/>. No source fixes an exact
date or a single inventor for either moment, and the two accounts describe
different things: an earlier, looser Buenos Aires street food, and a later
Córdoba refinement of the sandwich into its now-standard form.

By the end of that decade the dish had reached national and even
international notice — Fidel Castro reportedly ate a choripán from a cart on
Buenos Aires's Costanera Norte during his visit to Argentina in 1959, a
detail that places the sandwich squarely in the capital's street-food scene
by the time Balmaceda's Córdoba version was supposedly still spreading
<Cite ref="lanacion-choripan"/>. Neither Córdoba nor Buenos Aires has
conceded the point to the other. What is not disputed is the role the
sandwich plays: it is the food eaten while an asado is still cooking, sold
outside football stadiums and at roadside stalls, and treated as everyday
food rather than as anything needing an origin story — which may be exactly
why so little of one was written down before now.
