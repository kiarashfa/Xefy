Khinkali is credited to Georgia's northeastern mountain provinces — Pshavi,
Khevsureti, Tusheti, Mtiuleti and Khevi — where it is remembered as food made
for shepherds and farmers working long hours in a cold climate, portable and
filling enough to sustain a day of labour <Cite ref="wiki-khinkali"/>. Local
tradition inside Georgia credits particular mountain villages, Pasanauri among
them, with originating the dish <Cite ref="feast-khinkali"/>.

How the dish arrived in those mountains at all is not settled. One account
holds that it travelled with Mongol steppe armies passing along the Silk Road
around the thirteenth century, who carried meat wrapped in dough as
trail food, and that it entered Georgian cooking from that contact
<Cite ref="wiki-khinkali"/>. The theory is disputed rather than accepted: at
least one Georgian chef has rejected it outright as invention purely local to
the Caucasus, while other food writers argue the steppe connection plausible
without treating it as proven <Cite ref="feast-khinkali"/>. No single source
settles which account is right.

The dish's two main styles mark out where it comes from. The mountain
version, khevsuruli or mtiuluri, is seasoned sparely — minced lamb, beef or
pork with onion, salt, chilli and cumin, and no fresh herbs at all — while
the urban kalakuri style that spread to Tbilisi and beyond works parsley and
coriander into the filling <Cite ref="wiki-khinkali"/><Cite ref="feast-khinkali"/>.
The herbs are the dividing line between the two, not the meat.

One piece of etiquette survives from the mountains into the modern
restaurant: the twisted knob of dough at the top, formed where the pleats are
gathered and sealed, is tough enough that it is traditionally left on the
plate rather than eaten, and diners are said to count the discarded knobs to
tally how many they have had <Cite ref="wiki-khinkali"/>.
