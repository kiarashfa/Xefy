Challah is a braided loaf of enriched dough, made for the Shabbat table. The braid is what marks it as the Shabbat bread: Miller describes the braided loaves of white flour, later enriched with eggs and oil, as a way of marking the Shabbat bread as special, set apart from the dark rye loaves of the week <Cite ref="mjl-challah"/>. Chabad.org gives the braid as the customary form for Shabbat, and notes that holiday customs vary <Cite ref="chabad-challah"/>.

The braid's origin is explained in more than one way. My Jewish Learning reports Gil Marks's account that Austrian and southern German Jews braided their loaves in the style of a bread called berchisbrod, named after the braids of the witch Holle. The braiding was meant to resemble a sacrifice of braided hair that would ward off her anger. German Jews kept the braided form and dropped the myth <Cite ref="mjl-challah"/>. Chabad.org offers religious reasons instead. It says two braided loaves stand for the twelve showbreads of the Temple, and that braiding set challah apart from ordinary bread that could be eaten with meat or with dairy <Cite ref="chabad-challah"/>.

The name also has a history. My Jewish Learning says that by 1920 the Hebrew spelling had won out, and "challah" entered mainstream American usage <Cite ref="mjl-challah"/>.
