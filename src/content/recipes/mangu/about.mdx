Mangú is Dominican plantain fufu: green plantains boiled soft and mashed to a
smooth paste, a technique most food historians trace to West Africa, where
pounded and mashed starchy staples were already a staple. Plantains
themselves were a Spanish import to Hispaniola from the Canary Islands, so
the plant and the technique that turns it into mangú arrived from opposite
directions and met on the same island.

The dish's name is where the story gets contested rather than settled. The
most repeated version holds that an American soldier during the 1916–1924
occupation of the Dominican Republic tried the mashed plantain and declared
"Man, good!", which Dominicans supposedly turned into "mangú"
<Cite ref="wikipedia-mangu"/> <Cite ref="diariolibre-mangu"/>. A second,
less-repeated story instead traces the word to Haitian Creole, with a mother
mashing plantain for a child who exclaimed "¡Maman gou!"
<Cite ref="diariolibre-mangu"/>. A third theory ties "mangú" to "mangusi," a
supposed Bantu word for any mashed tuber, but an exhaustive search of
academic dictionaries and reference works on Bantu and Congolese vocabulary
has turned up no entry for the word at all, leaving that theory without
documentary support <Cite ref="diariolibre-mangu"/>. None of the three
stories is confirmed by the written record: the earliest documented print
appearance of "mangú" found so far is not a newspaper account of a soldier
or a folk tale but a 1939 Dominican cookbook, Eulalia Cordero Infante's *La
cocina dominicana*, which simply uses the word without explaining it
<Cite ref="diariolibre-mangu"/>. What survives all three unverified
etymologies is the plainer explanation: an African-descended word for a dish
with clear African-descended roots, its exact derivation lost the way most
everyday food vocabulary is lost, without a paper trail.

Mangú is eaten as breakfast most often, and most completely in the form
called los tres golpes, "the three hits", where the mash is served
alongside fried cheese, fried eggs and fried Dominican salami.
