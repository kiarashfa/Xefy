The name means "of Burgundy", but the dish is not, on the evidence, an old regional recipe. The French writer Marie Ébrard said in 1928 that, despite the name, it was not a regional speciality, and the dish does not appear on lists of Burgundian fare until well into the twentieth century <Cite ref="natgeo-bourguignon"/>, <Cite ref="wiki-bourguignon"/>.

The first mention in print is in Pierre Larousse's *Grand Dictionnaire universel du XIXe siècle*, published in 1867 <Cite ref="natgeo-bourguignon"/>, <Cite ref="wiki-bourguignon"/>. The first record of it served in a restaurant is an 1878 Baedeker's guide to Paris <Cite ref="natgeo-bourguignon"/>. The first printed recipe that National Geographic identifies is in M. Butler's *La Bonne Cuisine pour Tous* of 1885, which recommends the dish as a use for leftover meat <Cite ref="natgeo-bourguignon"/>, <Cite ref="wiki-bourguignon"/>.

Its nineteenth-century reputation was poor. The food historian Jim Chevallier observes that it "did not enjoy a great reputation", perhaps because it was often made from cooked meat that was already leftover <Cite ref="wiki-bourguignon"/>, <Cite ref="natgeo-bourguignon"/>.

The dish's American reputation comes largely from Julia Child, Simone Beck and Louisette Bertholle, whose *Mastering the Art of French Cooking* of 1961 called it one of the most delicious beef dishes ever made. Neither source credits Child with inventing it <Cite ref="natgeo-bourguignon"/>, <Cite ref="wiki-bourguignon"/>.
