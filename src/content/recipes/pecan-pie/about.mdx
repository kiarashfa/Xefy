Pecans are native to North America, and Indigenous peoples of the Mississippi
and southern river valleys were harvesting and trading them long before pie
was a category of food they had any reason to make. The earliest known written
pecan pie recipes are later and more modest: food historians point to a pecan
custard pie recipe printed in a Texas cookbook in the 1870s, and the earliest
mention of "pecan pie" by that name in a national publication is usually dated
to *Harper's Bazaar* in 1886 <Cite ref="th-pecanpie"/>. For its first decades
it stayed a regional Southern dish rather than a national one.

The thick, sweet, almost custardy filling now considered standard has a
specific and traceable source. In 1902 the Corn Products Refining Company
began selling Karo, the first widely marketed corn syrup in the United States,
and in the 1930s the company started printing a pecan pie recipe on the Karo
label itself as a way of suggesting a use for the syrup <Cite ref="slate-pecanpie"/>.
The story that a corporate sales executive's wife devised the recipe is
repeated often but is a company anecdote rather than a documented fact, and is
best treated as folklore attached to a real marketing campaign rather than a
settled origin.

What the Karo recipe undeniably did was standardise the pie nationally: by the
1940s, pecan pie had gone from a regional specialty to a fixture of mainstream
American cookbooks such as *The Joy of Cooking* <Cite ref="slate-pecanpie"/>.
