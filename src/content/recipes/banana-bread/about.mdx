Banana bread belongs to a category of baking, the quick breads, that did not exist in its modern form until chemical leavening did. Baking powder
reached American grocery shelves in quantity only in the early 1930s, and it was that availability, rather than any single inventor, that let bakers
raise a batter with bicarbonate and acid instead of waiting on yeast <Cite ref="wiki-bananabread"/><Cite ref="foodfueled-bananabread"/>. Recipes for
banana bread proper follow closely behind: it "began to gain popularity in the early 1930s" once the technique was available to home cooks
<Cite ref="foodfueled-bananabread"/>.

Why bananas specifically is less settled than the loaf's now-fixed place in home baking suggests. One account ties the recipe to the Great
Depression, when households facing real scarcity found a use for bananas already too ripe to eat out of hand, whose sugar stood in for some of the
sugar a recipe would otherwise need <Cite ref="wiki-bananabread"/><Cite ref="foodfueled-bananabread"/>. A competing explanation credits the flour and
baking-soda producers of the period with promoting the recipe as a way to move their own products, which would make the bread's association with
thrift a matter of marketing rather than necessity <Cite ref="wiki-bananabread"/>. The two accounts are not easily reconciled from the record that
survives, and the bread's popularity was in any case not new: a banana bread was already being sold in Lawrence, Kansas, as early as January 1881,
decades before baking powder made the quick-bread version possible <Cite ref="wiki-bananabread"/>.

The recipe kept changing well after its first popularity. Loaves grown plainer after the Second World War, with fewer additions, before brown sugar
and whole-wheat flour became common again from the 1960s <Cite ref="foodfueled-bananabread"/> — a reminder that "banana bread" has named a moving
target for most of its history rather than one fixed recipe.
