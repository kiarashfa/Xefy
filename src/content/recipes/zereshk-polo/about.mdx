Barberries, called *zereshk* across most of Iran, grow wild on a thorny shrub
whose red fruit ripens in midsummer, and their sharp, sour flavour has been
used in Persian cooking as a seasoning for soups, rice dishes and stuffings
for centuries, and zereshk polo (rice cooked with the dried berries stirred
through or scattered over the top, most often alongside saffron-braised
chicken) is the dish that turns that seasoning into its centrepiece
<Cite ref="iranica-barberry"/><Cite ref="wiki-zereshk-polo"/>.

Exactly when barberries and rice became this specific, named polow rather
than just a seasoning is not precisely dated. Rice itself became a mark of
Persian court luxury under the Safavid dynasty, the sixteenth to eighteenth
centuries, and rice cookery was refined further still under the Qajars of
the nineteenth century, whose court served rice dishes markedly more
elaborate and time-consuming than anything the Safavid table had, poultry
included among them at wedding feasts <Cite ref="wiki-zereshk-polo"/>
<Cite ref="iranica-qajar-cuisine"/>. Where zereshk polo itself (and the
saffron chicken, or *morgh*, often served alongside it) fits on that
timeline is not documented in detail, so the dish's own emergence is best
read as approximate rather than settled
<Cite ref="wiki-zereshk-polo"/><Cite ref="iranica-qajar-cuisine"/>.

What has stayed constant is the dish's status as festive rather than
everyday food. It belongs to the category Persians call *majlesi*
(gathering food) served at weddings, engagement parties and family
celebrations rather than on a Tuesday, valued as much for the visual
contrast of crimson berries against white, saffron-streaked rice as for its
sweet-tart flavour. The technique underneath
it is standard Persian rice cookery: the rice is soaked, parboiled and then
steamed to finish, the same method that produces the crisp *tahdig* crust
found under most Persian polows.
