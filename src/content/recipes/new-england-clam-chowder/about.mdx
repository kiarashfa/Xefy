Long before European settlers arrived, coastal Algonquian-speaking peoples of
the region were harvesting quahog clams and cooking them into shellfish stews,
a practice that runs back centuries and that the standard telling of chowder's
history routinely omits in favour of a purely European story
<Cite ref="ictnews-chowder"/>. The dish usually credited as chowder's direct
ancestor — French, Breton or English fish-and-biscuit stews carried over by
settlers — most plausibly absorbed and built on food and technique already
established on the coast rather than introducing the idea of a clam stew from
nothing <Cite ref="ictnews-chowder"/>.

The milk-based version now called New England chowder took its familiar shape
by the eighteenth century, thickened with crushed crackers or bread before the
now-standard combination of potato and dairy settled in
<Cite ref="wiki-clamchowder"/>. Its most famous rivalry is regional rather than
international: Manhattan clam chowder, built on tomatoes with no dairy
anywhere near it, is treated in New England as close to a heresy. Maine state
representative Cleveland Sleeper made a running joke of introducing bills to
ban tomatoes from chowder outright, and in 1939 the Maine Hotel Association
settled the argument with a cook-off rather than a law — a panel that
included the state's governor and Ruth Wakefield, inventor of the chocolate
chip cookie, tasted both versions and voted unanimously for the New England
recipe <Cite ref="nehs-clamchowderwar"/>.

The version here keeps to the New England line — potato, milk and cream, no
tomato — and follows the one technical rule most chowder disagreements do
agree on: the clams and the dairy go in last, so that neither toughens nor
splits over heat meant for the vegetables underneath them.
