The tarte Tatin takes its name from the sisters Stéphanie and Caroline Tatin, who ran the Hôtel Tatin in Lamotte-Beuvron, in the Loir-et-Cher <Cite ref="dm-tatin"/>, <Cite ref="wiki-tatin"/>. The historian Bernard Munos, as the Daily Maverick reports him, dates the opening of the hotel to 1894, when the tart already had a following <Cite ref="dm-tatin"/>.

The story most often told is that Stéphanie, overworked, left apples cooking too long in butter and sugar, then covered them with pastry and baked the tart, inverting it when it came out. The Daily Maverick calls this the account "most widely believed", and notes that the stories are plural and that the truth of any of them is uncertain. Wikipedia treats the accidental creation as a popular myth <Cite ref="dm-tatin"/>, <Cite ref="wiki-tatin"/>.

The documented record is thinner. A similar dish, the tarte aux pommes à la Solognote, already existed and took its name from the Sologne region around Lamotte-Beuvron <Cite ref="dm-tatin"/>. The first printed mention of the tart is from 1903, in the bulletin of a geographical society in the Cher, and the sisters never published the recipe or called it tarte Tatin <Cite ref="dm-tatin"/>.

The best-known anecdote about the tart's arrival in Paris is the Maxim's account, in which its owner Louis Vaudable says he learned the recipe while hired as a gardener. The Daily Maverick treats it as doubtful, since Vaudable was born in 1902, when the sisters were nearing retirement <Cite ref="dm-tatin"/>.
