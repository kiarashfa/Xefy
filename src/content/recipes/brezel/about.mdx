The Laugenbrezel is a knotted yeasted roll, dipped in a dilute lye bath before it is baked. The Austrian federal ministry describes the bath as a dilute sodium hydroxide solution, of two to six per cent under the food code, in which the piece is dipped briefly. The lye stays on the surface, and in the oven its caustic effect is lost <Cite ref="bmluk-brezel"/>. The lye is what gives the crust its colour and its flavour.

Most cooks outside Germany and Austria use a bath of bicarbonate of soda in its place. That is the easier route, but it is a weaker one: the crust browns more pale and lacks the gloss and depth that the lye gives, so the lye method is the one this recipe follows.

The origin is told in several ways, and the sources do not agree. Der Freitag calls the story of a baker from Bad Urach, who is said to have saved himself in the fifteenth century with pretzels dipped in lye, a legend <Cite ref="freitag-brezel"/>. The Austrian ministry places it among numerous stories, and Erlebe Bayern gives the earliest known depictions of the knot as a Last Supper scene in lectionaries at the Benedictine monastery of St Peter in Salzburg <Cite ref="erlebe-brezel"/>.

The Munich story of 1839 is the best known. It says that the baker Anton Nepomuk Pfannenbrenner dipped his pretzels in the lye he used to clean his baking trays, instead of sugar water. Erlebe Bayern presents it as the true story, without a source <Cite ref="erlebe-brezel"/>. Der Freitag calls it another myth <Cite ref="freitag-brezel"/>. The name is traced to the Latin for arm, brachium <Cite ref="freitag-brezel"/>.
