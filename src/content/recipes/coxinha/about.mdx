The story told about coxinha's invention is specific enough to sound
documented: at a royal residence in Limeira, in the countryside of São
Paulo state, a son of Isabel, Princess Imperial of Brazil, would eat nothing
but chicken drumsticks. When the cook ran short of real ones, she shredded
meat from the rest of the bird, shaped it with mashed potato into the form
of a drumstick and fried it. The boy loved it so much that Empress Teresa
Cristina is said to have travelled to Limeira herself to taste the new
delicacy <Cite ref="wiki-coxinha"/><Cite ref="aventuras-coxinha"/>.

The story does not survive contact with the documentary record. Princess
Isabel had three sons — Pedro de Alcântara, Luís Maria Filipe and Antônio
Gastão Filipe — and all three are recorded as healthy children who lived at
the Paço Isabel, now Rio de Janeiro's Guanabara Palace, until the
Proclamation of the Republic in 1889, not on a farm in the São Paulo
countryside with a fussy eater among them
<Cite ref="aventuras-coxinha"/>. Its fullest telling traces to a single
local-history book by Nadir Cavazin, and even Wikipedia's own account of the
snack's more recognisable, breaded and fried modern form hedges its date and
place of origin as something that "may have" happened in nineteenth-century
Limeira, rather than something established
<Cite ref="wiki-coxinha"/><Cite ref="aventuras-coxinha"/>.

More plausible accounts point elsewhere entirely. One holds that coxinha was
developed during São Paulo's industrialisation as a cheap, durable way to
stretch chicken further, sold at factory gates as a quick meal for workers
rather than invented in any noble kitchen at all <Cite ref="aventuras-coxinha"/>.
The shape itself may also be older than any Brazilian claim on it: the
French chef Antonin Carême published a recipe for pear-shaped chicken
croquettes in his 1831 cookbook, a precedent that predates every version of
the Limeira story by decades <Cite ref="aventuras-coxinha"/>.

What is not in question is the snack's later trajectory: from a contested
origin story to a fixture of Brazilian bakeries, bars and street stalls
nationwide, sold well beyond any one town's claim on its invention. Whatever
its true beginning, the coxinha eaten today is a mass, everyday food, its
supposed aristocratic origin worn as folklore rather than pedigree.
