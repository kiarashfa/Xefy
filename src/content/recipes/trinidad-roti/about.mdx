Dhalpuri roti reached Trinidad with the indentured labourers brought from
India over the second half of the nineteenth century and into the early
twentieth, most of them Bhojpuri speakers from what is now Bihar and eastern
Uttar Pradesh <Cite ref="wikipedia-dal-puri"/> <Cite ref="caribbean-camera-dhalpuri"/>.
Filmmaker Richard Fung's research into the dish's history found that it
travelled with them as a marker of home even though, in Bihar itself, it is
largely a festival food rather than one made day to day
<Cite ref="caribbean-camera-dhalpuri"/>.

What Trinidad made of it departed from the dish those labourers had known.
The Caribbean version is built on white flour rather than whole wheat, its
split-pea filling is ground rather than left whole, it is cooked on a flat
griddle rather than fried, and it is made larger than its Indian counterpart
<Cite ref="wikipedia-dal-puri"/> <Cite ref="caribbean-camera-dhalpuri"/>:
differences substantial enough that dhalpuri is best understood as its own
Caribbean dish rather than a regional recipe for the same bread.

In Trinidad, dhalpuri became the everyday wrapper for curry rather than a
dish eaten on its own, folded around curried meat, seafood or vegetables to
make roti a handheld meal, a use that has since carried the bread, and the
word "roti" with it, to the wider Caribbean diaspora.
