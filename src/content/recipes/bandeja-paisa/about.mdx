Bandeja paisa is usually introduced as ancient farm food, fuel for
nineteenth-century miners and field labourers in Antioquia who needed a
calorie-dense plate to get through a physical day's work. The individual
foods on the tray do have that kind of pedigree: beans, rice, pork and
maize were all staples of the region's rural diet, traditionally eaten as a
bean soup alongside a separate "seco" plate of rice, fried pork rind and a
corn arepa <Cite ref="alponiente-bandeja-paisa"/>.

The tray as it exists today, though, is very likely a newer invention than
its reputation suggests, and its documented history begins not in Antioquia
but in Bogotá. The name "bandeja paisa" itself was coined only in 1950, at
a meeting of the hotel and tourism association Cotelco assembling a manual
of Colombia's typical dishes, when its delegates found that Antioquia's
own everyday plate had no settled name of its own
<Cite ref="radionacional-bandeja-paisa"/>. One account of how the dish took
its combined, tray-served form points to a Bogotá restaurant, El Zaguán de
las Aguas, where Antioquian staples were piled together onto large plates
for a tourist-facing event rather than served separately as at home
<Cite ref="alponiente-bandeja-paisa"/>.

The distinction matters for what the dish actually represents. Its
ingredients are genuinely rooted in Antioquian farm cooking; the platter
itself, piled improbably high and now one of the more expensive dishes on
a Colombian menu, is closer to a twentieth-century, Bogotá-shaped tribute
to that cooking than to a meal any nineteenth-century miner would have
recognised on his own plate.
