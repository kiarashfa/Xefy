The name is a Hiligaynon word for a method rather than a dish: *inasal*
means to grill or roast over live coals <Cite ref="sunstar-inasal"/>.
Grilling over coals in Iloilo predates the dish's modern Bacolod branding by
decades: the Filipino photographer Félix Laureano's 1895 album of Iloilo and
Panay daily life, *Recuerdos de Filipinas*, is the earliest surviving
photographic record of the region's markets and daily customs
<Cite ref="villanova-laureano"/>.

Which of the two Western Visayas cities the dish properly belongs to is
still argued rather than settled. Wikipedia's own account credits Bacolod's
version to Banoy Velez, who is said to have brought it to the city from
Oton, near Iloilo, in 1946 — Bacolod's inasal reads as a touch more sour,
Iloilo's as sweeter, and Iloilo has its own claim to having originated the
dish first <Cite ref="wiki-inasal"/> <Cite ref="sunstar-inasal"/>. Bacolod's
version became a citywide fixture along Cuadra Street, later renamed
Manokan Country, or "Chicken Alley," a strip of open-air grills built
entirely around the dish <Cite ref="sunstar-inasal"/>.

That regional identity was made official only recently: in 2022, Bacolod
City passed a local ordinance recognising chicken inasal as an important
cultural property of the city, formalising an association that had built up
over decades of Manokan Country grills rather than resolving the older
dispute with Iloilo <Cite ref="sunstar-inasal"/>.

What both cities agree on is the method: chicken marinated in vinegar,
calamansi juice and garlic, then grilled over coals while repeatedly basted
with oil coloured deep orange by annatto, which is what gives the finished
chicken its distinctive colour rather than any chilli or paprika
<Cite ref="wiki-inasal"/> <Cite ref="sunstar-inasal"/>.
