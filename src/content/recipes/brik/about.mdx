Brik is eaten across Tunisia in a form specific enough to be recognisable at a
glance: a thin sheet of pastry folded around a filling and a whole raw egg,
then dropped into hot oil for just long enough to set the white and leave the
yolk soft. Its exact age is hard to pin down — the dish is old enough that its
origins are, in its own Wikipedia entry's words, difficult to trace directly,
though it is thought to reach back several centuries <Cite ref="wiki-brik"/>.
What is better documented is its family: brik descends from Ottoman *börek*,
the layered pastry that spread through the empire's North African provinces
from the sixteenth century onward, arriving alongside relatives such as
Algerian *bourek* and Moroccan *briouat* <Cite ref="carthage-brik"/>.

Tunisian cooks took that inheritance somewhere the rest of the family did not
follow. The pastry itself got thinner, taking on the semolina-and-flour
malsouka sheet used nowhere else in quite the same way, and the fold tightened
from börek's roll into a flat triangle or half-moon that fries crisp rather
than layered <Cite ref="carthage-brik"/>. Tunisian Jewish cooks call the same
sheet a warqa rather than a malsouka — the two names for one pastry, used
interchangeably depending on who is speaking <Cite ref="wiki-brik"/>.

The detail that defines the dish, the whole egg fried until the white sets and
the yolk does not, has no securely documented inventor or date attached to it
in either source consulted here; it is generally treated as a Tunisian
addition rather than an Ottoman one. Djerba's Sephardic Jewish community, in
particular, is credited with developing its own brik traditions over
generations, though the details of that development are not closely dated
<Cite ref="carthage-brik"/>. What both sources agree on is that the egg is not
a garnish — it is the reason a diner eats brik immediately, before the yolk
inside has time to finish cooking on its own residual heat.
