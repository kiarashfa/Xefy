The dish is Roman and, in its original form, austere. Alfredo di Lelio is
credited with creating it at his restaurant in Rome in the early twentieth
century, built from nothing more than fresh fettuccine, butter and
Parmigiano-Reggiano — no cream anywhere in it, the sauce formed entirely by
tossing the hot pasta hard enough to emulsify the butter and cheese into
something that looks creamy without containing any <Cite ref="wiki-alfredo"/>.

Its American career began with a honeymoon. The actors Mary Pickford and
Douglas Fairbanks are said to have eaten it at di Lelio's restaurant during
their 1920 honeymoon, loved it enough to ask for the recipe, and helped make
it famous once they brought it home <Cite ref="foodrepublic-alfredo"/>. What
travelled across the Atlantic did not stay the same dish for long: back in
America, working with butter and cheese of a different character than the
ones available in Rome, and without the tableside technique that emulsified
the sauce in the first place, cooks added cream to stabilise and enrich it,
and often garlic and extra cheese besides — the dish now sold as "Alfredo"
almost everywhere outside Italy <Cite ref="italysegreta-alfredo"/>
<Cite ref="foodrepublic-alfredo"/>.

The two are close enough in name to be mistaken for versions of one dish, but
they are not interchangeable: the Roman original has no cream in it at all,
and reaches its texture through technique rather than a second dairy
ingredient. What follows here is the American version, built deliberately
around cream rather than in place of the technique it replaced.
