Moules marinière is mussels steamed open in white wine with shallot, parsley and butter. It is one of the most popular mussel dishes in the world <Cite ref="fdl-moules"/>. The name, *à la marinière*, means in the manner of sailors.

Bouchot mussel farming, in which the mussels grow on wooden posts, is traced in popular accounts to a shipwrecked sailor, Patrick Walton, who is said to have been wrecked in 1235 in the Bay of Aiguillon. Connexion France calls the story a legend, and notes that some accounts make Walton a Scotsman rather than an Irishman <Cite ref="conn-moules"/>.

When the dish first appeared is undated.

Fine Dining Lovers gives the usual preparation as white wine, garlic or shallot, parsley, and oil or butter <Cite ref="fdl-moules"/>. It also describes a Provençal mussel dish as similar to the marinière, and says that in the eighteenth century a luxury version used champagne in place of the wine <Cite ref="fdl-moules"/>.
