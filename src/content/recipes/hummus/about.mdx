Hummus is Arabic for chickpea. The dish is properly *ḥummuṣ bi-ṭaḥīna* — chickpeas
with tahini — and the shortening is an English convenience that quietly renames a
sauce after one of its parts.

Its documented history runs back at least to the thirteenth century. Kitāb
al-Wuṣla ilā l-ḥabīb, a Syrian collection of some 635 recipes sometimes ascribed
to the Aleppan historian Ibn al-Adim, contains a chickpea preparation that any
modern cook would recognise <Cite ref="wiki-wusla"/>. A roughly contemporary
Cairo manuscript records a dish called *ḥummuṣ kasa*, built on puréed chickpeas
and tahini — but soured with vinegar rather than lemon, heavily spiced, thick with
herbs and nuts, and containing no garlic at all <Cite ref="eats-wusla"/>. The
ancestry is real; the continuity is looser than it is usually claimed to be.

The modern argument is about nationality rather than antiquity. Hummus has been
the subject of a long public dispute between Lebanon and Israel — competing
claims of ownership, competing record-breaking batches, an attempt at protected
status — and the quarrel has been serious enough to acquire a name, the hummus
wars <Cite ref="pha-hummus-wars"/>. It is not a question this page can settle, and
the honest position is that a dish eaten across the Levant, Egypt and southern
Turkey for centuries does not have a single national author.

What is not in dispute is what makes it good, and it is mostly texture. The
difference between an ordinary hummus and a very good one is not the ratio of
tahini to chickpea but how completely the chickpeas have been broken down — which
is why the recipe here cooks them well past tender and blends them hot.
