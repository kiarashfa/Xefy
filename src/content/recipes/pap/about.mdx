Pap is made from a grain that has no deep history in Southern Africa at all.
Maize reached the continent from the Americas by way of Portuguese trade, and
before it arrived, sorghum, millet and other indigenous grains were the
staple cereals across most of sub-Saharan Africa <Cite ref="wiki-ugali"/>.
Maize spread because it suited existing farming practice — it could be grown
using methods already familiar from sorghum, while yielding considerably
more per plant — and it kept without refrigeration, which mattered enormously
before rural electrification and still shapes why maize meal sits in shops
and pantries across the region today <Cite ref="wiki-ugali"/><Cite ref="wiki-mielie-meal"/>.
Sorghum and millet were not displaced quickly; the full switch to maize as the
dominant staple across the region is generally placed in the second half of
the twentieth century <Cite ref="wiki-ugali"/>.

What South Africans call pap is one name among a great many for essentially
the same dish. The stiff maize porridge eaten across the continent goes by
ugali in Kenya, nsima in Malawi, sadza in Zimbabwe and dozens of other names
depending on the language, all describing maize meal stirred into water and
worked to a dense, smooth mass <Cite ref="wiki-ugali"/>. Pap itself is not one
fixed dish but a family of consistencies recognised under their own names in
South African kitchens, including the crumbly uphuthu and the dry, granular
umphokoqo alongside pap proper <Cite ref="mzansi-mealie-meal"/>; the very
stiff, dense stywepap given here is worked hard enough to stand on a plate
rather than run off it, and is eaten as the starch alongside a grilled or
stewed main. A registered dietitian writing for the South African outlet
Health For Mzansi describes mealie meal as a staple present in most South
African households regardless of region <Cite ref="mzansi-mealie-meal"/>.
