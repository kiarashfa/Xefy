The name comes from the Arabic *tābil*, seasoning, and behind it an Aramaic root
*t-b-l*; in Lebanon the connection is made directly to *tatbil*, to season
<Cite ref="wiki-tabbouleh"/><Cite ref="lebanon-traveler"/>. The dish belongs to the
mountains of what are now Lebanon and Syria, where edible wild and cultivated herbs
were a substantial part of the rural diet long before this particular combination of
them existed <Cite ref="wiki-tabbouleh"/>. The Beqaa Valley grew a wheat variety,
salamouni, reckoned in the mid-nineteenth century to make the best bulgur
<Cite ref="wiki-tabbouleh"/>.

The one thing worth being firm about is the proportion. Tabbouleh is a herb salad, and
parsley is its main ingredient rather than its garnish <Cite ref="lebanon-traveler"/>;
the grain is a minor component that gives it body. Versions made outside the region
routinely invert this into a bulgur salad with herbs stirred through, which is a
different dish and has its own names elsewhere — the Turkish *kısır* and the Armenian
*eetch* are both properly grain-led <Cite ref="wiki-tabbouleh"/>.

It is not a single fixed recipe even at home. Lebanese regional versions substitute
green fava beans, split lentils or eryngo for parts of the usual mixture, and some are
sharpened with pomegranate or verjuice rather than lemon <Cite ref="lebanon-traveler"/>.
What travels is the idea: herbs cut fine, something sour, good oil, and just enough
cracked wheat to give the fork something to hold.

Its standing in Lebanon is formal enough to have a date attached. Lebanese National
Tabbouleh Day has been marked on the first Saturday of July since 2001
<Cite ref="wiki-tabbouleh"/>. The dish reached wider Western notice only in the second
half of the twentieth century — the word entered English in the 1950s, and Syrian Jewish
versions arrived in Israel and the United States in the 1970s
<Cite ref="wiki-tabbouleh"/>.
