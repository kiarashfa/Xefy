Lechón asado's marinade travelled further than the pig did. Mojo takes its
name from the Portuguese word for sauce, carried to the Canary Islands by
settlers from Madeira, and it was Canarian emigration — heavy to Cuba and the
wider Spanish Caribbean over the colonial and early modern periods — that
brought the sauce across the Atlantic, where it took on sour orange in place
of the citrus and peppers used at home <Cite ref="gran-canaria-mojo"/>. Pigs
themselves were a Spanish import to an island that had none, introduced by
colonisers along with the farming practices to raise them, and pork roasted
whole became, over generations, the centrepiece of the Cuban Christmas table
<Cite ref="cubanhistory-nochebuena"/>.

Cooking a lechón was, and largely still is, a ritual rather than a task
handed to one cook. Preparation traditionally began the night before
Nochebuena, when the pork was rubbed and re-rubbed with mojo until the sour
orange, garlic, oregano and cumin had worked into every fold of the meat,
before the whole pig or a large cut of it went onto the fire the next morning
<Cite ref="cubanhistory-nochebuena"/>. In Cuba that fire was traditionally a
backyard pit; among the Cuban diaspora in the United States, the same slow
roast is now as often done in La Caja China, a wooden charcoal roasting box
that has become almost as much a fixture of the holiday as the pork itself.

The tradition has not run in an unbroken line. Cuba's government eliminated
official Christmas festivities in 1969, and it was not until Pope John Paul
II's 1997 visit to the island that December the twenty-fifth was restored as
a non-working holiday <Cite ref="cubanhistory-nochebuena"/> — through which
Nochebuena itself, and the lechón at its centre, continued to be marked in
Cuban homes regardless of its official status.
