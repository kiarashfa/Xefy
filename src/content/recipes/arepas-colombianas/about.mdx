The arepa is old enough to predate the two nations that now argue over it.
Its name comes from *erepa*, the word for cornbread in Cumanagoto, an
indigenous language of what is now eastern Venezuela
<Cite ref="wikipedia-arepa"/> <Cite ref="latercera-arepa"/>, and the corn
cake itself was already being made by indigenous peoples across the region
that Colombia and Venezuela would eventually become, long before either
border existed <Cite ref="latercera-arepa"/>.

What has stayed remarkably constant is the arepa itself: ground corn,
formed into a disc and cooked on a hot surface, in a form said to differ
little from what pre-Columbian cooks would recognise. What has diverged is
the two countries' modern versions: Colombian arepas are typically thinner
and often eaten plain, split and buttered, or worked through with cheese;
Venezuelan arepas are made noticeably thicker and are more often split open
and stuffed after cooking. Both countries treat the dish as a matter of
national pride, and scholars and food professionals on both sides of the
border have taken to describing it not as a prize to be won but as a
heritage the two countries share <Cite ref="latercera-arepa"/>.
