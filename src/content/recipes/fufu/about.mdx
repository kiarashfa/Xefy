Fufu takes its name from Twi, the Akan language of Ghana, where historian
Miller reads *fufu* as meaning simply "white" — a plausible description of the
pale, pounded cassava dough most associated with the dish today
<Cite ref="wiki-fufu"/>. That association with cassava is, on the timescale of
the dish itself, a recent one. West African communities had been pounding
starchy roots into a smooth dough for a very long time before cassava arrived
at all, working with whatever the region offered — yam, plantain and cocoyam
chief among them <Cite ref="remitly-fufu"/>.

Cassava only enters the story in the sixteenth century, brought across the
Atlantic from South America by Portuguese traders <Cite ref="wiki-fufu"/>. It
proved easier to grow than yam on poor soil and more resistant to drought,
and in southern Ghana it gradually settled into a partnership with plantain
rather than replacing the older starches outright — the pairing used here is
that same cassava-and-plantain combination <Cite ref="wiki-fufu"/>. Elsewhere
the balance tipped differently: fufu made solely from fermented cassava is
the norm in much of Nigeria, while cocoyam remains the base, alongside
cassava, in other parts of the region <Cite ref="wiki-fufu"/>. The pounding
itself — starch and water worked by hand until it turns elastic and pulls
cleanly from the mortar — is the one constant across all of these, older than
any particular root that goes into it.
