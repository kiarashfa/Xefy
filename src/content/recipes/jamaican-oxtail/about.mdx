Oxtail earned its place in Caribbean and Black American kitchens by being
worth almost nothing. During slavery, the cuts of the animal considered
worth selling went to slavers and buyers; what was left over, including the
tail, went to enslaved cooks, alongside other offal like pig's feet and
chitterlings <Cite ref="cuisinenoir-oxtail"/>. Those cooks turned it into
braises, soups and gravies, discovering, out of necessity rather than
choice, that the bone's high collagen content was exactly what a long, slow
cook needed to turn a tough, cheap cut into something tender
<Cite ref="cuisinenoir-oxtail"/>.

That economics held for generations. As late as the 1960s and 70s, oxtail
sold for around twenty-nine cents a pound in the United States, a price that
reflected its status as an undesirable, throwaway part of the cow rather
than any shortage of demand <Cite ref="cuisinenoir-oxtail"/>. Within living
memory in Jamaica, meat shops gave oxtail away free to regular customers, and
home cooks reached for chicken, beef or mutton before they reached for it
<Cite ref="gleaner-oxtail"/>. Stewed oxtail with butter beans, served over
rice, is eaten as a main dish across Jamaica and the wider Caribbean today,
worked into the island's core seasoning vocabulary of allspice, scotch
bonnet, thyme and scallion.

The dish's status has since reversed entirely. Global demand for a cut once
priced as scrap has pushed oxtail toward luxury pricing; by the mid-2020s it
was selling for fourteen to fifteen dollars a pound in American markets, with
pandemic-era supply disruptions accelerating a rise that gentrification of
the ingredient had already begun <Cite ref="cuisinenoir-oxtail"/>. Import
disruptions and diaspora demand had already more than doubled American retail
prices in a matter of weeks once before, in 2021, when oxtail briefly reached
nineteen dollars a pound in some stores <Cite ref="gleaner-oxtail"/>. The stew
itself has not changed to match; it remains a braise built on patience and a
handful of Caribbean seasonings rather than an expensive ingredient list, and
its price today says more about the cut's cachet elsewhere than about
anything in the Jamaican kitchen that made it worth cooking in the first
place.
