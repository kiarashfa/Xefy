Roast potatoes have surprisingly little documented history of their own; what
can be dated is the cookery-writing tradition that fixed the method still
used today. Isabella Beeton's *Book of Household Management*, published in
1861 after first appearing as a magazine column from 1857, gives an
instruction that would be recognised in any British kitchen now: peel and
halve or quarter the potatoes, parboil them, strain and dry them over a low
heat, then put them into hot dripping in a roasting tin, either on their own
or in the tin already holding the joint <Cite ref="beeton-1861"/>. Beeton's
book followed Eliza Acton's *Modern Cookery*, published in 1845, which had
already introduced the now-standard format of listing ingredients and
timings ahead of method: the template Beeton's own roast potato
instruction still takes <Cite ref="toh-victorian"/><Cite ref="beeton-1861"/>.

Dripping itself was not a luxury ingredient in this period but an ordinary
one; a simple working diet in Victorian Britain ran on little more than
bread, dripping, vegetables and tea <Cite ref="toh-victorian"/>, which makes
Beeton's roasting method less an invention than a written-down version of
something already common practice in kitchens that rendered their own fat
from every joint they cooked.

What the record does not settle is when parboiling before roasting became
standard, or who first roughened the surface deliberately for a craggier
crust; both are simply present, fully formed, by the time the method is
written down <Cite ref="beeton-1861"/>. The dish's core technique (a first cook in water, a
second in screaming-hot fat) has not needed to change since.
