Sancocho's Spanish ancestry, repeated on this site for its Dominican and
other Caribbean relatives, is not accepted without argument closer to
home. The standard version traces the dish to cocido, descended in turn
from adafina, the Sephardic Sabbath stew, carried to the Americas by
Spanish colonists and reworked locally
<Cite ref="britannica-sancocho"/>. Colombian gastronomic anthropologists
have pushed back on this lineage for the Colombian dish specifically,
pointing out that its two defining ingredients, plantain and yuca, are not
Spanish at all — plantain is Asian in origin and yuca American — which
makes a straightforward line from a Spanish stew to a Colombian one harder
to draw than the standard story suggests
<Cite ref="senalmemoria-sancocho"/>.

What is not disputed is how specifically Colombian the dish's regional
variations are. In Ginebra, in the Valle del Cauca, chicken became the
sancocho's defining meat in part because chicken itself was one of the
more sought-after and expensive animals available during the colonial
period, a prestige that has settled into local tradition long after the
economics behind it changed <Cite ref="senalmemoria-sancocho"/>. Along
Colombia's coasts and rivers, sancocho is built on fish instead, and on
the Atlantic coast a version exists with as many as nine different meats
in the pot <Cite ref="senalmemoria-sancocho"/> — a reminder that "sancocho"
names a method and a mood as much as a fixed recipe.

That mood runs past the kitchen. Sancocho in Colombia is associated with
the paseo de olla, a communal outing built around cooking and eating the
dish together outdoors, and the same image of a shared pot has been
borrowed by social movements, where gathering around one cooking pot
stands for a shared cause rather than a shared meal
<Cite ref="senalmemoria-sancocho"/>.
