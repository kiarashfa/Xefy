Baked pasta and cheese has an Italian paper trail going back to the medieval
cookbook *Liber de Coquina*, and the dish reached American tables by way of
France rather than directly from Italy <Cite ref="historynet-hemings"/>. The
credit for its American arrival is usually given to Thomas Jefferson, who
owned a macaroni-making machine and served the dish at Monticello and at the
President's House <Cite ref="monticello-macaroni"/>. That framing understates
the person who actually made it happen.

James Hemings, enslaved by Jefferson from childhood, travelled with him to
Paris in 1784 and trained there under French chefs, including time with the
caterer Monsieur Combeaux and in the kitchen of the Prince de Condé
<Cite ref="historynet-hemings"/>. It was this training that let Hemings adapt
the French version of macaroni and cheese into the dish Jefferson's household
became known for; Jefferson supplied the opportunity and the imported
ingredients, but the culinary skill and the cooking itself were Hemings's.
Jefferson agreed to free Hemings in a 1796 deed of manumission, on the
condition that he first train a replacement cook; Hemings died in 1801,
several years after gaining his freedom <Cite ref="historynet-hemings"/>.

The dish's later, thoroughly American form — elbow macaroni bound in a
stovetop or baked cheese sauce rather than the French original's dressing —
developed over the following century as cheddar and processed cheeses became
the more common household cheeses, well after Hemings's own version had
already established the dish at the American table.
