Momo is generally traced to Tibet and understood to have reached Kathmandu
through the Newar traders of the Kathmandu Valley, who carried on an extensive
trade with Lhasa from as early as Nepal's Licchavi period and are held to have
brought the recipe home with them <Cite ref="smithsonian-momo"/>. No written
record fixes when this happened. As one Kathmandu chef quoted on the dish's
history put it, what survives is "oral history, trade memory and social
clues" rather than a documented date <Cite ref="smithsonian-momo"/>.

That account is not the only one in circulation. A popular legend credits the
seventh-century Nepali princess Bhrikuti with carrying momo to Tibet in the
opposite direction, as part of her marriage into the Tibetan court, while
other historians hold that dumplings reached the Tibetan plateau from China
under the Mongols in the thirteenth century and travelled to Nepal from
there <Cite ref="wiki-momo"/>. The three stories do not agree on either the
period or the direction of travel, and none is treated as settled.

Trade between Nepal and Tibet continued into the twentieth century until
China's annexation of Tibet closed the border in the 1960s and ended the
Newar trading presence in Lhasa, by which point momo was already established
across the Kathmandu Valley <Cite ref="smithsonian-momo"/>. Nepali cooks are
credited with the dish's two most distinctive departures from the Tibetan
original: working more spice into the filling, and jhol momo, in which the
dumplings are served sitting in a thin, spiced broth rather than alongside a
separate dipping achar <Cite ref="smithsonian-momo"/><Cite ref="wiki-momo"/>.
