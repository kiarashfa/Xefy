The story holds that early versions of tourtière were stuffed with *tourte*,
the French name for the passenger pigeon, a bird so abundant in early
Canada that it was easy to catch and simple to justify putting in a pie
<Cite ref="cff-tourtiere"/>. It is a tidy story, and it is not the only one
on offer. The simpler competing explanation is that *tourte* is an old
French word for pie, and a dish baked in a *tourtière* (the deep pan the
word originally named) eventually took the pan's name for itself, the way
a casserole is named for its pot rather than its contents
<Cite ref="wiki-tourtiere"/><Cite ref="sidney-tourtiere"/>. Neither theory
has been definitively settled, and a museum's account of the dispute treats
both explanations as still genuinely open rather than one having won out
over the other <Cite ref="sidney-tourtiere"/>.

What is on firmer ground is the pie's association with French settlement
along the Saint Lawrence from the 1600s, and its place at the centre of
Quebec's Christmas Eve *réveillon*, a late feast held after midnight mass,
long enough established among French-speaking Catholics to be a fixture of
the season rather than a novelty <Cite ref="cff-tourtiere"/>. Its first
appearance in print is later: a recipe using pork, mutton and veal appears in
*La cuisinière canadienne*, a cookbook from 1840 <Cite ref="cff-tourtiere"/>.

The pie was never one fixed recipe, and still is not. The version from
Saguenay–Lac-Saint-Jean is made with cubed rather than ground meat, cooked
slowly in a deep dish with potatoes and a thicker crust, quite different
from the finely ground, cinnamon-and-clove-seasoned filling more familiar
outside that region <Cite ref="wiki-tourtiere"/><Cite ref="sidney-tourtiere"/>.
What holds across the regional versions is less a recipe than an occasion: a
spiced meat pie, however it is filled, timed to the one night of the year
built around staying up for it.
