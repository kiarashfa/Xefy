The earliest known written recipe for an apple pie is English, not American, and considerably older than any national claim on the dish: it appears in a
collection dated to 1381, calling for figs, raisins, pears and saffron alongside the apples, all held in an unsweetened "coffin" of pastry meant to
contain the filling rather than be eaten with it <Cite ref="smithsonian-applepie"/>. That recipe's own influences reached further still, drawing on
French, Dutch and Ottoman cooking rather than arising in England alone <Cite ref="mentalfloss-applepie"/>.

The apple itself is no more American than the recipe. No apple native to North America is suited to eating or baking — only small, sharp crab apples
grew wild there — so colonists arriving at Jamestown in the seventeenth century brought cuttings and seed from Europe to grow the fruit they already
knew <Cite ref="smithsonian-applepie"/>. Those trees did extraordinarily well: by around 1800, American growers had produced something like fourteen
thousand distinct apple varieties, the raw material a pie recipe finally had to work with <Cite ref="smithsonian-applepie"/>.

The pie's American identity is a later, and more deliberate, construction. Soldiers baking pies from apples scavenged on Civil War-era farms gave the
dish a patriotic association it had not had before <Cite ref="mentalfloss-applepie"/>, and in 1902 the *New York Times* went further, calling apple
pie "the American synonym for prosperity" <Cite ref="mentalfloss-applepie"/>. The phrase that stuck, though, came out of the Second World War: American
soldiers, asked by journalists what they were fighting for, answered "mom and apple pie," and the pairing of the two has stood for national comfort
ever since <Cite ref="smithsonian-applepie"/>.
