The word "picanha" is usually traced to *picana*, a pointed pole used across
the Iberian Peninsula to drive cattle, applied to the animal's hindquarters
— the same part of the animal the cut itself comes from
<Cite ref="wiki-picanha"/><Cite ref="revistapb-picanha"/>. The connection between the tool and the meat is
plausible rather than documented, and it is one of at least two competing
etymologies in circulation, which is a familiar problem for a word that
entered everyday Portuguese long before anyone thought to write down where
it came from.

The cut's history as something sold on its own, rather than left attached to
the larger rump roast, is easier to pin down and more surprising: it appears
to be a mid-twentieth-century São Paulo invention rather than an old gaúcho
tradition. Laszlo Wessel, a Hungarian immigrant who opened a butcher shop in
the Bixiga neighbourhood of São Paulo in 1957, is credited by his son with
being the first butcher to separate picanha from the alcatra, the larger
rump cut it comes from, around 1959 — reportedly at the request of German
immigrant customers who wanted the cut for Tafelspitz, an Austrian boiled
beef dish, rather than for grilling at all <Cite ref="revistapb-picanha"/>.
Only from that separate cut did picanha go on to become the centrepiece of
Brazilian churrascarias.

From there its path outward is clearer. Brazilian steakhouses carried the
cut abroad as they expanded internationally from the 1990s onward, and it
reached Portugal and other markets largely through Brazilian communities and
restaurants rather than through any older shared tradition
<Cite ref="wiki-picanha"/><Cite ref="revistapb-picanha"/>. Because only a small portion of each animal
yields a true picanha, its international fame has made it a comparatively
scarce and prized cut wherever it travelled, a long way from a butcher's
shop in Bixiga separating it out for customers who wanted it boiled, not
grilled.
