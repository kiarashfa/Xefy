"Velvet" cakes — named for a fine, soft crumb rather than any particular colour
— were a Victorian-era category on both sides of the Atlantic, made with cocoa
powder to give a tender texture unlike a plain yellow or white cake
<Cite ref="splendidtable-redvelvet"/>. The reddish tinge now considered
essential to red velvet began as a genuine, if unreliable, side effect of
baking: natural, non-Dutched cocoa powder can react with an acid such as
buttermilk or vinegar in a way that shifts its colour slightly toward red, and
early twentieth-century bakers noticed and leaned into it. By around 1911 a
"velvet cocoa cake" using this reaction was already circulating
<Cite ref="splendidtable-redvelvet"/>.

The cake's best-known origin story credits the Waldorf-Astoria Hotel in New
York with inventing it in the 1920s, a claim the hotel's own cookbook
repeats. The claim does not hold up: the earliest documented cake using a
large, deliberate dose of red food colouring — as opposed to the faint,
natural reddish-brown of the cocoa reaction — is a "Waldorf red cake" recipe
that only appears in 1959, by which point the Waldorf-Astoria had never
actually served anything resembling today's red velvet cake
<Cite ref="untapped-redvelvet"/>. What the hotel is more plausibly connected to
is a later urban legend in which a customer was charged for the recipe and
retaliated by mailing it to everyone she knew — a story with no documented
source, told about more than one Waldorf dish, and not confirmed by either
source consulted here.

The modern cake — deep red from added colouring rather than from chemistry
alone, and typically finished with cream cheese frosting — is a mid-twentieth
century American product built on top of a much older, much paler cocoa cake
tradition <Cite ref="splendidtable-redvelvet"/>.
