Fermenting tea leaves to eat rather than to drink is, on the documentary
record, a practice of Myanmar origin rather than one derived from another
culture's tea tradition <Cite ref="joef-laphet"/>. The leaves are steamed,
packed airtight and left to sour slowly, for months rather than days, into
a food in its own right rather than a byproduct of drinking tea.

Lahpet's oldest documented social role was not as an everyday dish at all.
It served as a formal peace offering between warring Burmese kingdoms,
exchanged and eaten once a dispute had been settled as a way of sealing the
agreement <Cite ref="wiki-lahpet"/><Cite ref="joef-laphet"/>.

That symbolism of settlement and welcome is broadly the role lahpet still
holds today: the tray is offered to houseguests as a mark of hospitality
and remains an essential dish at traditional ceremonies, weddings and
novitiation rites among them
<Cite ref="wiki-lahpet"/><Cite ref="joef-laphet"/>, eaten as
*ahlu-lahpet* — the communal, build-your-own version of the tray, rather
than a plated salad served to one person.
