Ice cream thickened with an egg-yolk custard — sometimes called French-style —
is the older and more widespread of the two traditions, and it is the one that
travelled through Europe and into most classic recipes. Philadelphia developed
a distinct alternative during the nineteenth century: a base made from nothing
but cream, milk and sugar, with no eggs at all <Cite ref="today-phillyicecream"/>.

The Philadelphia style is not simply a simplification. Leaving out the egg
yolks removes the fat and emulsifiers that give custard-based ice cream its
characteristically dense, rich mouthfeel, producing something lighter and
cleaner-tasting instead — a difference that particularly suits a delicate
flavour like vanilla, which a heavy custard can otherwise mute
<Cite ref="today-phillyicecream"/>. The city's ice cream trade, driven by
brands such as Breyers and Bassett's, both founded in Philadelphia in the
nineteenth century, is generally credited with popularising the format and
giving it the city's name <Cite ref="phillymag-icecream"/>.

Despite its name, the Philadelphia style is not confined to Philadelphia: it is
also sold and described elsewhere in the United States as "American-style" ice
cream, in contrast to the custard-based style associated with France. Both
approaches are treated here as two legitimate versions of the same drink,
rather than one being a simplification of the other.
