Teff itself is genuinely ancient. It was domesticated in the Ethiopian and
Eritrean highlands, with cultivation generally dated to around the fourth
millennium BC, making it one of the earliest grains people brought under
cultivation anywhere <Cite ref="teton-teff"/>. That gives injera an old
ingredient, but not necessarily an old date of its own — teff can be milled
into porridge, roasted, or made into any number of things besides a fermented
flatbread, and none of that history proves when injera specifically began.

The dish itself is harder to date. It is commonly said to have originated
around 100 BC, but the botanists Robert B. Stewart and Asnake Getachew have
pointed out that this figure is folklore rather than anything confirmed in the
historical record, and that injera more plausibly arose by accident, when a
batch of teff flour was left wet for several days and fermented on its own
<Cite ref="wiki-injera"/>. The oldest physical evidence for anything like the
dish is archaeological rather than textual: mitads, the large flat griddles
injera is still cooked on, have been found at sites dating back to around 600
AD, alongside evidence that also points to shiro, the chickpea stew commonly
eaten with it <Cite ref="wiki-injera"/>. That leaves a real gap between teff's
deep cultivation history and the first hard evidence of the bread — one this
page has no way to close, and states as a gap rather than a settled date.

What the long fermentation does is not really in dispute, whatever its origin
story: days of wild fermentation build both the batter's sourness and the gas
that gives the cooked bread its distinctive surface of small, even bubbles,
known as its "eyes."
