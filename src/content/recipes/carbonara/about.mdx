Carbonara is a Roman dish and a young one. For a recipe so often described as
traditional, the striking thing about the record is how thin it is before the
Second World War: no cookbook, menu or newspaper appears to mention it at all
<Cite ref="wiki-carbonara"/>.

The dish surfaces immediately after. In 1950 the Turin newspaper *La Stampa*
reported that American officers had spent years hunting for spaghetti alla
carbonara in the restaurants of Trastevere, without troubling to say what it was
<Cite ref="wiki-carbonara"/>. The earliest recipe anyone has produced was printed
two years later, and not in Italy: it appears in *Vittles and Vice*, a 1952
Chicago cookbook by Patricia Bronté <Cite ref="wiki-carbonara"/>. Italy's own
first published version followed in *La Cucina Italiana* in 1954, and it is not
the dish as it is now made — that one used pancetta, garlic and Gruyère
<Cite ref="wiki-carbonara"/>.

The most repeated explanation ties it to Allied rations. American troops in Italy
had bacon and powdered egg, and one account credits a young Italian cook, Renato
Gualandi, with improvising something along these lines for an army dinner in 1944
<Cite ref="wiki-carbonara"/>. The food historian Luca Cesari places its birth in
Rome around the same period and for much the same reason
<Cite ref="gbc-carbonara"/>. None of this is settled, and the name itself — from
*carbonaro*, a charcoal burner — has attracted its own set of unevidenced
stories.

What the record does establish is that today's austerity is a recent achievement
rather than an ancient rule. Cream was ordinary in carbonara recipes for decades
and only fell out of favour in the 1990s <Cite ref="wiki-carbonara"/>. The
version now defended as the authentic one — guanciale not pancetta, Pecorino not
Parmigiano, no cream, no garlic — is better understood as a position the dish
arrived at than as the place it started.
