The chocolate chip cookie has what almost no famous dish has: a named author, an
address and a date. Ruth Graves Wakefield trained in household arts and worked as a
dietitian before she and her husband Kenneth bought a former toll house at Whitman,
Massachusetts, in 1930 and turned it into the Toll House Inn
<Cite ref="lemelson-wakefield"/>. The recipe reached print as Toll House Chocolate
Crunch Cookies in the 1938 edition of her *Toll House Tried and True Recipes*
<Cite ref="wiki-ccc"/>.

The part everybody repeats is the accident: short of an ingredient, she chopped up a
Nestlé bar and stirred it in expecting it to melt into the dough, and it refused
<Cite ref="lemelson-wakefield"/>. Wakefield's own accounts do not describe an accident.
Asked about it in the 1970s she said she had been serving a thin butterscotch nut cookie
with ice cream and set out to come up with something else, and described breaking a
semi-sweet bar apart with an ice pick alongside her pastry chef Sue Brides
<Cite ref="wiki-ccc"/>. Food writers have noted the obvious difficulty with the legend,
which is that she was a trained home economist running a professional kitchen
<Cite ref="wiki-ccc"/>.

The claim to invention is disputed on wider grounds too. Stella Parks, researching
*BraveTart*, found a chocolate jumbles recipe with much the same dough proportions and
grated chocolate through it, syndicated across American newspapers in 1877, and bakers
already selling something they called chocolate chip cookies in the 1920s
<Cite ref="takeout-parks"/>.

What followed is not in dispute and is the more consequential half of the story.
Nestlé arranged to print the recipe on its wrappers; Wakefield said she was promised a
dollar, and the retellings add a lifetime supply of chocolate <Cite ref="wiki-ccc"/>. By
the end of 1939 the company was scoring its bars into segments to be snapped off, and
soon after began selling small teardrops of chocolate it called morsels
<Cite ref="wiki-ccc"/>. The chocolate chip, in other words, was invented to fit the
cookie rather than the other way round.
