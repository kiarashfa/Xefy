Milanesa takes its name, and its basic idea, from Milan's cotoletta alla
milanese, a breaded cutlet that in its home city is traditionally cooked
bone-in and fried in butter <Cite ref="wiki-milanesa"/><Cite ref="lanacion-milanesa"/>.
Whether that dish or Vienna's schnitzel came first is its own long-running
dispute, tied up in an unverified legend about an Austrian field marshal
carrying the recipe home from Italy in the 1850s; no document has been found
to support it, and La Nación's own review of the claim warns that cities
competing over a dish's birthplace tend "to recreate past realities or
invent false accounts" rather than settle the question
<Cite ref="lanacion-milanesa"/>. That argument belongs to the cotoletta and
the schnitzel, not to what happened once the idea reached Argentina.

What happened there was immigration on a scale that reshaped the country's
food. Argentina absorbed one of the largest waves of Italian arrivals
anywhere outside Italy between 1870 and 1920, and that immigration, combined
with the quality and availability of Argentine beef, is what let the
Milanese preparation take root in home kitchens rather than stay a
restaurant dish <Cite ref="wiki-milanesa"/><Cite ref="lanacion-milanesa"/>.
Historians of Argentine food more broadly describe this period (Italian and
Spanish immigration converging in the same decades) as the point at which
received ideas about what counted as national cuisine were actively
contested and remade, rather than simply inherited from a colonial Spanish
table <Cite ref="cup-criolla"/>. The word itself is first documented in the
1914 eighth edition of Francisco Figueredo's *El libro de Cocina*, not yet as
a dish in its own right but as a modifier in recipe titles ("Costillas a la
milanesa" and "Escalopes de ternera a la milanesa"), with "milanesa"
appearing as a standalone noun only in later cookbooks, including Doña
Petrona's influential 1950 edition <Cite ref="lanacion-milanesa"/>.

The dish that resulted diverged from its Italian source as it settled in.
Argentine milanesa is made from a boneless, lean beef cut rather than a
bone-in veal rib, pounded thin and fried in a plain oil rather than clarified
butter, and it moved from an occasional dish to one of the most frequently
cooked meals in the country, eaten plain with lemon, topped with a fried
egg, or piled with ham, tomato and cheese as milanesa a la napolitana.
Surveys cited by La Nación put average consumption at around three servings
per person a week <Cite ref="lanacion-milanesa"/>, a scale of everyday
repetition that says as much about the dish's Argentine afterlife as any
single origin story can.
