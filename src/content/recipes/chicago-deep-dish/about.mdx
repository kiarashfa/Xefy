Deep dish pizza is dated with unusual precision: Pizzeria Uno opened on
Chicago's Near North Side in 1943, founded by Ike Sewell and Ric Riccardo, and
is universally agreed to be where the style began
<Cite ref="culinaryhistorians-deepdish"/>. What is not agreed on is who actually
built the dish. Ownership records suggest Sewell had no stake in the restaurant
until after it opened, which weakens his usual billing as the inventor, and
some accounts instead credit Riccardo, who is said to have brought the idea of
pizza to the venture in the first place <Cite ref="culinaryhistorians-deepdish"/>.
A kitchen employee, Rudy Malnati Sr., separately claimed credit for the crust
recipe itself, and the version most repeated today was simplified only after
Sewell sold the business in the 1970s, at which point he received sole credit
in the retelling <Cite ref="chicagomag-deepdish"/>.

Whoever built it, the construction is the genuinely unusual part. A high-sided
crust, often enriched with cornmeal or oil for a texture closer to pastry than
bread, is pressed up the sides of a deep, round pan. Cheese goes against the
dough rather than the sauce, and the tomato goes on top, uncooked before it
bakes, rather than being simmered beforehand — the reverse of nearly every
other pizza style. That inverted order exists for a practical reason: a layer
of melted cheese between the dough and a long bake under wet tomato is what
keeps the crust from turning soggy over the pizza's much longer time in the
oven.
