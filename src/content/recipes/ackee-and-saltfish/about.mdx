Neither half of Jamaica's national dish is native to Jamaica. Ackee
(*Blighia sapida*) is a West African fruit, most closely tied to Ghana, and
its name comes from the Twi word *ankye* <Cite ref="saveur-ackee"/>. It is
generally said to have reached Jamaica before 1725, and a more specific
account places its arrival around 1778, brought most likely aboard a slave
ship and purchased there specifically as a food source for enslaved people
<Cite ref="wiki-ackee"/><Cite ref="saveur-ackee"/>. That a fruit used
mostly for medicine and industrial purposes in parts of West Africa became,
in Jamaica, a national foodstuff is itself part of the dish's odder
history.

Salt cod's journey there is better documented and no less tied to slavery.
Newfoundland and Nova Scotia supplied cured, salted cod to Caribbean
plantations as a cheap, long-lasting protein for enslaved labourers, while
the better cuts of the same catch went to European markets —
a trade relationship that outlasted slavery itself and left saltfish
established as a Caribbean pantry staple long after
<Cite ref="wiki-ackee"/><Cite ref="saveur-ackee"/>.

What is not resolved is how the two ended up on the same plate. Both
ingredients have a documented route into Jamaica, but food writers
examining the dish's history describe the specific pairing of ackee with
saltfish as a genuine mystery, with no record fixing when or by whom the
combination was first made <Cite ref="saveur-ackee"/>. What is certain is
what it became afterward: a dish assembled from the leftovers of two
different arms of the Atlantic slave economy, now cooked as an everyday
breakfast and claimed, without argument, as Jamaica's own.
