The waffle iron is medieval. Street vendors were already pressing a
flour-and-water batter between hot metal grids by the Middle Ages, and by
the sixteenth century that plain batter had picked up sugar, eggs, spices
and yeast, becoming recognisably the honeycombed treat sold today
<Cite ref="natgeo-waffles"/>. What "Belgian waffle" flattens into one
export name are actually two distinct Belgian waffles, and only one of
them looks like what most people outside Belgium picture.

The Brussels waffle is the rectangular one: a light, yeasted batter baked
into deep, square pockets, mild enough in itself that it is built to carry
toppings rather than stand alone <Cite ref="natgeo-waffles"/>. It is also
the one responsible for the dish's global reputation: Belgian migrants
Maurice and Rose Vermersch sold it at the 1964 New York World's Fair under
the invented name "Bel-Gem" waffles, and the American idea of a "Belgian
waffle" descends more or less directly from that stand
<Cite ref="natgeo-waffles"/>.

The Liège waffle is the older-feeling of the two, though its own origin is
carried mostly as legend rather than record: an eighteenth-century
Prince-Bishop of Liège is said to have asked his chef for a new kind of
sweet, and the chef answered with a dense, brioche-like dough studded with
lumps of pearl sugar <Cite ref="wiki-liege-waffle"/><Cite ref="natgeo-waffles"/>.
Pearl sugar is the whole difference: its crystals resist melting at normal
baking heat, so instead of dissolving into the batter they half-caramelise
against the hot iron, leaving pockets of crunch and burnt-sugar flavour
through a small, dense, irregularly rounded waffle meant to be eaten plain
and warm from the hand, not dressed at a table
<Cite ref="wiki-liege-waffle"/><Cite ref="natgeo-waffles"/>. Inside
Belgium, the Liège waffle has in recent years become the more commonly sold
of the two, even as the Brussels waffle remains the version the rest of the
world learned first <Cite ref="wiki-liege-waffle"/><Cite ref="natgeo-waffles"/>.
