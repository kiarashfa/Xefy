The Southern buttermilk biscuit descends from British quick breads brought over by
English and Scottish settlers, but it is not a straightforward transplant
<Cite ref="kab-biscuits"/>. Wheat flour was scarce and expensive in the early
colonies, and it was successful wheat harvests in the Southern colonies,
alongside a ready supply of buttermilk and lard from dairy cows and hogs, that
gave cooks the ingredients to make something recognisably like a biscuit at all
<Cite ref="wnc-biscuits"/>.

What made it the biscuit eaten today came later, and it came from chemistry
rather than wheat. Baking soda and then baking powder reached American kitchens
in the nineteenth century, and it was these leaveners — not yeast, and not the
long fermentation a British scone might get — that let the dough rise quickly
and lightly in a hot oven <Cite ref="kab-biscuits"/>. Buttermilk, left over from
churning butter on a farm, supplied both the tang and the acid the soda needed
to react against, and the two arrived in Southern pantries at roughly the same
moment.

The biscuit's association with poverty is as real as its association with
comfort: flour, fat and soured milk were what a farm kitchen already had, and
biscuits and gravy became a cheap, dense meal for field hands and loggers before
they became a Sunday-morning indulgence <Cite ref="wnc-biscuits"/>. The
lamination that gives a good biscuit its visible layers — folding the dough over
cold fat rather than simply mixing it in — is a technique refined well after the
ingredients themselves had settled into place.
