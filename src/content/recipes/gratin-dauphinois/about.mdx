Gratin dauphinois comes from the Dauphiné, a region of south-eastern France <Cite ref="wiki-gratin"/>, <Cite ref="cwb-gratin"/>. It is made with thinly sliced potatoes baked in cream <Cite ref="wiki-gratin"/>, <Cite ref="cwb-gratin"/>.

The classic has no cheese <Cite ref="wiki-gratin"/>, <Cite ref="cwb-gratin"/>. Wikipedia says tradition excludes it, and Leslie Brenner reports that nearly every French expert consulted agrees.

Cheese and egg versions exist. Elizabeth David, citing Escoffier and Austin de Croze, notes that some recipes include them, and Brenner treats these as outliers. David's own authentic version is made only with potatoes and thick fresh cream <Cite ref="cwb-gratin"/>.

Brenner also quotes the *Oxford Companion to Food*, which describes the dish as correctly made of sliced potatoes baked in cream with no added topping <Cite ref="cwb-gratin"/>.

Brenner traces the Dauphiné back to Roman times, and notes that it joined the Holy Roman Empire in the eleventh century <Cite ref="cwb-gratin"/>. Jean-François Piège's recipe includes skim milk as well as the cream, which Brenner finds curious <Cite ref="cwb-gratin"/>.
