Ceviche is Peru's national dish, and in 2023 UNESCO added the practices around
preparing and eating it to its list of intangible cultural heritage
<Cite ref="wiki-ceviche"/>. Exactly where the underlying technique — raw fish
cured in an acid until it firms — began is disputed rather than settled.
Archaeological evidence of fish and chilli seeds in human remains on Peru's
northern coast points to the pre-Inca Moche, and the food historian Maricel
Presilla places the dish's likely birthplace near the fishing town of Huanchaco
<Cite ref="natgeo-ceviche"/>. Ecuador makes a rival claim resting on the
shellfish record of the Valdivia culture, further north and of comparable age
<Cite ref="wiki-ceviche"/>, and the honest position is that a shared coastal
technique predates any single national claim to it.

The citrus marinade now considered essential to the dish is itself a later
graft onto that technique: limes and bitter oranges reached the Peruvian coast
only after 1492, carried by Spanish and Portuguese traders, and displaced
earlier acidic macerations made with native fruit and fermented corn drink
<Cite ref="natgeo-ceviche"/>.

What the citrus does to the fish is a genuine chemical change and a common
source of confusion. Its acidity coagulates the muscle protein much as heat
does, turning the flesh firm and opaque, but the two processes are not the
same thing: acid denaturation is gentler than heat, which is why cured fish
stays moist where cooked fish dries out, and unlike heat it does nothing
reliable to the parasites raw fish can carry <Cite ref="atk-ceviche"/>.

The brief marinade served today is itself recent. Older Peruvian ceviche sat
in citrus for a long stretch; the now-standard version, dressed for minutes
rather than hours, followed the arrival of Japanese immigrants whose sashimi
tradition favoured showing off freshly cut raw fish, and took hold in Lima's
restaurants only within the last several decades <Cite ref="natgeo-ceviche"/>.
