Pelmeni takes its name from *pel'nyan'*, "ear bread," in the Komi and Udmurt
languages of the Finno-Ugric peoples of the Ural Mountains, and the dish is
rooted in Komi, Udmurt and Permian cooking before it was ever treated as
Russian at all <Cite ref="wiki-pelmeni"/>. From the Urals it is generally
held to have spread east into Siberia, where hunters carried sacks of the raw
dumplings frozen solid as winter provisions, boiling them straight from the
cold as needed <Cite ref="wiki-pelmeni"/>.

Pelmeni's own ancestry is argued to reach further than the Urals. One theory
holds the dish descended from Chinese jiaozi, pointing to the black pepper and
other spices in a traditional filling that are not native to Russia as a
trace of that route <Cite ref="wiki-pelmeni"/>. A single Ural-Siberian origin
is itself disputed: some historians propose that comparable dumplings reached
the region by several separate paths — through Crimean Tatar, Central Asian
and Dagestani cooking — and were only folded together under the one name by
the middle of the nineteenth century <Cite ref="moscow-times-pelmeni"/>.

What the record agrees on is that pelmeni is a late arrival to Russian
cookbooks. No printed Russian recipe for it is known before the 1820s and
1830s; an 1817 account described dumplings of this kind as "like Chinese
pastries," and in 1837 the writer Yekaterina Avdeyeva still filed pelmeni
under Siberian provincial vocabulary rather than treating it as a national
dish <Cite ref="moscow-times-pelmeni"/>. Its present status as an
everyday, all-Russian staple is a comparatively modern development set
against a much older regional history.
