Rice reached Iran from South and Southeast Asia, and for centuries it stayed a food
of the elite — grown and eaten mainly under Safavid patronage before it spread more
widely through Iranian society over the seventh to thirteenth centuries
<Cite ref="tasting-table-chelow"/>. *Chelo*, plain steamed white rice cooked and
served on its own rather than mixed through with other ingredients as in a *polo*,
became the everyday form that rice took once it was no longer restricted to court
kitchens.

The crust at the bottom of the pot has its own name, *tahdig* — "bottom of the pot"
— and its own telling of how it came to be prized rather than scraped out and
discarded. A widely repeated account, set in the nineteenth-century Qajar court,
has palace servants fighting loudly over the crisp rice left in the royal cooking
pot until the commotion reached the king himself, who tried it, approved, and had
it served to him every night after. That story is presented even by the sources
that repeat it as a popular legend rather than a documented event, with no
contemporary record behind it <Cite ref="aish-tahdig"/>.

Whatever the truth of its telling, tahdig's spread through Persian society was well
under way by the end of the nineteenth century, judged by how widely home cooks
were already known to attempt it <Cite ref="aish-tahdig"/>. It later travelled with
Persian Jewish communities as they left Iran across the twentieth century,
following the 1948 founding of Israel and later waves of emigration, becoming for
many a dish that carried the memory of home as much as it fed a table
<Cite ref="aish-tahdig"/>.

What tahdig is made from varies by household and region far more than the legend
suggests: a layer of potato, of flatbread, of yoghurt-thickened rice, or the rice
itself pressed directly against a well-oiled pot, each producing a differently
textured crust from the same underlying technique of parboiling the rice and
finishing it by steam alone.
