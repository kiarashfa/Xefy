Cantonese steamed fish is built around restraint rather than seasoning: a
whole fish, steamed briefly with ginger and spring onion and finished with
nothing more than soy sauce and a splash of hot oil, is judged almost entirely
on how well the cook has protected the natural texture and sweetness of the
fish itself. Any technique that masks the fish's own flavour is, by that
standard, a failure rather than a variation.

Serving the fish whole, head and tail intact, is not simply presentation. In
Mandarin, *yú*, "fish," is a homophone for *yú*, "surplus" or "abundance," a
long-recognised pun in Chinese culture <Cite ref="wiki-fish-mythology"/>
<Cite ref="foodrepublic-fish"/>. Serving a whole fish at a
celebratory meal — most visibly at the Lunar New Year reunion dinner — invokes
the wish *niánnián yǒu yú*, "may there be surplus every year," and families
will often deliberately leave part of the fish unfinished, so that the
uneaten portion stands for the abundance still to come rather than what has
already been used up <Cite ref="foodrepublic-fish"/>. Cutting the fish into
fillets before serving would break the pun as much as the fish itself, which
is one practical reason whole steamed fish, rather than a filleted dish, holds
this particular place at the table.

The ginger and spring onion do more than season the dish. Steamed alongside
the fish and then finished raw as a garnish under a ladle of smoking-hot oil,
they mask any trace of muddiness in the fish's flavour while doing almost
nothing to cover its texture — the balance the whole method is built to
protect.
