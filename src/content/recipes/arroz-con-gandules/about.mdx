Arroz con gandules is one pot built from three separate arrivals. The pigeon
pea itself has the longest journey of any ingredient in it: an old crop of
both Africa and South Asia, it was carried between the two by traders, and
its origin between them has long been argued <Cite ref="nass-pigeon-peas"/>.
From West Africa, it reached Puerto
Rico with enslaved people transported there under Spanish colonial rule, who
carried the dried pea as a familiar staple of their own diet
<Cite ref="familia-kitchen-gandules"/>. Rice and pork, meanwhile, were Spanish
introductions, planted and raised on the same island the Taíno already
farmed — and it was Taíno cooking practice, seasoning a single simmered pot
with local peppers and herbs, that gave the combination its form
<Cite ref="familia-kitchen-gandules"/>.

The pea proved suited to Puerto Rico's climate in a way that mattered beyond
flavour: it grew reliably where other crops failed, and a dish built around
it functioned as a hedge against hunger for the island's poorer agricultural
communities before it was later taken up more widely <Cite ref="familia-kitchen-gandules"/>.
That dual identity — practical sustenance food and, eventually, festive
centrepiece — persists in how the dish is eaten today: it appears on tables
across the island year-round but is especially associated with Christmas and
other holiday gatherings.

Sofrito is what turns the three components into one dish rather than three
things cooked side by side. The blended base of onion, garlic, peppers and
culantro or coriander is stirred through the rice before the liquid goes in,
so every grain cooks in it rather than being seasoned only on the surface.
Today arroz con gandules is widely regarded, together with roast pork, as
Puerto Rico's national dish, and closely related
versions travelled with Puerto Rican and other Caribbean communities to the
Dominican Republic and beyond, each keeping the same rice-and-pea base while
naming and seasoning it slightly differently.
