The buttery sponge now called Victoria sponge is a specifically Victorian
invention, and not only because of the queen it is named for. A sponge
batter heavy with butter needs more lift than beaten egg alone reliably
gives it, and that limitation lifted only in 1843, when the British food
manufacturer Alfred Bird invented baking powder, making it possible to add
butter and still achieve a high, even rise
<Cite ref="wiki-vs"/><Cite ref="byp-vs"/>. The cake could not exist in its
modern form before the ingredient that defines its texture did.

The cake's association with Queen Victoria herself is the part resting on
tradition rather than documentation. It is widely said to have been a
favourite of hers at afternoon tea <Cite ref="wiki-vs"/><Cite ref="byp-vs"/>, and one of the
earliest written references to it appears as "Victoria Sandwiches" in
Isabella Beeton's *Book of Household Management* <Cite ref="byp-vs"/>. One
complication worth noting: English Heritage's account suggests the cake may
first have been devised as a children's alternative to fruit and seed cake,
which early Victorian households considered unsafe for young diners, and
only later moved from the nursery to the adult tea table
<Cite ref="eh-cakes"/>. Afternoon tea as a fixed social occasion is itself a
later development than popularly assumed — it does not appear to have
become established practice until cookery books of the 1870s and 1880s
began circulating it <Cite ref="eh-cakes"/>, which sits awkwardly with the
image of a mid-century queen presiding over it from the start.

What has stayed constant is the cake's refusal to dress itself up: two
plain layers, jam and nothing else between them, and a dusting of sugar
rather than icing on top <Cite ref="wiki-vs"/><Cite ref="eh-cakes"/>.
Whatever the exact path from nursery or drawing room to national
institution, that plainness is the whole point — it is a cake built to be
judged on crumb alone.
