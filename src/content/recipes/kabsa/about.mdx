Kabsa's own name records its method rather than its history: it comes from the
Arabic root *k-b-s*, "to press" or "to squeeze," for the way meat, rice and
spices are cooked tightly together in one pot <Cite ref="wiki-kabsa"/>. Where that
method itself began is genuinely contested. One account holds it as a Bedouin
dish, built by desert tribes out of whatever rice, meat and spice were on hand; a
second traces it to Yemeni mandi, the older tradition of meat and rice cooked
together in an earth oven; a third, more surprising claim connects it to Spanish
paella by way of Andalusian cooking. None of these is treated as settled, and
Wikipedia presents all three side by side as competing theories rather than
choosing between them <Cite ref="wiki-kabsa"/>.

The Yemeni connection has independent support: kabsa's roots are traced to Yemen
and to mandi specifically by more than one source, which places kabsa within a
wider Arabian Peninsula family of rice-and-meat dishes distinguished mainly by
technique and regional spicing rather than by separate origins
<Cite ref="sauditimes-kabsa"/>. That same family stretches east as well — kabsa is
described as sharing "culinary DNA" with the Persian- and Indian-descended pilaf
tradition that also produced dishes such as biryani, built on similar principles
of rice cooked in a well-spiced stock <Cite ref="sauditimes-kabsa"/>.

Whatever its beginning, kabsa's standing as a national dish is a recent, deliberate
designation rather than an ancient one: Saudi Arabia's Ministry of Culture named it
as part of the country's promoted culinary heritage in 2021, formalising a status
that regional practice had already given it across the Gulf <Cite ref="wiki-kabsa"/>.
