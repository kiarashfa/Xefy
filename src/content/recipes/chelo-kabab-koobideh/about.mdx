The name of the dish is a description of how it is made. *Koobideh* is the past
participle of the Persian verb *koobidan*, "to pound" <Cite ref="wiki-koobideh"/>, and
that is the traditional method behind the modern kneading and squeezing by hand: the
meat was once laid on a flat stone or log and beaten with a wooden mallet until it
bound into a paste that would hold its own shape on a skewer with no egg or
breadcrumb added <Cite ref="wiki-koobideh"/>.

Where in Iran the technique was first used this way is not settled. Three places are
each named as the dish's home — Golpayegan, Tehran and Bonab — and the available
record does not adjudicate between them or supply a date for when any of them
arrived at it <Cite ref="wiki-koobideh"/>. Bonab's claim rests on its own named
kebab, pounded from mutton with onion and salt and served on a scale large enough
to be the dish's defining feature, which suggests a genuinely separate local
tradition rather than a simple relabelling of koobideh <Cite ref="wiki-bonab"/>.

A separate account puts the wider chelo-kabab tradition's arrival in Iran in the
Qajar era, crediting either Caucasian people directly or Nasser al-Din Shah's own
taste for what he encountered on his travels to Russia and the Caucasus, as recorded
by the nineteenth-century writer Mirza Mohammad Reza Mo'tamed-ul-Ketab Shams
Larijani <Cite ref="iranchamber-chelokabab"/>. That account is offered as one telling
among others rather than a settled fact, and it concerns chelo-kabab as a whole
rather than koobideh's pounding technique specifically, so it does not resolve the
Golpayegan-Tehran-Bonab question above <Cite ref="iranchamber-chelokabab"/>.

What is better documented is the kebab's place in a wider family: kabab koobideh is
one of several skewered minced-meat preparations across Iran and its neighbours
built on the same pounding technique, distinguished from one another mainly by the
ratio of lamb to beef, the seasoning, and how the meat is bound to the skewer rather
than by a separate invention each time.
