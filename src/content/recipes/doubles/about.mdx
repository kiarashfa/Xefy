Doubles has a founding story unusually specific for a street food: it names
the couple who made it, and the year. Emamool "Mamoodeen" Deen and his wife
Raheeman Rasulan Deen were selling from near to nothing in the southern
Trinidad town of Princes Town in 1936, having just sold the only goat they
owned <Cite ref="wikipedia-doubles"/> <Cite ref="juggernaut-doubles"/>. With
the little the sale brought in,
Rasulan bought chickpeas, fried them, and served them over a piece of fried
flatbread called bara <Cite ref="juggernaut-doubles"/>.

What she sold that first night was closer to a snack of fried chickpeas than
the dish doubles later became. Over time she braised the chickpeas down into
a curry seasoned with cumin, turmeric and the Trinidadian blend of fresh
herbs known as green seasoning, and switched the bara itself from a more
traditional mung-bean-flour dough to a cheaper one made with wheat flour
<Cite ref="juggernaut-doubles"/>. Emamool carried it through town by bicycle,
selling it from paper cones.

The name is the part of the story every account of the dish repeats. Deen
first sold the curried chickpeas over a single piece of bara; when customers
began asking him to double the bara in their order, the request is said to
have given the dish its name — a detail that travels with the founding
story above even where it is not independently documented. Some writers
have also pointed to a resemblance between doubles and chole bhature, the
chickpea-curry-and-fried-bread dish of northern India, offered as a
plausible influence rather than a competing account of the dish's
Trinidadian invention.

Doubles is now sold from roadside stands across Trinidad and Tobago at all
hours, eaten by hand with a spoon of tamarind or pepper chutney, and is
widely regarded as the country's national snack.
