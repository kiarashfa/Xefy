A Roman pasta sauce contains no cream and usually no liquid beyond what the
cook adds back at the end. What makes it a sauce rather than cheese and fat
sitting on noodles is an emulsion, and what holds that emulsion together is the
starch the pasta has been shedding into its water.

## What the water is carrying

Dried pasta loses a proportion of its surface starch into the pan. Those
released granules swell and burst, and the water thickens very slightly — enough
that it stops behaving like water. Starch molecules in suspension get between
droplets of fat and keep them from finding each other, which is the whole job.
Fat and water do not mix; fat, water and starch do.

This is why the instruction is always to keep a mugful *before* draining, and
why it has to be the cooking water rather than hot water from the tap. The tap
has no starch in it.

## Why the pan comes off the heat

The second half of the technique is temperature, and it is where the sauce is
usually lost. Egg proteins begin to set not far above <Temp c={60}/> and are
firmly scrambled by <Temp c={70}/>. A pan that has just held boiling pasta is
well above both. Tipping beaten egg into it produces sweet scrambled egg with
pasta in it — a failure that happens in seconds and cannot be reversed.

So the pan leaves the heat, drops below the setting point, and the residual
warmth plus the starch does the work. The same logic applies without egg: cheese
added to a pan that is too hot separates, its proteins tightening and squeezing
out the fat, which is the grainy, oily result people describe as the sauce
"splitting".

## Working it

The emulsion is built by agitation, not by waiting. Tossing hard and
continuously is what breaks the fat into droplets small enough for the starch to
hold apart, and a sauce that looks thin and disappointing will often come
together after another twenty seconds of vigorous tossing rather than needing
anything added.

Add the water in small amounts. It is far easier to loosen a sauce that has gone
too tight than to rescue one that has been flooded, and pasta continues to drink
liquid after it leaves the pan — a sauce that looks perfect in the pan is
usually a little too tight by the time it reaches the table.
